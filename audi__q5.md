#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: This review covers the Audi Q5 range you’ll find on the Australian used market from the 2018–2023 model years (second‑generation Typ 80A / “MQB‑sibling” period into the early third‑generation rollout). The Q5 name has been used continuously since 2008; recent models transition from Audi’s MLB (longitudinal) architecture to the newer Premium Platform Combustion (PPC) for the latest redesigns. See Audi’s model pages and platform notes for context: [Audi Q5 / PPC coverage](https://www.audi.com/en/audi-q5-59) and the older MLB platform background at [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkswagen_Group_MLB_platform).
- Product hierarchy: The Q5 sits squarely in the compact-luxury SUV sweet spot for Audi:
  - Sits above: Audi Q3 (smaller premium crossover)
  - Sits below: Audi Q7 / Q8 (larger luxury SUVs)
  - Performance sibling: SQ5 is the V6 performance variant that sits alongside (and above) the standard Q5.
- Shared global platform: Older Q5s use Audi’s MLB platform family; freshly redesigned Q5s use the PPC architecture shared with the A5 family and later Audi models — good news for parts and electronics commonality within the VW‑Audi group ([MLB platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkswagen_Group_MLB_platform), [Audi media on PPC](https://www.audi-mediacenter.com/en/press-releases/the-new-audi-q5-suv-proven-concept-in-its-third-generation-16232/download)).
- Market-specific powertrains, outputs and drivetrains (what you’re likely to see in NSW/Australia on the used market):
  - Petrol 2.0‑litre turbocharged four‑cylinder — often badged as 40 TFSI in later model nomenclature (various outputs depending on year and tune); typically paired with the 7‑speed S tronic dual‑clutch or 8‑speed torque‑converter automatic depending on model year/market. Source overview: [Audi Q5 — Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audi_Q5).
  - Petrol 3.0‑litre V6 (SQ5 / performance) — higher power, usually paired with quattro AWD and a 7‑speed S tronic in recent iterations ([Audi Media / US specs for newer Q5s](https://media.audiusa.com/view/releases/634)).
  - Diesel options: 2.0 TDI and 3.0 TDI variants in some markets/years — common in fleet and higher‑kilometre used cars (DPF/EGR maintenance realities described in diesel writeups).
  - Standard drive configuration: quattro all‑wheel drive is common on Australian Q5s; some markets offered FWD on lower‑spec models early on. Transmissions across the range include the 7‑speed S tronic DCT and various 6/8‑speed torque‑converter automatics ([Wikipedia Q5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audi_Q5)).
- Explicit rivals: BMW X3, Mercedes‑Benz GLC, Volvo XC60, Lexus NX, Genesis GV70 — the usual suspects in the compact‑lux luxury SUV class (comparison lists: [The Car Connection competitors list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/audi_q5_choices), [Edmunds/KBB roundups]()).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Audi Q5?
Practical, hard checks — what to examine on inspection and pre‑purchase.

Exterior
- Panel fit and trim:
  - Panel gaps are generally tidy, but owners report inconsistent seam tightness on some builds — check doors, bonnet and tailgate joins carefully for differences between left and right that might indicate past repairs. Community reports: owner forums and fit/finish threads ([myaudiq5 forum](https://www.myaudiq5.com/threads/exterior-body-seams.82481/)).
  - Rear wheel‑arch trim: a real-world safety recall — certain 2018–2020 Q5/SQ5 rear wheel arch mouldings can become loose and detach because of weak mounting clips. If you see loose wheel‑arch trims, demand proof the recall was completed (NHTSA recall 19V‑737 / Audi 66K8) — [recall doc (NHTSA PDF)](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2019/RCONL-19V737-3831.pdf) and aftermarket parts writeups ([Go‑Parts summary](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-audi-sq5-audi-q5-audi-q5-sportback-2018-2025)).
  - Paint peeling/bumper edge delamination: there are documented TSBs and owner complaints about paint adhesion where rear bumper meets panels — inspect the bumper join and number‑plate area for peeling clearcoat or flaking paint ([TSB 63‑23‑10 synopsis](https://www.tsbsearch.com/Audi/63-23-10), owner threads).
  - Rust: not rampant, but hidden rust and bubbling reported around weld seams and plate edges on older/untreated examples — check underside, sills and tow‑bar mounts (owner compilations: [CarNews rust overview](https://carnews.nl/en/car-problems/car-problems-audi/audi-q5/rust-and-bodywork-problems-with-the-audi-q5/)).
- ADAS/safety sensors:
  - Complaints about AEB (automatic emergency braking) and adaptive cruise misbehaviour — false or missed braking events, radar/camera warnings, and sensor fault lights are not unheard of. These often trace to sensor contamination, harness connectors, or required software/calibration updates. Look for service history showing ADAS software updates and calibration after panel repairs. Sources: [NHTSA complaints](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle/2019/AUDI/Q5), [CarComplaints](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Audi/Q5/), and press coverage of software/recall fixes ([Motor1/Autoblog ADAS updates](https://www.motor1.com/)).
  - Practical check: test ACC and AEB on a quiet road (speed‑limited conditions) — listen and feel for sudden, inappropriate braking or lost lock on a steady target.

Interior
- Wear and durability:
  - Materials are premium but not invincible under rideshare/taxi duty. Look for worn bolsters (especially on leather), scuffed lower doors and grubby seat insert edges on high‑kilometre cars. Aggregated owner reliability pages show electronics and interior wear as recurring items ([CoverageX reliability guide](https://coveragex.com/auto/audi-q5-common-problems-by-year)).
  - Entry/egress: reasonably low step‑over and wide doors — good for frequent family use; however door check angles can vary by spec and hinge wear shows on higher‑km cars.
- Electricals and infotainment:
  - USB ports and wireless charging: frequent failure/overheating and broken USB sockets; wireless phone box modules are a recurring replacement item and are sometimes costly to access because of centre console removal ([Go‑Parts USB/wireless guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/usb-port-audi-q7-audi-q8-audi-a3-2016-2026)). Expect nuisance faults like intermittent phone charging or “could not execute action” messages in owner forums.
  - Infotainment versions differ by year and trim — screens moved from smaller MMI units to larger touchscreens in later models. Confirm the MMI software version, Apple CarPlay/Android Auto behaviour, and whether any multimedia module recalls/campaigns were done. Frozen head units are reported occasionally; a dealer reflash sometimes cures software causes.
  - 12V battery drain: aftermarket reports show parasitic draws on older Audi models can be caused by modules not sleeping properly. Check the battery health, ask for a low‑voltage standby test record, and inspect whether the car has had aftermarket accessories fitted (alarms, stereo, trackers) which are a common source of drain.
- Practical checks: cycle through all screens, test all USB ports, check that wireless charging works with your phone without odd heating, and check for stored service codes on the infotainment (dealer scan).

Mechanically
- Engineering reputation: Audi builds quality cars but the Q5 sits in the “premium but maintenance‑sensitive” bucket — good engineering but some components are expensive and labour‑intensive to fix compared with non‑luxury rivals.
- Security and theft:
  - Modern Q5s with keyless systems are attractive to thieves. Newer theft techniques such as relay attacks and CAN‑injection hacks have targeted many premium vehicles, and Audi networks are not immune. The CAN injection method and headlight connector bypasses have been publicly documented — thieves can inject messages into vehicle CAN networks after accessing wiring looms to bypass immobilisers. Defences include physical steering locks, secured key storage, and asking your Audi dealer about the latest OEM software/anti‑theft modules. Sources: detailed technical writeups on CAN injection ([Kent Indell CAN injection writeup](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/)), industry coverage ([SecurityWeek CAN injection](https://www.securityweek.com/thieves-use-can-injection-hack-to-steal-cars/)) and reporting ([Autoblog theft variants](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update)).
  - Practical aftermarket measures: mechanical steering wheel lock (cheap and effective), OBD port locks, verified dealer-installed immobiliser updates or PIN‑to‑drive features, and secure key storage at home. Ask the dealer which anti‑theft patches were applied.
- High‑priority security vulnerabilities to ask about:
  - Evidence of alarm/aftermarket wiring splices (risk of parasitic drains and theft vectors).
  - Whether the VIN has been subject to salvage branding or repeated ABS/airbag module replacements (CAN manipulations sometimes leave traces).

Engines
Create an italicized subsection for each engine variant commonly seen in Australian used Q5s and name failure points and service guidance.

- *2.0 TFSI (petrol 2.0‑litre turbo four) — the most common used‑market engine*
  - Known issues: excessive oil consumption in many examples (owners report very high oil burn between services), timing‑chain tensioner and guide wear on older variants that can lead to chain skip and catastrophic engine damage if ignored, carbon buildup on intake valves (a common direct‑injection side effect), occasional HPFP and PCV faults, and plastic water‑pump failures. See multi‑source diagnostics: [CarJustify 2.0T guide](https://carjustify.com/audi-2-0-tfsi-problems/), [CartipsDaily timing chain/oil issues](https://cartipsdaily.com/audi-2-0-tfsi-problems) and other owner summaries ([Autozilla](https://autozilla.co.uk/audi/audi-2-0-tfsi-engine-problems-common-issues-explained/)).
  - Symptoms to watch for: blue smoke on cold start or after long freeway runs, oil consumption over 1 L per 2,000 km (owner reports vary — check dipstick), rattles on cold start indicative of tensioner problems, and check‑engine light for misfire/HPFP codes.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil: independent techs strongly recommend top‑quality synthetic oil and checking oil levels between services — consider 10,000 km or earlier oil checks if you’ll keep the car long term (many recommend 10,000 km oil drains for turbocharged petrols, especially older TFSI variants). If the car has high oil usage, verify a documented oil‑consumption fix or recent replacement of tensioners/HPFP/PCV components.
    - Timing chain/tensioner: inspect service history; tensioner failures commonly surface in the 60,000–160,000 km window (reports vary by variant); treat any rattle as a red flag. Sources referenced above discuss the range of failure miles.
- *3.0 TFSI / SQ5 V6 (petrol V6)*
  - Known issues: generally stronger and less prone to the small‑engine oil‑consumption problem, but turbocharger and charge‑cooler plumbing wear, high maintenance costs (valve cover gaskets, turbo seals) and higher service bills overall. Newer SQ5s use robust drivetrains but expect premium parts/labour rates.
  - Preventative maintenance: follow dealer schedule; earlier oil changes and attention to boost hoses and oil leaks are sensible.
- *2.0 / 3.0 TDI (diesel variants)*
  - Known issues: DPF (diesel particulate filter) blocking on low‑kilometre urban cars, EGR valve and turbocharger actuator faults, injector wear on high‑km examples. Diesel injection systems are sensitive to oil quality and interval discipline — cheap oil choices accelerate injector/DPF problems.
  - Preventative maintenance: regular longer‑run highway km help DPF regeneration; use manufacturer‑spec oil and address warning lights promptly. See diesel problem summaries in owner and mechanic writeups.

Transmission
- 7‑speed S tronic dual‑clutch (DCT):
  - Behaviour: sharp, quick shifts when new; on older units owners report jerkiness at low speeds and occasional mechatronic faults. DCTs demand correct software and oil service intervals. While not universal, DCTs can be costly to repair if the mechatronic or clutch packs go south.
  - Check: cold start low‑speed crawling behaviour, smoothness in reverse/parking manoeuvres, and any trans‑service lights. Ask for service history showing transmission oil/mechatronic service if requested by Audi for your year.
- Torque‑converter automatics (6/8‑speed):
  - Behaviour: generally smooth and durable, but watch for delayed engagement, slipping, or harsh shifts — possibly a sign of fluid neglect or internal wear. Diesels often used torque‑converter boxes and tend to be less symptom‑sensitive than DCTs.
- Known failure modes (summary):
  - Mechatronic failures, clutch wear and valvebody/solenoid issues on S tronic units; fluid and torque‑converter issues on older torque‑converter automatics. Confirm service history for transmission fluid services — not all owners do them on time.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Audi Q5?
Numbers first, then the judgement call.

- NSW dealer market pricing (your snapshot):

  - Price range: AUD $21,990 – $61,990
  - Median price: AUD $39,800
  - Year range: 2018 – 2023
  - Median odometer: 76,283 km
  - Transmission mix: Automatic only

- Depreciation and value:
  - The Q5 generally follows luxury segment depreciation patterns — steeper than mass‑market SUVs but better than some small prestige cars in the resale cycle. For hard data, check pricing curves at KBB or Edmunds for your local market comparisons ([KBB Audi Q5 values](https://www.kbb.com/audi/q5/), [Edmunds Q5 research](https://www.edmunds.com/audi/q5/)). Expect a baseline drop of ~40–50% from new after 3 years in most premium compacts (segment averages vary); the exact figure depends on trim, options, and whether it’s an SQ5 or a diesel model.
- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy — how I’d calculate if a Q5 makes sense for you:
  1. Annual running estimate:
     - Annual km (e.g., 15,000 km typical Aussie commuter/family).
     - Fuel consumption: take a conservative real‑world figure for the powertrain you’re looking at (for example, assume 2.0‑litre petrol real‑world 8–10 L/100 km; V6 and AWD variants higher; diesels lower on highway but expensive if DPF/EGR issues). If you can’t find a verified figure for the specific VIN/engine, add +10–20% to the lab figure to estimate real use.
     - Fuel cost: multiply consumption x price per litre x annual km.
  2. Service & maintenance:
     - Premium brands typically cost 1.5–2x non‑luxury dealers for parts & labour. Include scheduled services (pads/rotors, brake fluid, filters, spark plugs at interval), and budget for occasional larger items (suspension bushes, turbo hoses, clutch or mechatronic service for S tronic).
  3. Repairs & contingencies:
     - For 2.0 TFSI cars, budget for potential oil‑consumption fixes or timing chain work if service history is incomplete. For higher km diesels, budget for injector/DPF work.
  4. Insurance & rego:
     - Premium segment policies cost more; include the annual premium.
  - Example (very rough): if petrol use is 9 L/100 km, at 15,000 km/yr = 1,350 L/yr. At AUD $1.80/L = AUD $2,430/yr fuel. Add service & maintenance AUD $1,200–$2,000/yr averaged over years, plus insurance/rego — you’re looking at perhaps AUD $5k–7k/yr TCO excluding finance and depreciation for a mid‑spec petrol Q5. This is a worked methodology; plug in your numbers to get a personal total.
- Does paying a premium for hybrid/PHEV/EV variants make sense?
  - Audi has been adding MHEV and PHEV options in newer Q5s (Audi media outlines the move to mild‑hybrid and plug‑in options) — [Audi MediaCenter on third‑gen Q5](https://www.audi-mediacenter.com/en/press-releases/the-new-audi-q5-suv-proven-concept-in-its-third-generation-16232/download).
  - The answer depends on your driving:
    - Low‑km urban drivers who can regularly charge a PHEV and have short trips could see fuel savings. But in Australia PHEVs often fail to deliver savings if not charged regularly or if repeat highway runs dominate — you pay a premium up front and that premium takes years to recoup unless you exploit electric kilometres daily.
    - For MHEV (mild‑hybrid), there’s no fuel‑cost magic — it smooths stop/start and recovers a little energy but won’t dramatically lower running costs; it’s more of a refinement/efficiency feature.
  - Practical math: to decide, compare the purchase premium for a PHEV vs equivalent petrol model, divide the premium by your expected annual fuel saving (use the TCO method above) — if the payback is longer than how long you plan to keep the car, skip the PHEV premium. (Audi’s press and spec pages confirm availability of MHEV/PHEV variants but don’t guarantee local resale premiums or charging habits — see Audi pages above.)
- Depreciation metric (clear cut advice):
  - The Q5 depreciates like other compact luxury SUVs but holds slightly better than some niche models because the badge and quattro desirability keep demand steady. Prices from KBB/Edmunds and market listings support this pattern ([KBB](https://www.kbb.com/audi/q5/), [Edmunds](https://www.edmunds.com/audi/q5/)).
- Buying recommendation — the blunt, Australian verdict:
  - If you want the Audi experience (comfortable, refined, good tech) and you’re okay with premium servicing bills, a used Q5 is a perfectly sensible choice — especially if you buy right.
  - Optimal financial sweet spot (practical pick): Aim for a 2019–2021 2.0 TFSI quattro with full service history, preferably dealer‑maintained and with documented ADAS software updates and the wheel‑arch recall completed. These model years are old enough to have dropped significantly from new yet new enough to avoid early life teething problems.
  - Avoid: High‑km 2.0 TFSI cars without oil‑consumption and timing‑chain history, and any PHEV you can’t confirm charging history for. Also be cautious about low‑priced examples (sub‑AUD $25k in this snapshot) with missing service history — cheap Q5s can be false economy if they need engine or DCT work.
  - Price expectance in NSW market snapshot: with median AUD $39,800 for 2018–2023 and median 76k km, you’re in the ballpark for a well‑optioned mid‑life example. Use the MTA NSW snapshot to negotiate — low listing volumes mean good cars are still selling to keen buyers; bad ones linger.
  - Pre‑purchase inspection checklist (non‑negotiable):
    - Full service history back to new; check oil change receipts and timing‑chain/tensioner work.
    - Dealer/VIN check for completion of Audi recall 66K8 (wheel‑arch trim) and any ADAS or software campaigns.
    - Physical inspection for bumper paint delamination and seam rust.
    - Road test with ACC/AEB verification and DCT/transmission behaviour check.
    - Battery health and parasitic drain test; test phone wireless charging & USB ports.
    - Key security check — ask about immobiliser/anti‑theft upgrades or evidence of tampering.
    - If buying a diesel: see DPF regen history and injector service receipts.

Final line: the Audi Q5 gives you compact‑luxury polish with genuine Audi engineering — but you’re buying into a premium maintenance culture. Treat the purchase like a used premium watch: make sure it’s been looked after, and don’t be seduced by a low price that hides a high repair bill. If you stick to the 2019–2021 2.0 TFSI with full service history and verified recall/ADAS updates, you’ll get the balance of value and prestige without the heart‑ache.
