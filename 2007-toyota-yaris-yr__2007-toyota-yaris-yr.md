#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

Let's rip the sticker off immediately: buying a twenty-year-old Toyota Yaris in the current Australian climate is less of a purchase decision and more of a plea to fate wrapped in a hatchback. You're looking at the **2007 Toyota Yaris YR**, officially the facelifted first generation running the internal alphanumeric **NCP90R** chassis code. While Toyota tried to jazz this thing up with sharper lines and a slightly meaner front bumper back in 2005, the 2007 model lands squarely in the middle of that lifecycle—old enough to have its gremlins shaken out, new enough to still have decent metal remaining if the previous owners weren't using it as a daily dragster.

In the Toyota pecking order of 2007, the YR sat at the absolute bottom of the food chain. Below it? There was nothing. It was the bare-bones starter rocket designed to get teenagers and retirees behind the wheel. Step up, and you'd find the **YRS**, which slapped on alloy wheels, dual airbags across the board, and enough chrome to blind a sun god. Above the YRS, you'd occasionally catch sight of a **YRX**, though those were rare imports or top-spec local hits that commanded serious cash even back when diesel was cheaper than bottled water.

Architecturally, the NCP90R rides on the Toyota XP90 platform. This isn't just a fancy name Toyota threw around marketing brochures; it's a unibody skeleton shared with global siblings like the Toyota Passo and the Daihatsu Boon. For you, this is massive intelligence regarding parts availability. You're unlikely to starve a YR of components because everything from suspension bushings to door hinges is shared with volumes of other Kei-class derivatives that flooded Japan and regional markets. If you break a mirror, you can likely salvage a donor part from a boneyard Passo or a wrecked YR with half the odometer reading.

Locally, the powertrain lineup was straightforward. Toyota offered a single petrol displacement: the **1.3-litre 1299 cc** engine (often badged as **1.3i**), pumping out enough grunt to move the lightweight shell efficiently without inspiring any actual adrenaline. Transmission choices were binary and brutally simple: a four-speed torque converter automatic or a five-speed dog-leg manual. Drive configuration? Front-wheel drive, plain and simple. There's no AWD nonsense here; this car pushes its weight forward and hopes for the best.

Its explicit marketplace rivals in 2007 were the usual suspects fighting for the same budget-conscious demographic: the **Hyundai Getz**, **Ford Fiesta**, **Mazda Demio (Axela)**, and the **Honda Jazz**. Unlike the Jazz, which offered coupé styling and rear-seat magic tricks, the Yaris YR offered something far more valuable to the frugal Aussie buyer: Toyota reliability and the peace of mind that knows it won't strand you on the Princes Highway.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used 2007 Toyota Yaris YR?

##### Exterior
The exterior design holds up well enough, but age is doing its best work via oxidation and chip erosion. We're dealing with steel panels, so while aluminium corrosion isn't the enemy here, galvanic rust is lurking in the shadows. Gloss black elements? Minimal on the YR spec, which saves you from the scratching nightmare of soft-touch piano black, but you'll find gloss accents on the door mirrors and grille surrounds. These will fade to a chalky grey faster than your last holiday tan.

Paint durability is average for a budget Japanese car of this era. Watch the bonnet leading edge for stone chips that bleed straight down to the primer. On white models, specifically **Snow Cap White** (as documented in recent inventory), this fading is noticeable within three to five years. The panel alignment should remain consistent; these cars don't typically suffer from rattle-box syndrome, provided the underbody hasn't taken a hammering on corrugations. Be wary of mismatched panels on the sills—that's usually the handiwork of a previous minor insurance claim rather than structural concern.

Safety tech? You're living in the stone age. This vehicle has zero ADAS hardware. No autonomous emergency braking, no lane keep assist, and certainly no radar cruise control. If the car detects a pedestrian, the driver does the detecting. Software quirks aren't an issue because the software doesn't exist to crash. However, do inspect the light clusters for moisture ingress; the rear clusters are notorious traps for condensation due to sealant degradation over two decades.

##### Interior
Step inside, and you're greeted by a cabin engineered to survive abuse, provided you don't mind feeling like you're sitting on a laundry basket. The plastics are hard-wearing and easy to clean, which makes this Yaris an unofficial favourite among fleet managers and rideshare pioneers in years gone by. However, "durability" and "comfort" are two different stories. The seat bolstering collapses well before 150,000 km, leaving the driver perched awkwardly with thigh support evaporating.

Spatial entry and exit are genuinely strengths. The door sill height is low, and the door check angles allow the doors to open wide without threatening your hip flexors. This makes the YR an exceptional mobility vehicle for elderly passengers who struggle with taller SUVs. The visibility is excellent, with slim A-pillars and a commanding view of the road ahead.

Electrical behaviors are where you need to bring your multimeter. The **12V battery** is frequently the victim of parasitic drain. The dashboard unit, alarm module, and ECU tend to sip current when locked. If you park this car for three days and it wakes up dead, trace the parasitic draw—often traced to a glovebox light switch stuck in the 'on' position or an aftermarket stereo wired incorrectly. Connectivity is rudimentary; the YR lacks native smartphone integration. Expect a mono or basic colour screen (depending on optional packs), USB ports are rare on the 2007 spec, making the **Aux input** the holy grail for music lovers. If the Aux port is cracked or loose, you're stuck with FM static. Keep a backup auxiliary cable duct-taped to the sun visor.

##### Mechanically
Toyota's engineering reputation here is built on the premise of "it works until you stop working on it." These cars are mechanically robust, but they demand a level of routine maintenance that owners often neglect once the warranty expires. The general consensus is that the mechanicals are sound, but the ancillary components fail predictably.

High-priority security vulnerabilities are worth noting. While the YR relies on a traditional transponder immobiliser, some variations equipped with early smart-key systems fall prey to relay attacks where thieves amplify the key fob signal from inside your house to unlock the car. If yours has the push-button start upgrade, invest in a **Faraday bag** for the key or install a physical steering column lock to deter opportunistic smash-and-grabs. For non-smart key models, the CAN bus injection exploit isn't a threat yet—these units aren't susceptible to remote ECU flashing—but the door locks themselves can be pried if the latch mechanism wears. Install a secondary anti-theft device, like a hidden fuel cut-off switch or a Kryptlock-style ignition collar. It won't stop a professional thief, but it will absolutely ruin their Tuesday afternoon.

##### Engines
All powertrain variants in this review share the same atmospheric philosophy. There are no turbochargers to explode, no direct-injection carbon buildup, and no hybrid batteries to replace for a kidney. It's pure, simple petrol engineering.

* **1.3-litre 1299 cc Petrol Variant**
 This engine, often referenced in specifications as the **1299 cc** unit [cite: chadstonetoyota.com.au], produces modest outputs but prioritises longevity over pace. The primary failure point to scrutinise is the **timing chain tensioner**. These engines use a hydraulic tensioner that loses prime if oil changes are stretched beyond intervals. Listen for a metallic rattling on cold starts—that's the chain whipping against the cover. Over time, the plastic tensioner guides also fragment, sending debris through the oil gallery.
 
 Coolant issues manifest via the **water pump**. Early iterations utilised ceramic impellers that could crack under thermal stress, leading to sudden overheating. Inspect the coolant hoses connected to the pump for swelling or leakage. The **coolant bypass valves** are generally bulletproof, but the thermostat housing gaskets tend to harden, resulting in slow drips near the alternator.
 
 Preventative maintenance is non-negotiable. While Toyota quotes service intervals, the reality of Australian conditions demands adherence to **10,000 km** intervals for severe usage. This includes oil and filter changes, plus a critical inspection of the timing chain tension and valve clearance adjustments. Delaying this past 100,000 km invites catastrophic noise and potential interference damage if the tensioner fails completely.

##### Transmission
The transmission landscape offers two paths, and your choice dictates your long-term pain levels.

**4-Speed Automatic:** This is the **Aisin Warner AW71** series torque converter auto. It's legendary for three things: reliability, simplicity, and laziness. It survives neglect better than any dual-clutch gearbox on the planet. However, the shift behavior is notoriously vague. It takes its sweet time hunting gears, often kicking down unnecessarily on inclines. As mileage climbs, watch for **converter shudder** during acceleration between 40–60 km/h. This indicates degraded fluid friction modifiers or worn clutch plates inside the torque converter itself. At high kilometre marks, the solenoid pack in the valve body can stick, causing harsh shifts or failing to engage fourth gear. Fluid changes are vital; if the fluid smells burnt, the internals are wearing. Never force-shift through the gates; the detents are weak.

**5-Speed Manual:** A basic cable-operated layout with a single-diaphragm clutch. The failure modes are mundane: **clutch slave cylinder** leaks are common, turning your garage floor into an oil slick and killing the pedal feel. The throw-out bearing may develop a whine under load. Shifting should be crisp; if the gearbox feels notchy, the gear cables require lubrication or adjustment, not a rebuild. The synchros are durable, but aggressive third-gear grinding signals worn synchronizer rings.

#### 3. Should you buy a used 2007 Toyota Yaris YR?

The numbers tell a story of total depreciation surrender. According to **live NSW dealer data**, the market is effectively frozen with just one active listing priced at **AUD $6,990**, carrying a median odometer reading of **194,070 km**, and entirely skewed toward the **automatic** transmission variant. This scarcity suggests dealers are either clearing out the final remnants of stock or hoarding gems, but realistically, this reflects a market saturated with high-kilometre ex-company cars.

Price ranges in the wild currently stretch from roughly **$5,500** for projects with questionable histories up to a ceiling of **$7,500** for examples that have been pampered with full service records and lower kilometre counts (e.g., sub-150,000 km). Compared to core segment rivals, the Yaris YR exhibits a baseline percentage drop of nearly **98%** from its original RMDS value. It has lost more money than a crypto scam. But that's your advantage. You're buying stability. When this car drops to zero value, someone else is losing money, not you.

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) calculation strategy is straightforward. Fuel consumption figures hover around **7.5 L/100km** combined in real-world mix. At current petrol prices, this burns about **$30–$35 per day** on a 20,000 km year. Insurance will be rock-bottom, likely under **$300 per annum** for comprehensive cover if you're over 25. Servicing is cheap; expect **$200–$250** per visit at an independent Toyota specialist versus **$600+** at the franchised dealership. Paying a market premium for alternative powertrains is irrelevant here because there are no hybrids or EVs in this generation to compete with. The conventional ICE Yaris is the mathematical winner simply because there is no electric tax, no battery replacement risk, and no proprietary scan tool requirement.

**Buying Recommendation:**
Yes, buy it, but strictly on your terms. The optimal financial sweet spot is a **manual transmission** model with under **180,000 km** and a service booklet that proves regular oil changes. If you can only find automatics, cap your bid lower. An automatic at 194,000 km is gambling; the torque converter is likely nearing the end of its energy absorption capacity. Insist on a pre-purchase inspection focusing on the **timing chain rattle** and **transmission fluid condition**.

Avoid the YR if you desire luxury, connectivity, or performance. This car will get you to work, it will survive a dent in the shopping centre bay, and it will never judge your life choices. Just don't try to beat a Golf GTI to the next set of lights, and for heaven's sake, change the oil every ten thousand kilometres. If you respect the contract, this little Yaris will respect you with thirty more years of reliable transport.
