#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

The Ferrari **400 GT** is a singular chapter in Maranello’s endurance-testing playbook, manufactured between 1976 and 1979 as the direct evolution of the **365 GT4 2+2**. Internally recognised through Ferrari’s sequential alphanumeric coding, it sits squarely in the brand’s flagship four-seat grand touring hierarchy. It replaced the outgoing **365 GT4**, bridged the gap until the introduction of the fuel-injected **400 i** in 1979, and ultimately paved the architectural runway for the **412** (1985–1989). If you visualise Ferrari’s pre-mid-engined V12 era as a three-ring circus, the 400 GT was the heavy-lifting centrepiece designed for cross-continent motorway cruising rather than track-day heroics. https://silodrome.com/ferrari-365-gt4-22/

Underneath the hand-beaten aluminium and steel bodywork lies a longitudinal front-engine architecture paired with a rear-mounted five-speed transaxle. This backbone is mechanically interchangeable with the broader **365 GT4 2+2 / 400 / 400i / 412** family, meaning suspension uprights, steering racks, differential carriers, and wiring loom segments share platform-level commonality across two decades of production. That parts-bin generosity is a quiet blessing in a market where vintage Italian component lead times can stretch longer than a Melbourne tram ride during a snowstorm. https://www.supercars.net/blog/all-brands/ferrari/ferrari-model-list/

For the Australian right-hand-drive import landscape, the **400 GT** offers a choice of powertrain and driver interface:
- **Engine:** Stretched to **4.8-litre** displacement from its predecessor’s 4.4L, producing roughly 340 bhp at 5,600 rpm.
- **Transmission:** Either a **GM Turbo-Hydramatic 400 (THM400)** three-speed automatic or a **ZF S5-25** five-speed manual transaxle.
- **Drive Configuration:** Rear-wheel drive via a live rear axle with de Dion tube suspension and Panhard rod lateral location.
- **Australian Fuel Reality:** Expect genuine road figures in the **24–29 L/100km** bracket in urban settings, dipping to roughly 18 L/100km on open highways. There is no official Australian ADR fuel efficiency labelling for a machine that predates the emissions testing frameworks we now treat as gospel.

In the global used supercar arena, the **400 GT** trades territory with the Aston Martin **Lagonda**, the Mercedes-Benz **SEC (W126)**, and the Bentley **Mulsanne (Series III)**. It does not chase Lamborghinas or Porsches; it chases luggage space, autocratic torque delivery, and the ability to swallow two adults and a weekend’s worth of hardware without demanding constant babysitting. In Australia, it competes against private collectors willing to trade liquidity for a rolling piece of Pininfarina sculptural history.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Ferrari 400Gt?

##### Exterior
Panel alignment on a 1976–1979 Maranello-built grand tourer is less about factory tolerance and more about artisan negotiation. The sheet metal was largely formed by hand and fitted over wooden bucks, which means you will encounter inconsistent gap widths along the boot lip, door seams, and bonnet edges. Gloss black trim mouldings and chromed window surrounds are notorious for micro-scratching during valet washes, and once that clear coat gives up, it flakes rather than oxidises. Paint durability leans towards brittle single-stage enamel finishes; UV exposure across coastal NSW corridors accelerates surface crazing, particularly on roof panels and trailing deck lids. 

Regarding modern safety tech: this car carries zero autonomous emergency braking, lane departure warnings, or blind-spot monitoring. Attempting to cannibalise an ECU from a contemporary donor to implant rudimentary reverse sensors usually short-circuits the instrument cluster and voids any remaining insurability. The authentic approach is to upgrade stopping power with vented rotors, AP Racing calipers, and modern DOT4/5.1 brake fluid, accepting that physics still governs a 1.8-tonne Italian saloon.

##### Interior
Cabin longevity under strenuous duty is a non-starter. These seats were upholstered in Connolly leather and wool-blend carpets intended for polite motorway jaunts, not rideshare mileage or daily school-run abuse. Repeated ingress/egress cracks the foam damping layers and splits the side bolster stitching within five years. Door sill heights sit relatively low thanks to the flat-floor tunnel design, but door check angles are narrow; the thick B-pillar and deep window apertures mean shoulder clearance is generous, but knee room in the tiny rear bench is theoretically functional rather than practically comfortable.

Electrical behaviour follows predictable vintage patterns. The **12V battery drain** typically originates from corroded ground straps bonding the chassis to the bulkhead, failing alternator brushes, or parasitic draws from retrofitted stereos wired directly to the fuse box without relay isolation. Infotainment hardware dates to a blank dashboard faceplate; most surviving examples house a modern double-DIN head unit mounted behind a custom facia, connected via ISO adapters to preserve the OEM clock, heater controls, and voltmeter. Connectivity reliability hinges entirely on whether the previous owner ran data cables alongside the original loom or stapled them to the firewall. Insulation breakdown on aged fabric-wrapped wires leads to intermittent fault codes even though the car lacks OBD-II diagnostics.

##### Mechanically
Ferrari’s engineering philosophy during this era prioritised thermal capacity and low-end grunt over electronic refinement. The chassis rigidity is impressive, but the ancillary components age predictably. Security vulnerabilities today do not involve CAN bus injection exploits or smartphone keyless bypass hacks; the ignition relies on a physical barrel lock and keyed stalk switches. However, the absence of immobilisers makes the **400 GT** highly susceptible to opportunistic mechanical theft. Alloy wheels, early stainless exhaust manifolds, and pristine carbon-trimmed dashboards become magnetised targets in unmonitored street parking. Counter-measures should include:
- Hard-mounted wheel nut locking rings and under-bonnet bolt-down plates
- Secondary mechanical steering column clamps with visible decoy keys
- Geofenced GPS/accelerometer trackers hardwired into the fuse panel (bypassing the accessory circuit)
- Secure, climate-controlled storage with vibration-dampening ramps to protect worn suspension bushes from dry-rot fatigue

##### Engines
Every variant of the Colombo-derived straight-six-upscaled-to-V12 architecture carries distinct ageing signatures. Maintenance cadence for a machine of this era demands aggressive intervention: **5,000 km maximum** for synthetic mineral blends or period-correct castor-free oils, with brass mesh oil pump strainers cleaned annually. Valve train inspection runs at 12-month intervals regardless of kilometreage. Brake hydraulic fluid must be flushed every two years due to hygroscopic absorption. Coolant replacements require distilled water and silicate-free inhibitors to protect soldered radiators and aluminum cylinder heads.

- ***4.4L Colombo V12***
 The foundation unit suffered from Weber DCOE carburettor gasket embrittlement and float needle starvation after sitting dormant. Coolant bypass valves housed within the timing cover tend to leak externally onto the crank pulley. High-pressure fuel lines often exhibit permeation sweating at the banjo bolt threads. Preventative overhaul includes reconditioning the SU/Weber assemblies, replacing all neoprene fuel supply hoses, and installing updated silicone thermostat housings.

- ***4.8L Colombo V12***
 Introducing larger bore dimensions improved mid-range torque but magnified vibration through the monocoque floorpan. Head gasket failures typically trace back to uneven torque sequencing during past rebuilds or overheating episodes caused by blocked radiator cap pressure screens. The camshaft drive chain elongates past specification near 150,000 km equivalent, manifesting as a metallic rattle on cold starts. Servicing requires precise distributor point gap verification (0.35 mm), dwell angle adjustment via external tachometer, and reinforcement of the vacuum advance diaphragm hose routing away from hot exhaust manifolds.

- ***4.8L Bosch K-Jetronic EFI***
 Transitioning to mechanical fuel injection in 1979 eliminated carb sync headaches but introduced new failure vectors. The K-Jetronic centrifugal air divider seals degrade over time, causing lean misfires under light throttle cruise. Coolant temperature sensor resistances drift out of tolerance, enriching the mixture unnecessarily and raising consumption figures. Cooling vent filters located inside the air intake plenum frequently clog with pollen and highway dust, starving the idle mixture control units. Recommended service schedule emphasizes checking all o-rings on the fuel distributor, cleaning thermovalve actuators with contact spray, and verifying boost control piston travel remains unbinding.

##### Transmission
The power delivery path diverges sharply depending on whether you encounter the American-sourced automatic or the European-spec manual. 

- **GM Turbo-Hydramatic 400 (THM400) Three-Speed Automatic:** This torque converter box was married to the **400 GT** to satisfy North American comfort expectations, but it remains exceptionally robust when maintained. Known failure modes include hardened transmission cooler lines that fracture under Australian summer highway heat, leading to slow fluid loss and eventual band slippage. Shift flare on second-to-third upshiftes indicates worn accumulator piston seals. Harsh first-gear engagements often stem from degraded flexplate bolts loosening over decades of vibration, introducing harmonic chatter through the mounts. Preventative care involves draining and refilling ATF every 20,000 km with Dexron II/III specification fluid, flushing the remote cooler loop, and inspecting the dipstick tube sealing o-rings for dehydration cracks.

- **ZF S5-25 Five-Speed Manual Transaxle:** When left in factory condition, the manual gearbox delivers crisp mechanical feedback but suffers from gnarled synchro synchronisation rings and stiff cable-operated linkages. Selector fork wear produces vague gate movement, particularly from third to fourth ratio. The clutch hydraulic master/slave cylinders develop internal seal blowouts, resulting in pedal sag and incomplete disengagement. Maintenance requires periodic lubrication of the selector shaft bushes using high-temp grease, replacement of the diaphragm spring fingers, and bleeding the clutch reservoir using fresh DOT4 fluid while the system is warm to purge trapped moisture pockets.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Ferrari 400Gt?

Current market positioning reveals a paradox wrapped in velvet. Live NSW dealer data shows a single active listing priced at a symbolic AUD $40, with a median odometer reading of 100 km and an automatic transmission mix of 1. This obviously reflects a placeholder or token holding rather than commercial reality. In the actual Australian collector ecosystem, properly documented **400 GT** examples command between **AUD $650,000 and $1,100,000** depending on Classiche certification status, restoration provenance, and originality of running gear. Restored variants consistently hold valuation better than heavily modified equivalents, because authenticity drives liquidity. https://www.ferrarichat.com/forum/threads/1977-400gt.640746/page-2

Depreciation metrics completely invert the conventional curve. While mainstream Japanese and German sedans shed 40–55% of their capital over three years, the **400 GT** has appreciated approximately 12–18% annually in recent cycles, outpacing core segment rivals like the W126 SEC or Series III Bentley Mulsanne. The asset class rewards patience, correct documentation, and minimal cosmetic tampering.

Total cost of ownership demands a clear-eyed calculation strategy. You are not purchasing transportation; you are financing a rolling museum exhibit that occasionally moves under its own power. Annual expenses typically break down as follows:
- Specialised maintenance contracts: **$18,000–$35,000** (covering valve adjustments, suspension bush refresh, leather conservation, and electronics recalibration)
- Storage and climate control: **$6,000–$12,000** (dedicated garage hire with humidity regulation to prevent leather delamination and loom oxidation)
- Insurance premiums: **$8,000–$15,000** (agreed value policies require independent valuations and restricted usage clauses)
- Consumables: Fuel averages **~26 L/100km**; at current RON98 pricing in NSW, expect **$1.10–$1.50 per kilometre** driven

Does paying a market premium for alternative powertrains deliver mathematical long-term savings? Irrelevant. Conventional combustion versus hybrid or electric paradigms collapse entirely when applied to a 1970s grand tourer. The financial sweet spot exists strictly in preserving factory configuration: retaining the original **GM Turbo-Hydramatic 400** if your priority is period-correct cruising comfort, or securing a professionally rebuilt **ZF S5-25** if you value mechanical engagement and resale velocity among purists.

Buying recommendation: Target a **1977–1978** chassis carrying complete factory tool kits, original jack layouts, matching-numbers engine stampings, and an unbroken paper trail through reputable Australian marque specialists. Avoid vehicles with repainted body panels lacking underlying filler transparency reports, or examples that have undergone electrical "modernisation" involving undocumented fuse box modifications. The optimal transaction aligns with acquisition of a numbers-matching automatic variant accompanied by verified Classiche authentication. Pay upfront for proof of originality; negotiate aggressively only on cars bearing excessive aftermarket additions that compromise historical integrity. In the Australian used car market, the **400 GT** rewards the buyer who respects craftsmanship over convenience, and punishes the speculator who treats classic metals like quarterly dividend payouts.
