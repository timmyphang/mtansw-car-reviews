#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

- Generation covered: ninth-generation Lancer (commonly sold in Australia from about 2007 → 2017, broadly refreshed through to 2016/2017). Internal/technical lineage: the later “Lancer EX / Lancer (2007–2017)” family that shares Mitsubishi’s compact monocoque architecture and MIVEC engines. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Lancer) and manufacturer history pages list the timeline and engine families used across these years.
- Where it sits in the Mitsubishi line-up: compact family sedan — below Outlander/Outlander PHEV and above smaller Mirage/Eclipse Cross in overall showroom positioning; the sporty Evolution variants sit several rungs above as halo/performance models. Official history shows how the Lancer occupied the compact-sedan slot for decades. [Mitsubishi Lancer history](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/lancer-history).
- Platform / shared architecture: the late Lancer shares global compact-platform underpinnings used across regional Lancer variants and relatives; this is why many mechanical parts, engine families and some electrical modules are shared with other Mitsubishi small/compact models and Evo derivatives (helpful for parts availability). Consolidated spec sites summarise those platform and engine crossovers. [AutoData1](https://www.autodata1.com/en/car/mitsubishi/lancer), [UltimateSpecs](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Mitsubishi-models/Mitsubishi-Lancer).
- Engines, outputs, transmissions and drivetrains found in the Aussie market (selection across later 2008–2016 model years):
  - 1.6L MIVEC — typically the **4A92** in world specs (small petrol 4‑cyl). 5‑speed manual or CVT/automatic depending on market/spec. [Auto-Data / Autoevolution](https://www.autoevolution.com/mitsubishi/lancer/).
  - 2.0L naturally aspirated petrol — **4B11** (non‑turbo) used widely in 2.0 variants; paired with 5‑speed manual or INVECS‑III CVT/automatic. Power ~110–125 kW depending on tune and year. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Lancer), [MotorReviewer](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=124).
  - 2.4L (in some markets) and turbocharged 2.0 **4B11T / 4G63** found in Ralliart / Evo derivatives (rare as regular used‑car buys). [Mitsubishi history / MotorReviewer](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/lancer-history), [MotorReviewer](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=124).
  - Drivetrains: Front‑wheel drive in standard models; all‑wheel drive on Evolution/rally variants only.
  - Transmissions: 5‑speed manuals, conventional torque‑converter automatics on older trims, and INVECS‑III CVT (continuously variable transmission) on many post‑2010/2012 models. [Autoevolution, AutoData1](https://www.autoevolution.com/mitsubishi/lancer/).
- Marketplace rivals in Australia: Subaru Impreza / WRX (for sporty variants), Honda Civic, Nissan Pulsar/Sentra (depending on year), Volkswagen Jetta/Golf, Ford Focus — compact‑sedan/hatchbacks that buyers typically compare with a Lancer. [The Car Connection competitor list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/mitsubishi_lancer_choices).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mitsubishi Lancer?

Exterior
- Paint and clearcoat: clearcoat peeling, flaking and hood/roof paint failures are a recurring owner complaint — look for UV-related clearcoat failing on horizontal panels (hood, roof). Expect spot repairs or respray costs if you don’t want visible sun-damage; RepairPal and owner complaint aggregators list numerous reports. [RepairPal paint-peeling reports](https://repairpal.com/problem/mitsubishi/lancer/paint-peeling-on-hood-and-roof), [CarComplaints paint list](https://m.carcomplaints.com/Mitsubishi/Lancer/body_paint/).
- Panel alignment and trim: generally conservative Japanese build tolerances — but check cheaper private imports for uneven gaps or poor-quality refinish. Gloss‑black and piano‑black trims (if fitted) scratch easily; chrome trim holds up better but shows pitting with age.
- Corrosion / structural: not a broad endemic like some older Japanese models, but check lower rear finish panels on earlier 2004–2007 sedans for cracking/water ingress if you’re looking at older years. [Go‑Parts rear finish panel notes](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/rear-body-panel-finish-panel-mitsubishi-lancer-2004-2007).
- Recalls and safety systems: the model line is affected historically by the Takata airbag inflator campaigns on certain early 2000s Lancers — always check VIN/recall status. Mitsubishi’s recall portal is the primary Australian/region route for VIN checks. [Mitsubishi recall lookup](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/recall), and NHTSA recall tool for US recall history if you’re importing. [NHTSA Recall page](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls).
- ADAS quirks: ADAS on later Lancers (adaptive cruise/forward collision systems where fitted) has a small set of owner complaints — sensors can be fussy in heavy rain or if the calibration is out after a repair. Owner‑reported complaints exist; if the car has radar‑based cruise or AEB fitted, demand the pre‑purchase ADAS calibration history and test on motorway speeds. [CarProblemZoo adaptive cruise complaints](https://carproblemzoo.com/mitsubishi/lancer/adaptive-cruise-control-problems.php), [I-CAR ADAS guidance](https://rts.i-car.com/oem-information/mitsubishi/mitsubishi-adas.html).

Interior
- Wear and trim cracking: dashboards and A‑pillar trims can craze and crack under heavy sun exposure — common on 2008–2017 interiors. Owners recommend sun‑screens or ceramic window tints to slow degradation. [CarInterior dashboard cracking guide](https://carinterior.alibaba.com/tips/lancer-dashboard-cracking-fix).
- Hard‑wear suitability for rideshare/taxi: cloth seats and basic plastics are durable, but cheap finish on door sills and handles will show age; high‑use examples often have loose door check stops and squeaky trims. If the car has been used as rideshare, expect interior wear beyond what odometer suggests — check seat bolsters, rear seat base springs and the door check angles for fatigued catches.
- Entry/exit ergonomics: the Lancer’s ride height and door aperture are friendly for most Australians — not low‑slung like a sports coupe, and the rear sill height is reasonable for kids and elderly. Still physically check the door check pivots at full open — cheap bushings wear on high‑use cars.
- Electrical and infotainment:
  - The MMCS 7‑inch infotainment/touchscreen used across Lancer/Outlander/Evo of this era is known to freeze, require resets or fail backlights on older units. The hardware is integrated into the CAN bus, so replacements aren’t always plug‑and‑play. [MMCS overview / replacement notes](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/infotainment-display-mitsubishi-lancer-mitsubishi-outlander-mitsubishi-lancer-evolution-2007-2016).
  - Watch for 12V battery drain patterns on cars with aftermarket audio or poor wiring repairs — owners report parasitic draws from aftermarket alarms or poorly repaired audio installs. CarComplaints and RepairPal note general electrical gremlins across the range. [CarComplaints Lancer issues](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Mitsubishi/Lancer/).
  - Screen sizes vary by trim and market year — earlier basic trims have smaller non-touch radios; later mid/high trims offer 7‑inch touch units. Ask which firmware version is installed; some firmware updates fixed Bluetooth/dropout issues in later years (ask dealer for software history).

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation: Mitsubishi built hard‑working small engines and simple suspensions — generally robust, but reliability is a mixed bag across the years. Owner complaint sites show recurring powertrain and transmission faults that skew overall reliability impressions. [CarComplaints aggregated](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Mitsubishi/Lancer/), [RepairPal summary](https://repairpal.com/problems/mitsubishi/lancer).
- Security vulnerabilities (modern theft techniques): like many older vehicles with an accessible CAN network, Lancers are potentially vulnerable to CAN‑injection style thefts where attackers access lighting wiring/chassis connectors to inject unlock/start messages. Academic and press writeups outline how attackers exploit unauthenticated CAN broadcast messages — not unique to Mitsubishi. Be pragmatic and fit an aftermarket CAN‑aware immobiliser if you’re worried. Read more on the attack and mitigation options: [CAN injection explainer / The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/), [Dr Ken Tindell CAN injection note](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/).
- Aftermarket anti‑theft advice: a properly installed CAN‑aware alarm/immobiliser (e.g., Autowatch Ghost 2) adds a concealed authentication layer that stops the car starting even if the factory immobiliser is bypassed — strongly recommended for cheaper, older Lancers parked on the street overnight. [Autowatch Ghost 2 installer page example](https://www.carsoundsecurity.com/product/ghost-2-can-bus-immobiliser-by-autowatch-for-mitsubishi-fully-fitted/).

Engines
- Note on timing systems: the Lancer family used both timing belts and timing chains depending on engine/generation. It’s critical to identify the exact engine code before purchase — a snapped timing belt on an interference engine can be catastrophic. Guides stress the 2008 redesign changed timing systems for some engines — check the engine code and service history. [CarsCounsel timing belt/chain guide](https://carscounsel.com/mitsubishi-lancer-timing-belt-or-chain/).

For each engine variant below, the title is italicised with the engine code and variant, followed by known failure points and recommended service intervals (Australian use: compare 10,000 km vs 15,000 km service frequency).

- *4A92 — 1.6L MIVEC (small petrol 4‑cyl)*
  - Failure points: general longevity is OK but watch for early oil consumption on high‑mileage units and intake valve carbon buildup if oil change intervals have been ignored. Poor service history increases risk of cam/valve issues.
  - Preventative maintenance: oil and filter every 10,000 km for high‑use/urban or 12 months — 10,000 km is safer for rideshare/taxi duties; inspect valve cover/cam seals at 100,000 km.
  - Other: the 1.6L is lighter on fuel (expect ~7.0–8.5 L/100 km in mixed driving for a well‑maintained example) — real‑world figures from owner sites show variation depending on transmission. [Fuelly aggregated owner MPG](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/lancer).

- *4B11 — 2.0L naturally aspirated (MIVEC family)*
  - Failure points: timing‑chain stretch and **MIVEC timing codes (P0012)** have been reported on 2008–2017 examples where chain guides/OCV solenoids wear or oil quality is neglected; owners report cold‑start rattles and chain‑related noises. Excessive oil consumption at higher mileages is also reported. [Go‑Parts P0012 / timing chain info](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0012-mitsubishi-lancer-2008-2017-4b11-4b12-2), [Go‑Parts 4B11 overview](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/parts-cars-trucks-mitsubishi-outlander-sport-mitsubishi-rvr-mitsubishi-lancer-2007-2022).
  - Preventative maintenance: oil + filter every 10,000 km if you do short trips or heavy urban driving; conservative owners push to 10k rather than 15k. Check timing‑chain/tensioner condition at 150,000 km and expect possible guide/tensioner replacement if the car has noisy cold starts. If you find a P0012 code, ask for the TSB/repair history. [TSB notes referenced in technical articles and forums](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0012-mitsubishi-lancer-2008-2017-4b11-4b12-2).

- *4B11T / 4G63 — turbo 2.0L (Ralliart / Evo variants)*
  - Failure points: turbocharger wear, oil contamination, and heavily modified examples with neglected maintenance can show head gasket and bearing issues. These engines were engineered for higher stress and thus need meticulous service history.
  - Preventative maintenance: frequent oil and filter changes (every 7,500–10,000 km), attention to intercooler/oil return lines, and boost control health. If you’re not buying a documented service history performance Lancer, steer clear — repairs get expensive fast. [MotorReviewer 4B11/4B11T background](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=124).

Transmission
- CVT (INVECS‑III) — known failure modes and owner experience:
  - The CVT fitted to many later Lancers developed a reputation for slipping, overheating and torque‑converter clutch (TCC) electrical/solenoid faults in owner reports. Symptoms: high revs with little speed gain, gearbox limp mode, shudder, and overheating warnings. Regular CVT fluid checks and correct Mitsubishi CVT fluid are essential to longevity. [EngineCrux CVT review](https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-lancer-cvt-transmission-problems-explained/), [YOUCANIC Mitsubishi CVT problems](https://www.youcanic.com/mitsubishi-transmission-problems/).
  - Diagnostic code P0740 is commonly associated with torque‑converter clutch circuit faults on CVT/Lancer automatics and often traces back to solenoid, valve body or degraded fluid. [Go‑Parts P0740 discussion](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0740-mitsubishi-lancer-2007-2017).
  - TSBs and dealer reprogramming have been issued in some markets for CVT ECU updates; ask for transmission service records and whether the dealer performed any ECU reflash work. [NHTSA TSB PDF example referencing CVT ECU reprogramming](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2022/MC-10208827-0001.pdf).
  - Cost reality: CVT rebuilds or replacements can be expensive — often several thousand dollars — and replacement CVTs aren’t cheap on the aftermarket. [CVTMechanic cost overview](https://cvtmechanic.com/mitsubishi/mitsubishi-lancer-cvt-transmission-problems-and-costs/).
- Torque‑converter automatics (non‑CVT):
  - Known failure modes include TCC shudder, clutch wear and valve body issues — diagnostic steps start with fluid condition and pressure checks. Many symptoms mimic CVT issues (shudder/high rev) but mechanical diagnosis differs. [700r4transmissionhq on bad torque converters](https://www.700r4transmissionhq.com/mitsubishi-lancer-bad-torque-converter-signs/).

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mitsubishi Lancer?

- NSW dealer market snapshot (live MTA NSW data provided)
  - Active listings found in the NSW dealer scrape: 6 units.
  - Price range: AUD $8,990 – $51,888. Median price: AUD $12,490.
  - Year range: 2010 – 2016. Median odometer: 49,542 km.
  - Transmission mix in that snapshot: Automatic: 5 units, Manual: 1 unit.
  - Use that as your negotiating baseline — a tidy mid‑spec 2013–2015 example with ~50k km should be around the $11k–$15k band at dealer level given this sample.

- Depreciation metrics (practical guidance, Australian used market reality)
  - Historically the Lancer has already taken the large part of its depreciation hit — buyers in the compact sedan market saw new Lancers lose roughly 40–50% value in the first 3 years when new, similar to segment norms. But because Mitsubishi withdrew the sedan and focus shifted to crossovers, Lancers have softer demand in Australia, so long‑tail depreciation tends to be a bit worse than some rivals — expect an extra few percentage points of value erosion vs. Honda Civic or Subaru Impreza equivalents when comparing similar age/condition units (rough guide: Lancer ~45–55% drop in first 3 years back when new vs rivals ~35–45% for stronger sellers). This broad-stroke estimate follows market behaviour for discontinued sedan lines and owner complaint influence on used demand; use the NSW median prices as your real snapshot. (For deeper historical depreciation studies see buyer guides like CarGurus). [CarGurus buying guide summary](https://www.cargurus.com/research/articles/mitsubishi-lancer-buying-guide).

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to calculate and what matters
  - Key components: purchase price, fuel, scheduled servicing, likely unscheduled repairs (CVT/engine chain risk), insurance, registration/rego and depreciation/resale.
  - Fuel: real‑world consumption across Lancers varies by engine and transmission. Expect roughly:
    - 1.6L: ~7–8.5 L/100 km mixed (lighter duty, manual tends to be lower).
    - 2.0L NA: ~8–10 L/100 km mixed (CVT examples can be thirstier under load).
    - Owner real‑world reports vary — check [Fuelly](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/lancer) and official figures for the exact year/trim.
  - Servicing: plan on scheduled services every 10,000 km for high‑use/urban owners (rideshare/taxi) or 12,000–15,000 km if you do lots of steady highway and follow Mitsubishi’s recommended maintenance — but for the MIVEC timing chain engines, erring to 10,000 km oil changes avoids sludge and oil‑control failures. [Local dealer service intervals vary — check your service book].
  - Risk allowance: add a “CVT repair risk buffer” for CVT‑equipped cars. Practically, set aside $2,000–$5,000 over 5 years as an insurance reserve if you’re buying a CVT example with patchy service history. For manual/transmission conventional automatics, the buffer can be smaller.
  - Hybrid/Electric alternative: Mitsubishi does not sell a Lancer hybrid in Australia — the brand’s electrified technology is concentrated in Outlander PHEV and larger crossovers. [Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV specs](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/outlander-phev/specs).
    - Does paying a premium for an electrified Mitsubishi (Outlander PHEV) make financial sense vs a petrol Lancer? Not directly comparable (different vehicles for different roles), but hybrid/PHEV TCO calculators and analysis (KBB, Edmunds) show that hybrids only break even if you do lots of city driving and keep the vehicle long enough to recoup higher purchase price through fuel savings. [KBB hybrid vs gas break‑even guidance](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/), [Edmunds on hybrid costs](https://www.edmunds.com/fuel-economy/the-real-costs-of-owning-a-hybrid.html). For the Lancer buyer looking purely at compact‑sedan costs, a well‑maintained 2.0L NA or 1.6L manual will be cheaper to buy and maintain than stepping into a PHEV crossover unless you have specific EV/PHEV needs.
  - Insurance & parts: Mitsubishi parts are generally available through dealer networks and aftermarket suppliers thanks to the commonality of engines and platform components — but specialised Evo/Ralliart parts can be pricier.

- Buying recommendation — what to buy, what to avoid
  - Buy if:
    - You want a cheap, conservative compact sedan with a decent parts pool and simple engineering.
    - You find a well‑serviced 2.0L **4B11** (non‑turbo) or 1.6L **4A92** with full service history and <100,000 km for under $15k; NSW data shows at‑dealer median ~$12,490 so you can get fair bargains. Use the dealer snapshot as negotiation leverage.
  - Avoid if:
    - The car has a CVT and no transmission service records or you notice jerking/high rev behaviour — CVT rebuilds can be ruinously expensive. Ask for a CVT fluid change history and any ECU reflashes (TSBs). [CVT issues overview](https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-lancer-cvt-transmission-problems-explained/).
    - It has P0012 or timing MIVEC codes active without documented repair — timing chain stretch can lead to expensive repairs. Ask for proof the issue was fixed with OEM parts. [Go‑Parts on P0012](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0012-mitsubishi-lancer-2008-2017-4b11-4b12-2).
  - Best value sweet spot:
    - Target model years 2012–2015 with a documented service history, ideally the 2.0L manual or a 2.0 CVT with full CVT service records and mileage under 120,000 km. These years got equipment and small updates but haven’t hit the older‑tech extremes; they’re plentiful in the NSW market snapshot (2010–2016 range). CarGurus recommends later 2013–2017 years as better equipped — that correlates with better safety and infotainment fitment. [CarGurus Lancer buying guide](https://www.cargurus.com/research/articles/mitsubishi-lancer-buying-guide).
  - Extra checks to do at pre‑purchase:
    - Get a transmission specialist to road test a CVT example and verify fluid condition/temperatures.
    - Check for infotainment freezing and ask for a touch‑unit reset history; confirm Bluetooth/phone pairing works.
    - VIN recall check: confirm Takata airbag and any other open recalls are completed via Mitsubishi’s recall site. [Mitsubishi recall lookup](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/recall).
    - Ask for the engine code and confirm timing-belt vs chain; if it’s a belt engine, confirm belt replacement history (typically ~100,000 km interval on older belts); if chain, watch timing chain noise and P0012 history. [CarsCounsel timing guide](https://carscounsel.com/mitsubishi-lancer-timing-belt-or-chain/).

Final blunt take: if you want cheap, sensible transport with a bit of Mitsubishi character and you score a low‑kilometre, well‑serviced 2012–2015 Lancer with a clean history and preferably the manual or a CVT with documented fluid services, it’s a useful car. If the price is low because the seller can’t prove CVT or timing chain health, walk away — the saving will evaporate into repair bills. And for God’s sake, if you park it on the street, spend the extra $400–$800 on a modern CAN‑aware immobiliser — prevention is cheaper than the heartbreak of a car that’s suddenly driven off down the lane.
