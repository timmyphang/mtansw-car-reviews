#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

The **2016 Toyota 86 GTS** sits squarely in the first generational cycle (internal designation **ZN6**, built 2012–2020) of Toyota’s return to lightweight, driver-focused sports coupes. In the domestic hierarchy, it bridges the gap between volume-selling hot hatches and the brand’s performance halo, effectively replacing the Celica legacy while deliberately positioning itself below the flagship Supra. It shares the **Toyota Subaru Global Platform (TS-GP)** architecture with the Subaru BRZ and prior-generation Scion FR-S. This shared foundation is a massive advantage for owners: bulkhead components, suspension uprights, brake callipers, and drivetrain mounts are largely interchangeable across the triad, meaning independent specialists and parts distributors like Repco and Supercheap Auto stockpile common wear items cheaply and reliably.

In the Australian market, the 2016 GTS was exclusively fitted with the **FA20** 2.0-litre, naturally aspirated horizontally-opposed four-cylinder engine producing 147 kW and 205 Nm of torque, sending power exclusively through the rear wheels via either a TR60F six-speed manual or an A900 torque-converter automatic. As documented in live dealer inventory builds, compliance units consistently specify the **1998 cc** displacement with petrol delivery [https://www.adelaidevehiclecentre.com.au/cars/used-silver-2016-toyota-86-00047197]. The powertrain layout remains uncompromising: a high-revving, freely breathing flat-four driving the rear axle with a near-perfect front/rear weight distribution [https://blaque.co.za/toyota-86-sports-car-true-sense-word/]. 

Marketplace rivals at launch included the Mazda MX-5 (ND chassis, though softer and lighter), the Nissan 370Z (heavier, more powerful, but outdated tech), and the locally available Ford Mustang V6 (broader appeal but fundamentally different usage case). Today, the 86 fights against used Subaru BRZ equivalents, early Miata imports, and increasingly aggressive crossover SUVs attempting to capture youth dollar budgets. Despite being displaced by the newer **GR86**, the ZN6 retains cult status among weekend canyon runners and track day regulars who value analog feedback over digital assistance.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used 2016 Toyota 86 GTS?

##### Exterior
- Panel alignment on early-mid lifecycle ZN6 bodies is generally tight, but owners frequently report inconsistent front-bumper-to-hood gaps caused by factory shim tolerance variances or post-accident repair imprecision.
- Gloss-black mirror caps, side-window surrounds, and boot handle shrouds suffer rapid micro-scratching under normal Australian wash conditions; ceramic coating mitigates this, but replacement bezels are cheap aftermarket fare.
- Paint durability tracks well with Japanese export standards, though clear-coat oxidation accelerates on unshaded Queensland or Western Australian highways. Minor swirl removal and UV sealant maintenance reset appearance at negligible cost.
- Body recall coverage for 2016 units centres primarily on pre-delivery inspection (PDI) fluid top-ups and occasional door hinge adjustment notices. No structural or crash-safety deflections were issued nationally.
- ADAS landscape: None equipped. The 2016 GTS predates mandatory autonomous emergency braking, blind-spot monitoring, and lane-departure warnings in Australia. Expect zero software sensitivity quirks because the systems don’t exist. Modern buyers must accept this as a feature, not a bug—the car rewards physical attention, not algorithmic babysitting.

##### Interior
- Cabin materials age predictably: brushed aluminium switchgear holds up beautifully, while cloth-trimmed seats show bolster wear after heavy lateral loading or rideshare duty. Leather options resist compression set but crack under sustained desert-heat exposure without conditioner.
- Spatial entry/exit is genuinely excellent for a coupe. Door check angles provide a wide apertures, and floor pan mounting lowers the step-in height significantly compared to muscle cars or grand tourers. Tall drivers (>185 cm) may find headroom tight over time, but hip room remains class-leading.
- Electrical behaviours centre on two predictable failure pathways:
 - **12V battery drain**: Parasitic draw spikes (0.8–1.2A) occur when the instrument cluster CAN gateway fails to sleep after key-off, often triggered by aftermarket dashcams hardwired to constant live circuits rather than switched ignition feeds.
 - **Infotainment hardware**: The base GTS utilises a standard Toyota audio head unit without Apple CarPlay. Screen size settles at a functional 6.1-inch display with physical knobs for climate control—a deliberate ergonomic choice that avoids glass-panel fatigue. Connectivity relies on legacy Bluetooth stacks prone to multipoint pairing drift; firmware refreshes haven’t been officially released for the MY16 ECU, making Uconnect or Android Auto receiver replacements a popular workaround.
- Switchgear endurance is high. Window regulators, lock actuators, and HVAC blend doors rarely fail before 150,000 km, provided moisture doesn’t breach the cabin air filter housing during river-crossing or monsoon-season washing.

##### Mechanically
- Toyota’s engineering philosophy shines through in chassis rigidity and NVH (noise, vibration, harshness) tuning. The ZN6’s welded-in-house strut tower braces and reinforced lower crossmember deliver predictable roll stiffness without wallowing into body-roll sickness. General brand reputation leans toward over-engineered longevity, though the sports-car orientation means bushings, tyres, and pads degrade faster than Corolla norms.
- Security vulnerabilities are systemic to era-appropriate immobilisers. The factory EWS/Transponder system lacks rolling-code encryption strength against K-line diagnostic spoofing or OBD-port relay attacks. CAN bus injection exploits allow unauthorised cranking cycles if the port isn’t physically isolated.
 - Physical countermeasures: Install a certified steering-wheel club (visible deterrent alone reduces opportunistic theft by ~60%) and route a secondary immobiliser kill-switch behind the glovebox or kick panel.
 - Digital countermeasures: Fit an OBD-II port blocker with tamper alert, disable keyless-go proximity reception via fuse pull when parked overnight, and monitor VIN-linked telematics for unauthorised CAN traffic using aftermarket fleet-trackers with ECU read capabilities.

##### Engines
**_FA20 FSE_**
- Component failure pathways are highly specific to direct-injection architecture and high-RPM operation:
 - **Carbon deposition on intake valves**: Port fuel injection was eliminated to improve thermal efficiency, meaning methyl-elegant residues accumulate silently on upstream valve faces. Symptoms manifest as rough cold starts, misfire under load, and compressed-air blowback readings exceeding 18% at idle.
 - **Coolant bypass valve & thermostat housing**: Plastic composite housings develop hairline stress fractures along mounting tabs, leading to slow weep patterns near the exhaust manifold. Coolant loss triggers lean-running conditions and erratic fan cycling.
 - **PCV gasket & oil filter housing seal**: High crankcase pressure from forced-induction-style boost management (even in NA form) stresses rubber matrix seals. Oil seepage onto serpentine belts causes squeal, while low oil levels risk cam bearing scoring past 120,000 km.
 - **High-pressure fuel pump diaphragm**: The rotary-type pump feeding the direct injectors experiences membrane fatigue, resulting in momentary fuel starvation during sustained wide-open throttle events or steep incline acceleration.
- Preventative maintenance intervals: Toyota mandates service every **15,000 km or 12 months**, whichever occurs first. However, enthusiast consensus and independent workshop telemetry recommend halving that to **10,000 km** for oil, filter, and spark plug inspection cycles. The FA20 runs hot under track duty; switching to fully synthetic 0W-20 or 5W-30 API SP-spec oil prevents piston ring land erosion and maintains hydraulic lifter clearance.

##### Transmission
- **TR60F (6-speed Manual)**: Mechanical behaviour is crisp with defined gate travel and short throw. Known failure modes cluster around third/fourth synchrono wear when drivers skip shifts aggressively, and slave cylinder seals drying out past 100,000 km, causing drag or incomplete disengagement. Clutch plate thickness typically drops below 2mm limit around 130,000–150,000 km depending on street versus circuit bias. Flywheel resurfacing is non-negotiable during replacements.
- **A900 / A960E (Torque Converter Automatic)**: Shifting logic prioritises smoothness over engagement speed, resulting in noticeable pause between second-third transitions and hesitation from standstill. Solenoid stack fatigue manifests as hunting in Drive, delayed kick-down responses, and occasional clunking on torque converter lockup engagement. Fluid degradation accelerates if replaced outside manufacturer timelines; the sealed-for-life marketing is obsolete. Flushing at **80,000 km** intervals preserves clutch pack friction material and prevents valve-body chatter. Gearbox rebuilds are common past 160,000 km on high-mileage examples, but DIY fluid exchanges routinely extend life another 50,000 km.

#### 3. Should you buy a used 2016 Toyota 86 GTS?

- **Current used marketplace pricing**: Live NSW dealer data (snapshot from MTA NSW scraper) shows active listings concentrated at AUD $24,990 – $24,990, with a median price of AUD $24,990, tracking odometers around 112,638 km, and exclusively offering the Sports Automatic variant. Broader Australian national markets currently see competitive entry pricing at AUD $18,500 for higher-kilometre manual examples, scaling to a ceiling of AUD $28,500+ for sub-60,000 km GTS packages with factory navigation and clean history reports.
- **Depreciation metrics**: New MSRPs hovered near AUD $39,000–$42,000 at launch. Five-year depreciation sits at approximately 48–52%, noticeably outperforming mainstream compact SUVs and sedan segments which typically bleed 60–70% over the same horizon. Sports coupe niche demand, limited supply of clean-ZN6 examples, and the cultural shift away from turbo-reliance sustain residual values better than peer predictions suggest.
- **Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) calculation strategy**: Running a 2016 GTS domestically averages 9.5–10.8 L/100km combined urban/highway mixing depending on driving style and ambient temperature. At current Australian unleaded rates (~AUD $1.85/L), annual fuel expenditure for 15,000 km sits near AUD $2,500. Servicing every 10,000 km at independent specialists costs roughly AUD $350–$450 per visit, including minor inspections and fluid top-ups. Insurance premiums hover mid-tier due to performance classification but decrease significantly with safe-driver discounts and garage storage. Paying a market premium for alternative powertrains isn’t mathematically viable here—there are no hybrid or EV variants in the ZN6 lineage, and retrofitting electric conversion kits exceeds AUD $40,000 while destroying handling dynamics. Sticking to conventional NA ICE delivers predictable maintenance curves, straightforward diagnostics, and zero range-anxiety during weekend autocross trips or mountain passes.
- **Buying recommendation**: Target a **2016–2017 MY GTS with manual transmission and under 130,000 km**, serviced according to the stricter 10,000 km protocol. Avoid vehicles with visible roll-cage welds lacking TÜV or FIA certification, modified supercharger setups bypassing OEM cooling, or heavily discounted automatics showing patchy transmission service history. The optimal financial sweet spot lands on unmodified, logbook-complete examples priced between AUD $20,000–$23,000. They deliver genuine driver engagement, modest operating expenses, and depreciation floors that protect your capital better than any practical daily runner. If you want a car that demands respect rather than apologising for it, the 2016 Toyota 86 GTS remains one of the few remaining affordable machines that forces you to earn your smile—and still costs less than half a tank of premium unleaded to keep doing it.
