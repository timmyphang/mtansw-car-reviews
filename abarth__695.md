#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation & codes: This review covers the Abarth 695 (Type 312 family — the high‑performance derivative of the Fiat 500 run from roughly 2012 through to 2024 in various special‑edition forms). The 695 badge is effectively the top‑tier, factory‑prepared version of the modern Abarth 500/595 lineup; Evo’s long review summarises the 695’s place across the full run [Evo review](https://www.evo.co.uk/abarth/500/207100/abarth-695-2012-2024-review-why-well-miss-italys-mini-cooper-s-rival).
- Product hierarchy: In Abarth’s small‑car pecking order the 695 sits above the Abarth 595 and the standard Fiat 500 Abarth derivatives — it’s the no‑compromise, hand‑prepared, limited‑edition sibling of the 595 line [Abarth official pages](https://www.abarth.com/new-abarth-595-695).
- Shared platform & parts commonality: The 695 is built on the Fiat 500 (Type 312) architecture shared with Abarth 595 and Fiat 500 variants — that means wide parts availability for consumables (brakes, bushes, interior trim) and plenty of independent Fiat/Abarth specialist support in Australia and Europe [Abarth product page and specs aggregators](https://www.abarth.com/695-rivale), [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/abarth-695-generation-5001).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive:
  - Engine family: 1.368 L (1.4 L) turbocharged inline‑4 T‑Jet variants with outputs commonly in the 165–190 hp range depending on edition. Competizione/Rivale editions generally quoted at ~180 hp / 132 kW and 250 Nm torque [Stellantis tech sheet PDF](https://www.media.stellantis.com/uploads/em/2012/ABARTH/SCHEDE_TECNICHE/120917_AB_695_maserati_eng.pdf), [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/abarth-695-generation-5001).
  - Transmissions offered: 5‑speed manual (traditional) and the robotised automated manual often called Dualogic/AGS (sometimes referenced as an electrically‑operated manual or “Abarth Competizione” automated system depending on edition) — both exist across model years [Fiat NZ buyer’s guide (Final Edition)](https://www-aem-origin.fiat.co.nz/content/dam/cross-regional/asean/fiat/en_nz/brochure/2024/Abarth%20695%20Final%20Edition%20-%20November%202024_Web.pdf).
  - Drive layout: Front‑wheel drive (FWD).
- Direct marketplace rivals: Mini Cooper S / John Cooper Works (3‑door) and the small hot‑hatch segment (older Ford Fiesta STs, Volkswagen Polo GTI equivalents in spirit). Industry comparators list the Mini as the primary competitor in character and price positioning [CarExpert comparison](https://www.carexpert.com.au/abarth/695/vs/mini/cooper), [YallaMotor coverage](https://www.yallamotor.com/news/the-2025-abarth-695%3A-competition-and-rivalry-in-the-automotive-world-33546).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Abarth 695?

Exterior
- Panel fit & paint:
  - The 695 wears pronounced bumpers and scoops and, if it’s been modified with larger intercoolers or aftermarket parts, panel alignment/gap issues can appear — aftermarket intercooler vendors explicitly warn about bumpers not sitting correctly when a larger core is used [TMCM Motorsport intercooler note](https://www.tmcmotorsport.com/products/ade-custom-large-front-mounted-intercooler-kit-abarth-500-595-695).
  - Bright finishes and aggressive styling make chips, stone‑dings and faded clearcoat obvious; aftermarket owners’ guides call this out routinely [How to Deal with Common ABARTH Paint and Bodywork Issues](https://www.131.pl/uncategorized/how-to-deal-with-common-abarth-paint-and-bodywork-issues/).
- Trim vulnerabilities:
  - Sports seat bolsters and high‑wear interior trim are frequently reported as showing faster wear than mainstream small cars — expect scuffed gloss black bezels and worn seat bolsters on cars used hard or by tall drivers [IsItAGoodCar / owner reports](https://isitagoodcar.com/problems/abarth).
- Undercarriage & suspension:
  - Owners and aggregated MOT data frequently show accelerated suspension top‑mount and drop link wear on rough roads — the low ride height and sport suspension mean earlier replacement of top mounts, links and bushes compared with a standard Fiat 500 [CarCostCheck / MOT summary](https://www.carcostcheck.co.uk/common-problems/abarth).
- Body recalls and safety systems:
  - There’s no Australia‑specific, headline ADAS recall for the 695 in the search snippets, but always VIN‑check for outstanding campaigns [NHTSA recall lookup is the standard tool](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls). Given small‑car transplanting of Fiat parts, check for campaigns that might have affected Fiat 500 derivatives.

Interior
- Durability under heavy use:
  - Rideshare or taxi duty? Don’t. The 695’s sports seats, low ride height and performance setup aren’t built to be daily taxi cars; bolsters and trim will show quickly under heavy ingress/egress and repeated rear‑seat use [owner guides](https://what-car-should-i-buy.com/cars/abarth/695/).
- Entry/egress and ergonomics:
  - Door sills are low (good), but seat height and bolstering make ingress/egress awkward if you have dodgy knees. Door checks are short — expect a cramped swing angle typical of a 3.6 m city coupe [Vehicle sizes/dimensions reference](https://www.vehiclesizes.com/cars/abarth/695/).
- Electrical & infotainment:
  - Uconnect 5"/7" infotainment units used across the FCA/Fiat pool are known to freeze, go blank or become intermittently unresponsive — there are multiple forum threads and repair services dedicated to Uconnect display failures on 500/595/695 models [Uconnect 7" problems thread](https://www.fiatforum.com/threads/uconnect-7-display-becomes-unresponsive.453893/), and specialist repair pages list display replacement as a common fix [advancedrepair.co.uk unit repair listing](https://advancedrepair.co.uk/abarth-595-695-radio-sat-nav-media-unit-lcd-display-touch-screen-repair-4470-p.asp).
  - Owners also report central locking, dashboard light and general electrical niggles — not catastrophic but annoying and sometimes intermittent [HonestCarCheck common problems](https://honestcarcheck.co.uk/cars/abarth/695/common-problems/).
  - 12V battery drain: there are enough scattered forum notes about phantom drains after aftermarket stereo fits or modules failing to sleep. Always check recent jump starts or battery replacements during inspection; if the battery has been changed recently without a clear reason, find out why.
- Connectivity:
  - Bluetooth, voice recognition and sat‑nav reliability depend on the exact head unit fitted (Uconnect versions vary) — check which version you’ve got and confirm firmware updates were applied where possible (dealers can show log entries).

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation:
  - Abarth takes the Fiat 500 platform and heaps on performance hardware. That gives brilliant character but also concentrates wear into brakes, suspension and turbocharger systems. Independent Abarth specialists exist and are experienced, but mainstream dealer servicing can be patchy depending on technician experience [Abarth Workshop resource](https://www.abarthworkshop.co.uk/).
- Security & theft vulnerabilities:
  - Modern cars — even small Abarths — are not immune to “CAN injection” style theft techniques where attackers exploit weak segmentation and inject messages to simulate a valid key. This technique has been used across many makes and is a current, practical threat [The Register CAN injection analysis](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/), [technical writeup by Dr Ken Tindell](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/).
  - Factory immobilisers may not deter determined thieves; many owners and aftermarket security shops recommend OBD locks, encrypted immobilisers (e.g., Autowatch Ghost) and physical deterrents [TTW Installations Abarth security accessory list](https://ttw-installations.co.uk/fiat-abarth-accessories-security/), [MobileCarAlarm note on upgrades](https://mobilecaralarm.co.uk/fiat-abarth-security).
- Practical anti‑theft measures:
  - Fit an OBD lock, an aftermarket CAN‑aware immobiliser/Ghost-style system, and consider simple physical devices (wheel/steering lock) if you park on the street overnight. Ask any seller whether the car has had security upgrades — absence of upgrades may increase risk if you live in a high‑theft area.

Engines
Note: the Abarth 695 family uses variants of the 1.368 L turbo T‑Jet engine across the range. Each italicised subsection below covers a variant commonly seen in listings and the recurring failure points owners report in service logs and forums.

- *312A1 1.4 T‑Jet — XSR / 165 hp variants*
  - Failure points:
    - Early‑life electrical sensor gremlins (mass air, MAP sensor) reported intermittently causing rough idle or limp mode.
    - Turbocharger is susceptible to underboost symptoms if boost hoses crumble or the wastegate solenoid/actuator starts to fail.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Synthetic oil change every 10,000 km if used regularly in spirited driving; change interval can be relaxed toward 15,000 km if very low‑stress urban use and the correct oil spec is used — but for turbo health stick to 10,000 km for peace of mind.
    - Visual inspection of boost hoses and vacuum lines at every service (10k) and replacement of any brittle rubber immediately.

- *312A1 1.4 T‑Jet — Competizione / Rivale — ~180 hp (132 kW, 250 Nm)*
  - Failure points:
    - Turbo underboost (OBD code P0299) commonly blamed on failed wastegate actuators or solenoids; owners and specialist shops list these components as frequent failures on worn examples [P0299 turbo underboost discussion](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0299-fiat-500-abarth-2009-2019-1-4l-multiair).
    - Monza‑style exhaust valve can seize or the actuator on the exhaust note system can stick — owners recommend checking the valve operation and exhaust service history [IsItAGoodCar exhaust valve note](https://isitagoodcar.com/problems/abarth/595-695).
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil and filter: 10,000 km synthetic oil (turbo protection). If previous owner ran long intervals, budget for a turbo inspection and possibly an intake/intercooler clean.
    - Wastegate actuator/solenoid: inspect and test for free movement at 40–60k km; replace proactively if symptoms appear.
    - Intake and intercooler cleanliness: decoking and intercooler inspection every 40k km in spirited use.

- *Higher‑output limited editions / Biposto ~190 hp*
  - Failure points:
    - These extreme versions are track‑biased; expect accelerated wear on clutch, gearbox and brakes. Owners frequently fit uprated cooling and oiling mods when used hard.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Shorten service intervals for oil/filter to 7,500–10,000 km if the car sees track days. Monitor clutch wear and gearbox behaviour closely.

Transmission
- Manual (5‑speed) — what to check:
  - Clutch wear is accelerated by aggressive driving; confirm a smooth engagement and check for chatter or slipping under load.
  - Gear synchro damage is uncommon but possible in high‑km or heavily driven cars.
- Automated/robotised (Dualogic/AGS):
  - Known symptoms: delayed or hesitant shifts, jerky take‑offs, or a gearbox that falls back to limp mode. Owner threads and buyer guides report these robotised automated manuals are a convenience but can be fiddly and expensive to repair if the mechatronics or actuators fail [Fiat forum gearbox threads](https://www.fiatforum.com/threads/gear-box-issue-help.514569/), [What‑car buyer guide](https://what-car-should-i-buy.com/cars/abarth/695/).
  - Mechanical behaviour:
    - The system is effectively a manual gearbox with actuators. Solenoid/actuator wear, clutch actuator miscalibration and occasional hydraulic/TCM faults are the common failure modes.
  - Recommended checks:
    - Road test in varied conditions — light throttle, heavy throttle, low speed and highway — and confirm the gearbox behaves consistently. Watch for delayed downshifts on overtakes and slipping on launch.
    - Ask for any gearbox software updates or service actions in dealer history.
  - Repair notes:
    - Mechatronic or actuator repairs can be expensive if the unit needs replacement; some owners opt to retrofit a traditional manual or have specialist reconditioning performed.

Depreciation & marketplace context
- How the 695 holds value:
  - The 695 sits in a niche: it’s more collectible and characterful than a standard Fiat 500 but lacks the broad dealer network, warranty continuity and brand perception of a Mini. That can make depreciation patchy — limited editions and well‑maintained Competizione/Rivale cars can hold value better than run‑of‑the‑mill small hatchbacks, but mainstream small hot‑hatches (like recent Mini JCW) generally retain values more steadily [Evo commentary on desirability and end of production](https://www.evo.co.uk/abarth/500/207100/abarth-695-2012-2024-review-why-well-miss-italys-mini-cooper-s-rival), and depreciation calculators highlight that small sports cars’ resale depends heavily on mileage, edition and condition [TheMoneyCalculator depreciation tool](https://www.themoneycalculator.com/vehicle-finance/calculators/car-depreciation-by-make-and-model/ABARTH/695/).
- Practical metric:
  - Expect sharper percentage drops in the first 3 years than premium rivals if you buy a used example that’s already had one big drop in value. Limited editions can buck this trend — but buyer demand is smaller and prices fluctuate more than for mainstream rivals.

Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to think about it
- Fuel consumption baseline:
  - Manufacturer and technical sources list combined consumption for Competizione/Rivale variants in the vicinity of ~6.0 L/100 km (combined), with some editions showing 5.8–6.1 L/100 km in combined testing [Auto‑Data spec sheets](https://www.auto-data.net/en/abarth-695-generation-5001), [Abarth Final Edition buyer’s guide with consumption figures](https://www-aem-origin.fiat.co.nz/content/dam/cross-regional/asean/fiat/en_nz/brochure/2024/Abarth%20695%20Final%20Edition%20-%20November%202024_Web.pdf).
- Ownership math approach:
  - Fuel: Use the quoted ~6.0 L/100 km and your annual km (e.g., 15,000 km) to estimate litres used and multiply by local petrol price to find annual fuel cost.
  - Servicing: Expect specialist or dealer scheduled servicing every 10,000 km for oil & filters and inspections of boost system. Budget more than the mainstream hatch — sporty brakes, tyres and clutch replacements will arrive earlier.
  - Insurance & registration: Performance tag ups premiums — get quotes before you buy.
  - Repairs: Allocate a contingency fund for turbo‑related parts (wastegate solenoid/actuator), the robotised gearbox if equipped, and infotainment repairs on older Uconnect units.
- Hybrid/EV premium vs 695 petrol:
  - If you drive low to moderate annual kms (under 15,000 km) and keep the car for fun/weekend use, the petrol 695 will probably be cheaper to own than paying a big premium for a hybrid/EV. The 695’s decent combined consumption (~6.0 L/100 km) and small tank mean fuel is reasonable — the real cost drivers are maintenance (tyres, brakes, clutch if driven hard) and potential gearbox or turbo repairs.
  - For high annual kms (long commuter), a hybrid or EV could be cheaper long‑term if you value lower running costs and less frequent wear items — run the AFDC or a TCO calculator to compare with your actual fuel price and kilometres [AFDC cost calculator](https://afdc.energy.gov/calc/), [EV vs Hybrid vs Gas calculator](https://best-calculators.com/automotive/ev-vs-hybrid-vs-gas-calculator/).

Buying recommendation — yes/no and which variant?
- Short answer: Buy one if you want a characterful, compact, properly sporty small car and you accept the reality of a 695: niche appeal, firmer ride, higher running‑cost risk than a standard small hatch, and the need for careful pre‑purchase checks. Don’t buy one as a cheap transport bargain — they’re lifestyle purchases.
- When to buy:
  - Buy if:
    - You want the 695 for its driving character and you’re prepared to pay for specialist servicing when needed.
    - You find a low‑km, well‑maintained Competizione or Rivale with a complete service history and documented gearbox/engine care.
  - Walk away if:
    - The car is high‑km (>100k) with a sparse service history, an automatic that displays shift glitches, or lots of cosmetic damage and evidence of hard track use without receipts for upgrades.
- Optimal sweet spot:
  - Financially and mechanically, the sweet spot is a mid‑life Competizione/Rivale (2016–2019) with single ownership, full dealer or specialist service history, and manual transmission — these balance usable performance with lower risk of expensive robotised‑gearbox failures and offer better value than later limited final‑edition cars that carry a premium. The NSW listing you have (2019 auto, 74k km, $36,888) sits toward the premium end for a 695 with that km and automatic transmission — ask why the price is that level and whether the gearbox has been serviced or updated.
- Practical negotiation tips:
  - Demand full service history (look for 10,000 km oil changes and explicit checks/replacements for boost hoses and wastegate parts).
  - Get a specialist Abarth or Fiat technician to do a pre‑purchase inspection — pay the $200–$400; it’s worth it. Have them check boost pressure log, wastegate actuator play, and head‑unit firmware.
  - If the seller cannot demonstrate regular 10,000 km oil changes, factor in a turbo inspection and potential wastegate solenoid replacement into your offer.
