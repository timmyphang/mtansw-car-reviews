#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: Holden Ute (final modern coupe‑utility family built on Commodore platforms; modern VE/VF generation built 2007–2017). The VF-series Ute was produced June 2013 – Oct 2017; VE Ute range introduced 2007 (series codes VU → VY → VZ → VE → VF across the lineage). [Wikipedia VF Commodore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VF)), [Holden Ute overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Ute).
- Product hierarchy: the Ute is essentially the two‑door/one‑cab coupe‑utility variant of the Commodore family. In the Holden range it sat below full HSV Maloo performance utes (HSV took Commodore Ute bones and made them hot), and above the family sedan’s cargoless variants. HSV Maloo is the performance halo; base Omega / SV6 / SS / SS V split the buyer ladder in‑between. [Carsales VE Ute summary](https://www.carsales.com.au/editorial/details/holden-ve-utes-omega-sv6-ss-ss-v-5208/?isModal=true), [HSV Maloo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Special_Vehicles_Maloo).
- Shared platform: VE and VF Utes share the Commodore VE/VF global rear‑wheel‑drive platform (General Motors’ Holden platform). That platform underpins sedans, wagons and HSV derivatives — good news for parts availability of suspension, brakes and body panels. [Wikipedia Holden Ute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Ute), [MotorSpec VE platform notes](https://www.motorspec.net/model/holden/holden-ute).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive configurations (short rundown):
  - 3.6 L V6 variants: *LY7* / **LLT** (Alloytec / SIDI on later engines) — outputs ~195–210 kW on sporty tunes; rear‑wheel drive, 6‑speed manuals and 6‑speed automatics (6L80E on many V6/V8 auto cars). [vaber.au VE specs](https://www.vaber.au/commodore/ve/ve-specs.html), [UltimateSpecs SV6 details](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Holden/39118/Holden-Commodore-IV-(VE)-SV6-Ute.html).
  - 3.0 L V6 (less common in earlier Commodore/ute lineups) — longitudinal V6 in some VE variants. [vaber.au](https://www.vaber.au/commodore/ve/ve-specs.html).
  - 6.0 L V8 variants: **L98/L76/L77** (Gen‑IV / LS‑family V8s in older SS/SS V and HSV models) — RWD, manual or 6‑speed auto. [vaber.au VE specs](https://www.vaber.au/commodore/ve/ve-specs.html), [Australian Holden LS guide](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/08/common-ls-engine-problems/).
- Marketplace rivals: this is a niche car/ute heritage item rather than a mainstream dual‑cab 4x4: direct buyers now compare it to lifestyle and performance utes (older Ford Falcon ute, HSV Maloo on the performance side) and modern dual‑cab competition for practical buyers — Toyota HiLux, Ford Ranger, Isuzu D‑Max, Mitsubishi Triton, Nissan Navara and Holden Colorado (and lifestyle newcomers like the Maverick). For mainstream comparisons see Drive and CarsGuide overviews. [Drive ute comparison](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/ute-comparison-ford-ranger-v-holden-colorado-v-isuzu-d-mAX-v-mazda-bt-50-v-mitsubishi-triton-v-nissan-navara-v-toyota-hilux-v-volkswagen-amarok-2/), [CarsGuide 4x4 dual‑cab guide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/car-reviews/hilux-vs-colorado-vs-ranger-vs-navara-v-d-max-v-triton-76235).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden Ute?
This is the meat-and-4x4 of the guide. I’ll be blunt: if you buy a Commodore‑based Ute as a daily hauler or weekend hero, you need to know where the rust and electronics gremlins live, and what parts will bite your wallet.

- Exterior
  - Panel fit and alignment: earlier series and older repairs can show panel gaps and shim‑needed guards — owners often fix by manual shimming (see DIY threads). Expect odd gaps on repaired or coastal cars. [YouTube shim video example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hyoswXlw-s4).
  - Paint durability / flaking: owner threads report paint flaking/adhesion problems on some Commodore/ute roofs and panels — not widespread factory recall level but a real annoyance on higher‑mileage, sun‑baked cars. If you see bubbling or flakes around sills/arches, budget a respray. [Just Commodores paint thread](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/paint-flaking-off.295675/).
  - Rust hotspots: sills, wheel arches, door bottoms and pinch welds are classic Holden rust spots — check for bubbling under inner guards and inside tailgate seams especially on coastal cars or those that have been used off‑road. [Early Holden rust problems overview](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/16/early-holden-rust-problems-common-trouble-spots-warning-signs-and-how-to-fix-them/), [AutoGuru rust repair guide](https://www.autoguru.com.au/repairs/rust-repair/holden/ute).
  - Trim vulnerabilities: gloss black plastics and lower bumper trim on SV6/SS kits scratch easily; sports kits on SV6/SS models can hide underside damage from loads and utes used as tradie vehicles often have scuffs and tray dents.
  - ADAS / safety system niggles: the Commodore/VF family included AEB and lane‑assist tech on later models; these systems use camera and radar modules and are sensible but can be disabled by sensor faults, wiring issues or obstructed sensors. There was at least one GM software field action affecting braking EBCM units — see recall/field action REC‑005475 (EBCM software update). Later firmware updates helped some behaviour, but radar/camera faults still show up as "AEB unavailable" and often require calibration or sensor replacement. [ANCAP safety report on Commodore driver assist](https://www.ancap.com.au/safety-ratings/holden/commodore/21bc54), [GM recall REC‑005475](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-005475).
  - Practical check-list at inspection:
    - Look behind wheelarch liners for bubbling/filler.
    - Check tailgate and tray seams for rust and previous repairs.
    - Verify ADAS lights on dash clear with a scan — "AEB unavailable" on a used car often means a sensor needs calibration.

- Interior
  - Durability under hard use: Commodore/Ute cabins (VE/VF) are solidly built but wear quickly under tradie/ride‑share duty. Expect driver‑side bolster wear, sagging seat foam in high‑kilometre examples, and scratched load‑through trim on the tray bulkhead.
  - Entry/exit ergonomics: generous door opening and lower sill compared with modern high‑riding dual‑cabs — easier to get in/out for taller crew but check the door checks (old catches get stiff).
  - Electrical quirks and infotainment:
    - MyLink infotainment is common across VF models (7‑inch touchscreen). The platform ages badly: owners report random reboots, frozen displays, Bluetooth dropouts and complete black screens — repair specialists list common MyLink failures. Replacements/repairs are available but can cost several hundred dollars. [Australian Holden MyLink problems](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/07/vf-commodore-mylink-problems/), [MyLink repair services](https://getelectronic.net/products/holden-lcd-touch-screen-mylink).
    - 12V battery drain: forum threads show random MyLink resets and battery drain concerns tied to infotainment/ECU behaviour; look for aftermarket alarm installations that have been butchered — these are a common 12V parasitic drain source.
    - Screen sizes and trims: most VE/VF utes have 7" MyLink on mid/high trims; base Omegas may have smaller units or basic radios. Confirm the unit matches the trim pack and that the reverse camera (if fitted) works.
  - Practical check-list at inspection:
    - Test Bluetooth pairing, Apple CarPlay/phone audio, camera feed and check for sudden reboots.
    - Check windows/locks, sunroof (if fitted) and interior lighting for intermittent faults.

- Mechanically
  - Brand engineering reputation: Holden Commodore hardware (VE/VF) is fundamentally robust — rear‑wheel drivetrain, strong chassis. But reputation is a mixed bag because of specific engine and transmission issues and increasing theft attacks on Commodores in recent years.
  - Security vulnerabilities: Commodores/modern Holdens have been targeted heavily by sophisticated thieves using OBD/key‑programming devices and CAN‑bus message injection hacks. Police and news reports show a surge in thefts using programming devices; experts warn push‑button and CAN accessible cars are at risk. Mitigate with physical OBD locks, steering wheel locks or aftermarket immobilisers and parking in secure locations. [ABC story on key programming theft devices](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2025-02-27/victoria-car-thefts-surge-electronic-programming-devices/104987680), [CarExpert on Commodore theft crisis](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/holden-parent-aware-of-commodore-theft-crisis-solution-no-closer), [RACV OBD lock advice](https://www.racv.com.au/royalauto/transport/car-safety-security/prevent-push-start-car-theft-obd-lock.html), [CAN Injection tech detail](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/).
  - Aftermarket anti‑theft measures I recommend:
    - Install an OBD‑port lock or case.
    - Add a mechanical steering wheel lock as a visible deterrent.
    - Consider a dedicated immobiliser with a hidden switch or relay‑kill.
    - VIN etch and alarm system with verified installers.

- Engines
  - I’ll give each engine/variant its own italic subsection and list the specific failure points and recommended service intervals. Sources: technical summaries and owner forums.

  *LY7 (early 3.6L V6)*
  - Notes: non‑direct injected 3.6 V6 seen in VE variants (early Alloytec family). Reported problems: timing chain tensioners wearing, hydraulic lifter noise if oil maintenance neglected. Keep oil fresh and at the correct spec. [vaber.au VE specs](https://www.vaber.au/commodore/ve/ve-specs.html), [Alloytec faults explainer](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/19/common-alloytec-faults-explained/).
  - Typical preventative maintenance:
    - Oil/service: 10,000 km or 12 months minimum (10,000 km recommended for hard use).
    - Timing chain/inspection: monitor oil pressure and noise; replace guides/tensioners if lifter noise or oil pressure dips.

  *LLT (3.6L Alloytec / SIDI variants)*
  - Notes: later LLT/SIDI tunes used in SV6/SS variants; more power but higher sensitivity to EGR/PCV sludge with short‑trip duty. Owners report oil sludge and timing chain stretch if oil changes are spaced out. [UltimateSpecs / vaber spec pages](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Holden/39118/Holden-Commodore-IV-(VE)-SV6-Ute.html), [Alloytec faults](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/19/common-alloytec-faults-explained/).
  - Failure points:
    - Timing chain guides/tensioner wear.
    - PCV valve blockages leading to oil contamination/sludge.
    - High‑pressure fuel pump issues on SIDI systems if fuel filter is neglected (less common than diesel HPFP issues).
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil and filter every 10,000 km (15,000 km acceptable on gentle use, but I’d stick to 10k on high‑stress or high‑km cars).
    - Change engine breather/PCV service parts at 80–120k km if symptoms arise.
    - Replace fuel filter as per book; inspect HPFP if misfires/pressure issues appear.

  *LF1 (3.0L V6)*
  - Notes: less common, lower output — simpler, but lower torque. Issues generally are age‑related (cooling hoses, thermostat housings). [vaber.au VE specs](https://www.vaber.au/commodore/ve/ve-specs.html).
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Cooling system checks every 30k km; replace plastic thermostat housings before they crack.

  *L98 / L76 / L77 (6.0L Gen‑IV / LS‑family V8s)*
  - Notes: LS‑family V8s are generally durable but age and mileage introduce oil leaks, valve‑cover gasket seepage, PCV and ignition coil issues. LS owners praise longevity but warn of oiling quirks when mods are fitted. [Australian Holden LS notes](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/08/common-ls-engine-problems/).
  - Failure points:
    - Valve cover gasket and rear main seal leaks.
    - Oiling issues if aftermarket cams or poor maintenance; lifter noise if neglected.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil & filter every 10,000 km (sooner if towing or high RPM use).
    - Tune‑up every 100k km with spark plug and coil inspection.
    - Cooling system service every 30k–60k km.

  *Alloytec (collective name for Holden V6 family)*
  - Notes: "Alloytec" engines have mixed reputations — capable but sensitive to oil maintenance and PCV/PCV‑related sludge. Owners on forums report timing‑chain stretch and oil‑pressure symptoms if neglected. [Alloytec faults explained](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/19/common-alloytec-faults-explained/).
  - Preventative maintenance: same as above; be religious about oil and breather services.

- Transmission
  - Common autos across VE/VF utes include the **6L80E** 6‑speed automatic (used widely in V6 and V8 Commodores). Manuals were 6‑speed on sportier models.
  - Known failure modes:
    - Torque converter shudder, slipping or loss of higher gear engagement reported in some 6L80E cars — long service intervals, overheating and neglected fluid are common culprits. GM had technical service bulletins addressing 6L80 behaviour on high‑power cars. [6L80E common issues guide](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/08/6l80e-common-issues-guide/), [BrisbaneTuning 6L80 problems](https://www.brisbanetuning.com.au/6l80-transmission-problems).
    - Shift solenoid and valve body failures on high‑km cars.
    - Expensive torque converter replacement in some failure cases; some owners reported extended warranties/field actions on other GM autos for torque converter batches.
  - Behaviour notes:
    - A healthy 6L80E is smooth and unobtrusive; when it starts to fail you’ll notice shuddering at low speeds, delayed engagement or missing 4th/5th/6th gear under load.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Fluid and filter changes every 60–80k km for a used vehicle (shorter if towing or hot climates).
    - If you buy a high‑km car with original fluid, budget a fluid+filter service immediately.

- Depreciation metrics
  - Hard fact: Utes that sell well new (HiLux, Ranger) hold value noticeably better than discontinued or niche vehicles. Comparative market reports show Toyota HiLux, Ford Ranger and Isuzu D‑Max as the resale kings; Holden/Commodore derivatives lose share in that retention. [Cars24 trade‑in values](https://www.cars24.com.au/car-guide/10-used-utes-australia-that-still-deliver-strong-trade-in-value/), [CarsGuide resale analysis](https://www.carsguide.com.au/car-news/resale-kings-the-surprising-utes-that-hold-their-value-best-exclusive-report-reveals-the).
  - Practical metric (how to interpret the available data): the snapshot median of ~$14.5k for 2011–2017 Utes versus comparable HiLux/Ranger asks for similar years/condition (commonly tens of thousands) demonstrates the Holden Ute typically trades at a steeper discount vs mainstream 4x4 dual‑cabs. Use RedBook to quote specific historical residuals per VIN/trim for an exact percentage: [RedBook valuation tool](https://www.redbook.com.au/valuation-report/).
  - Bottom line: expect steeper depreciation than the segment leaders — plan to keep the car long term or buy it for enjoyment/value rather than as an investment.

- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy and hybrid/alternative powertrain payback
  - Fuel consumption baseline: V6 mid‑range Holden Ute real world ~11–12 L/100 km; V8s nearer 14–15 L/100 km. [Auto‑Data / Auto‑Data.net fuel spec ~11.4 L/100 km for V6 SV6](https://www.auto-data.net/en/holden-ute-ii-3.6-v6-265hp-sv6-11759), [Analyticauto consumption comps](https://analyticauto.com/all/holden/ute/fuel), [Fuelly owner logs](https://www.fuelly.com/car/Holden/UTE/all).
  - Example TCO calculation method (do this at home; don’t trust the dealer to do it for you):
    - Inputs: annual km (I use 15,000 km as standard), fuel price (example $1.80/L — replace with current local price), average consumption (11.5 L/100 km for V6 Ute).
    - Annual fuel = (15,000 / 100) * 11.5 = 1,725 L → cost = 1,725 * $1.80 = $3,105/year.
    - Compare to a modern diesel dual‑cab with ~8 L/100 km: annual fuel = 1,200 L → $2,160 at $1.80/L → saving ~$945/year.
    - If a diesel/newer truck costs a $10k premium, payback is ~10.6 years — not always worth it. Use the hybrid/price premium break‑even calculators like Drive/Best‑Calculators and FuelEconomy as a guide. [Drive on hybrids](https://www.drive.com.au/caradvice/are-hybrids-worth-the-extra-money-heres-the-truth/), [Hybrid break‑even calculator](https://best-calculators.com/automotive/hybrid-break-even-calculator/), [FuelEconomy comparison tool](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/hybridCompare.jsp).
  - Takeaway: Holden utes offer honest V6/V8 machinery; they are thirsty. Paying a premium for a modern diesel dual‑cab often only makes sense if you do high km or heavy towing. Hybrids aren’t relevant to the Commodore Ute — the number crunching rarely favours a hybrid premium unless city driving and very high local fuel prices.

- Final buying recommendation (plain talk)
  - Who this car is for:
    - You love the Aussie coupe‑ute history, want rear‑wheel‑drive fun or a cheap sporty tray truck for weekend towing and you don’t need factory new 4x4 capability.
    - You’re ok with a higher chance of electronics niggles, rust inspection needs and the theft risk — and you’ll invest in simple anti‑theft hardware.
  - Who should walk away:
    - You need a bulletproof workhorse for daily towing, coastal tradie use, or you want top retained value. Buy a Hilux/Ranger/D‑Max instead.
  - Best value sweet spot:
    - Look for a late VE or early VF Ute (approx 2011–2015) with a full service history, recent transmission fluid service, and importantly, evidence that the MyLink unit, battery and ADAS warnings have been resolved. The SV6 or SS with a documented service history is the sweet spot — sportier, better spec, but avoid modified/tuned V8s with unknown history.
    - Given the NSW dealer snapshot, target cars priced near the lower‑end of the local range (sub‑$15k) but only if you can confirm no major timing, transmission or rust work is looming.
  - Walk‑away checklist at purchase:
    - Full service history to confirm oil changes every ~10k km for Alloytec engines.
    - Transmission service evidence (fluid/filter) for cars with 6L80E.
    - No active ADAS faults on dash; recall/field actions completed (check VIN via [Vehicle Recalls Australia](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/)).
    - Physical inspection for rust at sills, wheelarch lips and tailgate seams.
    - MyLink screen works, Bluetooth pairs, reverse camera functions.
    - Ask if owner installed aftermarket alarms/OBD‑port devices — if so, inspect wiring.
