#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation & codes: the Eclipse Cross in Australia is the compact crossover produced from 2017–present (first generation launched 2017; major mid‑cycle updates and a 2024/2025 refresh lead into the current 2025/2026 lineup). Newer models (2024–26) consolidate around Mitsubishi’s 1.5‑litre turbo petrol and CVT layout; earlier years also saw 2.0‑litre petrol, diesel and PHEV variants in some markets. (See Mitsubishi and Wikipedia summaries) [Mitsubishi specs](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/eclipse-cross/specs), [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Eclipse_Cross).
- Product hierarchy: the Eclipse Cross sits under the larger Outlander in Mitsubishi’s Australian line-up — it’s the brand’s compact/city‑sized SUV, positioned between small crossovers and family‑sized SUVs (Outlander above it). [Mitsubishi model page](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/eclipse-cross).
- Platform & shared architecture: early Eclipse Cross models used Mitsubishi’s GS / compact SUV underpinnings; later global variants and refreshed models keep a shared compact‑SUV architecture, which helps parts availability across Mitsubishi compact models. (General platform and model lineage summary) [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Eclipse_Cross).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions & drivetrains (Australia‑relevant):
  - 1.5‑litre direct‑injection turbo petrol — the modern default (listed ~152 hp / 184 lb‑ft in US specs) mated to a CVT with an 8‑speed Sport simulation mode; many trims offer AWD via Mitsubishi’s S‑AWC system, with FWD on base/lower trims in some markets. [Mitsubishi specs](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/eclipse-cross/specs), [Edmunds](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/eclipse-cross/2026/features-specs/).
  - 2.0‑litre naturally aspirated four (earlier/lower market trims in some regions).
  - 2.2‑litre diesel (in some overseas markets; watch service history and DPF care).
  - Plug‑in Hybrid (PHEV) variant with electric range ~45–55 km and combined low L/100km in blended usage — real world numbers vary. [Driven NZ PHEV review](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/reviews/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-phev-vrx-review-global-efficiency/), [Spritmonitor PHEV real‑world](https://www.spritmonitor.de/en/detail/1587894.html).
  - Transmission: predominantly CVT across the modern line; older manual options were rare. AWD available on many newer trims [Edmunds](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/eclipse-cross/2026/features-specs/).
- Primary rivals (what you’ll also test-drive): Hyundai Kona / Tucson, Kia Seltos, Volkswagen Taos, Nissan Qashqai/Rogue, Jeep Compass — the usual compact crossover crop. [TheCarConnection competitor list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/mitsubishi_eclipse-sport-cross_choices).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mitsubishi Eclipse Cross?

Exterior
- Panel fit & trim:
  - Owners commonly report trim and moulding issues — adhesive-backed mouldings and exterior trim can peel, crack or detach if previously exposed to heat or poor repair/fitment. OEM parts and clip‑type fixings are available from Mitsubishi parts pages. Expect to replace clips/mouldings on older cars or cars used in hot coastal climates. [Mitsubishi parts](https://parts.mitsubishicars.com/part-categories/exterior/mouldings-and-trim/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross), [CleanVins recall aggregator](https://www.cleanvins.com/recalls/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross).
  - A‑pillar and console trim rattles are a common complaint on older examples — relatively cheap fixes but annoying. [Go‑Parts trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/body-a-pillar-trim-panel-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2025).
- Paint & body:
  - No wide international epidemic of paint flaking, but local owner reports show scuffs on gloss black trim and bumper corners. Check for poor respray work — Eclipse Crosses sold as fleet/rideshare often have scrapes.
- ADAS and exterior sensors:
  - Radar sensors for Forward Collision Mitigation (FCM) and Adaptive Cruise (ACC) can produce fault codes — P2583 is the common front radar fault (blocked sensor, misalignment or damage). Cleaning helps but calibration after impact or bumper removal is often required; until fixed, ACC/FCM may be disabled. [Go‑Parts P2583 explanation](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p2583-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2024).
  - Rear blind‑spot radar warnings (“BSW Service Required”) are a recurring message; Mitsubishi published a TSB (TSB‑23‑54‑001) that in some cases is solvable with a software update rather than module replacement. If you see BSW warnings, ask the dealer for the TSB check. [TSB details](https://tsbsearch.com/Mitsubishi/TSB-23-54-001).
  - Recalls and owner complaints show occasional exterior moulding detachment while driving — VIN recall checks are a must. NHTSA pages let you search VIN‑specific recalls. [NHTSA Eclipse Cross page](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle/2025/Mitsubishi/Eclipse%20Cross/).

Interior
- Cabin durability & rideshare duty:
  - Interior plastics and trim will show wear under hard use. Expect scuffs on door sills, worn seat bolsters on cloth/soft leather in high‑km cars. Forum threads frequently call out centre console and door panel rattles. [Go‑Parts interior guides](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/console-trim-panel-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2026), [Eclipse Cross forum](https://www.eclipsecross.org/).
  - Entry/exit: standard compact‑SUV door sills — not high step‑in like an off‑roader but check rear legroom if you plan shuttle runs.
- Infotainment & electrical:
  - Screen hardware failures are a well‑documented headache: black screens, ghost touches, delamination and unresponsive touch inputs commonly occur on 2018–2023 units. DIY videos and replacement guides are widespread — this is a known weak point; replacement screens or module swaps are possible but can be expensive. [Go‑Parts audio/infotainment guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/audio-system-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2023), [YouTube repair example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=haLVwtHOxpE).
  - Bluetooth and smartphone connectivity bugs — frequent software resets reported after ~20,000 km in owner reports. [Autodoc problems summary](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross).
  - 12‑volt battery drain: sporadic reports of parasitic draws, particularly where multiple aftermarket accessories or poor software updates were fitted. Check for aftermarket alarms and test for parasitic drain during pre‑purchase inspection.
- Practical check list for buyers:
  - Power up infotainment, test Apple CarPlay/Android Auto across multiple phones.
  - Watch for flicker/blackout on startup and ghost touches.
  - Confirm all ADAS warning lights go away after ignition — persistent warnings mean dealer diagnostics and possible radar module work.

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation:
  - Mitsubishi builds rugged basic mechanicals but the Eclipse Cross mixes newer forced‑induction engines and CVTs — and that cocktail has attracted a fair slice of owner complaints.
- Security and theft risks:
  - Modern Eclipse Crosses with keyless entry are vulnerable to sophisticated CAN‑bus/CAN‑injection attacks that bypass immobilisers if attackers have physical access to wiring harness entry points (e.g., headlight harness). This is not Mitsubishi‑only; it’s an industry problem. Practical aftermarket steps reduce risk. [Canis/technical writeup](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/), [The Drive coverage](https://www.thedrive.com/news/shadetree-hackers-are-stealing-cars-by-injecting-code-into-headlight-wiring).
  - Recommendations: fit a visible mechanical deterrent (Panther/Club), an OBD port lock/cover, and consider an immobiliser relay or aftermarket tracking device with movement alerts. Park under lights and submerge the VIN in your headlight/engine bay inspection for tamper signs.
- High‑priority physical/digital anti‑theft countermeasures (quick list):
  - Heavy duty steering wheel lock.
  - OBD port blocker and VIN‑tagged security screws on headlight access.
  - Alarm with tilt/impact + GPS tracker with real‑time alerts.
  - Disable keyless trunk/entry if you can via dealer configuration (ask them).

Engines
- *1.5L Turbo — bolded engine code where referenced **4B40***:
  - Known weak points:
    - Turbocharger wear and failure symptoms have been reported on early high‑km examples: lag, oily smoke on spool, and loss of boost. Owners & repair guides track turbo issues on the 1.5T Eclipse Cross. [Go‑Parts turbo guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/turbocharger-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2024).
    - Timing/cam‑crank correlation codes (P0017) have been seen in the 4B40; Mitsubishi issued Technical Information Notices noting software/ECM interplay can cause timing flags even when the mechanical timing is OK — but mechanical causes must be checked. [Go‑Parts P0017/TIN reference](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0017-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2023-4b40-1-5l).
    - Direct injection carbon buildup — as with many DI turbo engines, carbon and intake‑valve deposits reduce performance over time.
  - Preventative maintenance (recommendations):
    - Oil & filter: every 10,000 km with high‑quality synthetic oil (10W‑30 / 5W‑30 grade per Mitsubishi spec). Short trips/stop‑start usage: consider 7,500 km intervals.
    - Spark plugs: inspect/replace ~60,000 km.
    - Turbo intake & intercooler: visual check and clean every 40,000 km; watch for oil in intake.
    - Fuel system and injectors: service/cleaning at 40–60,000 km where diesel‑grade detergents aren’t appropriate (use petrol injector service).
    - Software updates: check dealer records for ECM/TCU updates (Timing code TIN 20‑13 noted). [Go‑Parts P0017](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0017-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-2018-2023-4b40-1-5l).
- *2.0L naturally aspirated*:
  - Fewer turbo‑specific issues but lower power; simple, less stressed design tends to be mechanically durable. Oil changes as above (10,000 km intervals).
  - Check for high‑mileage head gasket or intake sensor issues in older examples — typical age‑related wear, nothing systemic in searches.
- *2.2L diesel*:
  - Diesel variants reported DPF and turbo issues around moderate mileage (some owners see problems ~40,000 km). Check DPF regeneration history and EGR cleanliness. [Autodoc diesel notes](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-eclipse-cross).
- *PHEV (Plug‑in Hybrid)*:
  - Battery & charging: PHEV uses a battery pack (~13.8 kWh in some markets) and motors; battery health should be checked with a dealer log. Real world blended fuel consumption varies dramatically — owner logs show around 5.5 L/100km blended if you don’t predominantly run on short electric trips. If you can charge regularly and do many short trips, the PHEV can be frugal (~1–2 L/100km claimed in ideal conditions). [Driven PHEV review](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/reviews/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-phev-vrx-review-global-efficiency/), [Spritmonitor real world](https://www.spritmonitor.de/en/detail/1587894.html).
  - Maintenance: follow Mitsubishi dealer battery inspection schedule; electric drive components generally require less routine maintenance but cooling systems and high‑voltage cabling must be inspected at service intervals.
  - Beware: PHEV complexity raises potential long‑term repair costs if battery or inverter work is needed; confirm remaining warranty coverage and get battery state‑of‑health checked pre‑purchase.

Transmission
- CVT behaviour and known failure modes:
  - The Eclipse Cross is commonly fitted with a CVT and many owners have logged symptoms: shuddering, surging, hesitation, whining, and in some reports delayed engagement — complaints significant enough to spawn a broader conversation and lawsuits over Mitsubishi CVTs in the past. These symptoms may arise by ~30,000–60,000 km in some owner reports but can vary by usage and maintenance. [CarComplaints CVT class action summary](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2021/mitsubishi-cvt-problems-class-action-lawsuit.shtml), [EngineCrux CVT summary](https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-cvt-problems-facts-and-feedback/).
  - Leakage and overheating of CVT fluid can accelerate wear. CVT fluid and filter service is not optional if you want longevity — many technicians recommend CVT fluid changes at 60,000 km intervals or earlier for hard urban use (towing, hot climates) — see CVT service guidance. [CVTMechanic advice](https://cvtmechanic.com/mitsubishi/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-cvt-problems-costs-options/).
- Practical notes:
  - If you feel shudder or delayed engagement on test drive, walk away unless the owner can show a recent CVT service and you can negotiate price for potential rework.
  - Transmission replacement or rebuilds are costly; a pre‑purchase transmission diagnostic plus a warm‑fluid check is worth the $150–300.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mitsubishi Eclipse Cross?

- NSW dealer snapshot (live MTA NSW scrape):
  - Active listings: 15
  - Price range: AUD $17,690 – $44,990
  - Median price: AUD $28,690
  - Year range: 2017 – 2024
  - Median odometer: 29,817 km
  - Transmission mix: Automatic: 15
  - Use this as your negotiating grid — the market is shallow, so you’ll either find low‑km nearly new cars near the top of the range or older high‑km bargains at the bottom.
- Depreciation & value:
  - Mitsubishi positions the Eclipse Cross at the value end of the compact crossover segment — it starts cheaper than some rivals and tends to come down in price faster than Japanese segment leaders with stronger resale (think Toyota/Kia). Edmunds and other reviews note its low cost of entry as a feature; that also translates to a softer resale profile. [Edmunds positioning](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/eclipse-cross/).
  - Practical takeaway: you can buy newer kit for less money than rivals, but expect resale values to be on the softer side. That’s not a flaw if you’re buying to keep 5+ years; it’s a problem if you plan to flip in 2–3 years.
- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy:
  - Fuel consumption to expect:
    - 1.5T petrol: manufacturer combined figures translate roughly to mid‑7s L/100km in real Australian driving — CarsGuide lists petrol versions around ~7.3 L/100km in aggregate reporting. [CarsGuide fuel page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/eclipse-cross/fuel-consumption).
    - PHEV: Mitsubishi/claims show very low liters per 100km when used as a city shuttle, but real‑world blended figures are higher — Driven and Spritmonitor show blended results of ~2–5.5 L/100km depending on charging behaviour; don’t assume the claimed 1.9 L/100km unless you charge every day and drive mostly short trips. [Driven PHEV review](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/reviews/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-phev-vrx-review-global-efficiency/), [Spritmonitor](https://www.spritmonitor.de/en/detail/1587894.html).
  - Maintenance & risk premiums:
    - Budget for CVT maintenance and possible early servicing (CVT fluid every ~60k km or sooner). Expect potentially higher repair bills for turbo and CVT components versus a naturally aspirated FWD rival.
    - PHEV owners need to factor in potential battery or inverter repairs outside warranty — but if you drive mostly short commutes and charge frequently, the PHEV’s lower fuel bill and city efficiency can offset higher purchase premiums.
  - Use tools like Edmunds True Cost to Own methodology or the US DOE cost calculators for an apples‑to‑apples multi‑year TCO model; in Australia adjust fuel costs and insurance accordingly. [Edmunds TCO](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/eclipse-cross/2025/cost-to-own/), [AFDC calc](https://afdc.energy.gov/calc/).
- Is the premium for a PHEV worth it?
  - Short answer: yes, but only if the math fits your life. If you:
    - Can charge at home/work reliably, and
    - Do many short urban trips where the EV range covers the trip,
    - Then the PHEV’s blended fuel consumption often realises big savings — Driven and user logs show compelling numbers in the right use case. [Driven PHEV review](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/reviews/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-phev-vrx-review-global-efficiency/), [Spritmonitor real‑world](https://www.spritmonitor.de/en/detail/1587894.html).
  - If you do lots of long highway trips, the PHEV gains evaporate and the standard 1.5T petrol becomes more economical overall.
- Buying recommendation (clear and blunt):
  - If you want low purchase price and a tidy urban vehicle, buy a mid‑life petrol Eclipse Cross with a proven service history and recent CVT fluid change. In the NSW market snapshot the median price of AUD $28,690 with ~30,000 km is the realistic mid‑market: aim for a 2019–2021 example in that price band if you want modern equipment without the new‑car premium.
  - If you can charge frequently and want the green headlines plus real fuel savings, a PHEV with verified battery health and service history is a good buy — but expect to pay the premium and verify warranty transfer and state‑of‑health before you hand over cash. [Spritmonitor + Driven reviews](https://www.spritmonitor.de/en/detail/1587894.html), https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/reviews/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-phev-vrx-review-global-efficiency/.
  - Avoid buying any Eclipse Cross that:
    - Exhibits CVT shudder or hesitation on test drive (unless priced for a rebuild),
    - Has unresolved ADAS warnings (BSW, FCM, ACC messages) without dealer TSB remediation,
    - Shows infotainment blackouts or ghost touches without repair records — these are a time‑sink.
- Specific sweet spot trim/year:
  - Best financial sweet spot: late‑model (2019–2021) mid‑trim (SE/SEL equivalent) with the 1.5T, AWD optional, and a full service history showing CVT fluid service in the last 60,000 km. On the NSW market snapshot this aligns with the median price band (AUD $25k–$32k) and will give you modern kit with reduced risk of early CVT or turbo bills. Use the NSW median price AUD $28,690 as your negotiation pivot.
  - If you want cutting fuel bills and can charge, the PHEV VRX is the one to search for — but budget for diagnostics and likely slightly higher asking prices at dealerships. [Driven PHEV review](https://www.drivencarguide.co.nz/reviews/mitsubishi-eclipse-cross-phev-vrx-review-global-efficiency/).

Quick pre‑purchase checklist (essentials)
- Verify full service history — look for CVT fluid/service within 60,000 km and software/TSB fixes for ADAS.
- VIN recall & TSB check via Mitsubishi owners site or NHTSA VIN lookup. [Mitsubishi open recalls](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls), [NHTSA Eclipse Cross](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle/2025/Mitsubishi/Eclipse%20Cross/).
- Test‑drive cold and hot: listen for CVT whine, shudder, turbo lag or smoke on spool.
- Infotainment: run long enough to see if screen glitches appear.
- ADAS: verify ACC/FCM and BSW work; persistent warnings mean dealership calibration.
- PHEV: demand a battery health report and charging/discharging logs where available.

Final verdict (short, no BS)
- The Eclipse Cross is a smart buy if you prioritise value, AWD availability and compact‑SUV practicality — but don’t buy blind. Expect quirks: infotainment gremlins, CVT sensitivities and turbo/service needs on the 1.5T. If you’re mechanically pragmatic (or can afford dealer servicing) you’ll get a decent, affordable crossover for Aussie roads. If you demand bulletproof resale and the lowest long‑term hassle, a mainstream rival with a simpler drivetrain (non‑CVT, non‑turbo) may suit you better.
- Best pick for balance: a 2019–2021 1.5T SE/SEL with documented CVT service and a clean ADAS/infotainment history — or a verified PHEV if your charging and commute patterns make its arithmetic sing.
