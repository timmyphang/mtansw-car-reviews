#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation / production timeframe / internal codes  
  The iMax sold in Australia is the second‑generation TQ‑series passenger van derived from Hyundai’s Starex/H‑1 family — commonly referenced as the TQ‑W iMax. It was sold here across the 2008–2021 era with incremental Series updates (Series II mid‑life upgrades appeared in later years) and examples still traded as late as 2020–2021 in dealer yards ([Hyundai specs PDF](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/models/imax/docs/Hyundai_iMax_Specifications_Sheet.pdf), [AustraliaCar.Reviews](https://australiancar.reviews/review-hyundai-tq-w-imax-2008/)).

- Product hierarchy (what sits above / below)  
  The iMax sits as Hyundai’s large people‑mover offering — below premium SUVs and MPVs (e.g., luxury imports) but above compact MPVs. It competes head‑to‑head with mainstream big people‑movers like the Kia Carnival and Toyota Granvia in buyer consideration sets ([CarsGuide comparison](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/vs/kia-carnival), [CarExpert Granvia vs iMax](https://www.carexpert.com.au/hyundai/imax/2025)).

- Shared global platform architecture & models sharing it  
  The iMax is the passenger‑focused variant of the Starex/H‑1/iLoad family — same bones as the iLoad commercial van and H‑1/i800 in other markets. That parts commonality helps availability for mechanical spares and panels across Hyundai’s light‑commercial parts network ([Wikipedia Starex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Starex)).

- Market‑specific engine, outputs, transmissions & drive layouts (Aus)  
  - Petrol: **G4KG** 2.4‑litre 4‑cylinder (approx. 127 kW / 224 Nm) — paired with a 5‑speed automatic or 5‑speed manual in older specs depending on year/market ([TotalEngines](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Hyundai/iMax), [carsales specs](https://www.carsales.com.au/research/hyundai/imax/2020/active/)).  
  - Diesel: **D4CB** 2.5‑litre turbo‑diesel CRDi — outputs differ by tune and year; Aussie listings commonly show the diesel paired to a five‑speed automatic (diesel auto quoted around 125 kW / 441 Nm in some specs) or six‑speed manual depending on variant ([Hyundai spec sheet](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/models/imax/docs/Hyundai_iMax_Specifications_Sheet.pdf), [carsales](https://www.carsales.com.au/research/hyundai/imax/2020/active/), [Auto Data on D4CB](https://auto-data.net/en/hyundai-h-1-i-starex-2-5-crdi-140hp-13854)).  
  - Drive layout: Rear‑wheel drive (RWD) across the range — the commercial roots make the driveline robust but less car‑like in dynamics ([AustraliaCar.Reviews](https://australiancar.reviews/review-hyundai-tq-w-imax-2008/)).  
  - Competitors: Kia Carnival, Toyota Granvia, Volkswagen Multivan/Transporter (people‑mover specs), plus newer MPVs and large SUVs buyers might consider ([CarsGuide comparisons](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/vs/kia-carnival), [CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/hyundai/imax/2025)).

- Explicit marketplace rivals  
  Kia Carnival, Toyota Granvia, Volkswagen people‑movers, and secondarily large people‑carrier SUVs (e.g., late‑model 7–8 seat SUVs) are the direct choices shoppers compare the iMax against in Australia ([CarsGuide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/vs/kia-carnival), [CarExpert Granvia vs iMax](https://www.carexpert.com.au/hyundai/imax/2025)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Hyundai iMax?

Exterior:
- Panel fit & trim durability  
  - Panels are generally simple and workmanlike — it’s a van at heart, not a couture sedan. Owners report trim fastener fatigue and rattles on sliding‑door trims; removal threads and DIY fixes are common on Australian forums ([Hyundai forums on trim removal](https://www.hyundai-forums.com/threads/removal-of-sliding-door-interior-trim-for-imax-starex.660024/)).  
  - Gloss‑finish plastics age quickly; expect scuffs on step rails and sliding doors where family life or taxi/rideshare duty has been heavy ([CarsGuide problems page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/problems)).  
- Paint durability & recalls  
  - There have been broader Hyundai paint‑peel warranty actions in recent years (manufacturer communications reference paint bubbling/peeling remedies) — check for paint warranty extensions and any panel resprays before buying ([NHTSA manufacturer communication on paint warranty](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2025/MC-11018888-0001.pdf)).  
- Electrical/fire‑risk recalls and sliding‑door hardware  
  - Important safety recall: Hyundai Australia recalled large numbers of iLoad/iMax (2016–2018 build groups) because an alternator‑fusebox cable sheath could deteriorate and expose wiring (fire risk). Check VIN against the official recall listing and verify dealer‑completed remedy paperwork ([REC‑006247](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-006247), reported analysis at [CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/hyundai-iload-imax-recalled-due-to-fire-risk)).  
  - Another recall logged for 2018 iMax/iLoad covered faulty sliding door locking mechanisms that may not hold the door open — important if the vehicle is used for frequent passenger loading ([REC‑000625](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-000625)).  
- ADAS & safety systems  
  - The iMax is an older platform and lacks the full suite of modern ADAS found in more current Hyundais. Earlier models rely on more basic stability and braking systems; later series added features like reversing cameras. Owners should not expect contemporary adaptive systems — and where adaptive cruise or driver aids exist, owner threads warn of quirks and limitations (ACC warnings and driver‑assist cautions are documented in Hyundai owner manuals and owner forums) ([Hyundai manual on Smart Cruise limitations](https://ownersmanual.hyundai.com/docview/webhelp/Hyundai/1adff4af-ff10-4fb6-93d8-0223b776dcd6/topic_h23_3tl_w5b.html), CarsGuide problem listings showing fewer modern ADAS features](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/problems)).

Interior:
- Cabin durability under heavy use  
  - The iMax interior was built to a light‑commercial spec: robust seat frames, hardwearing plastics and vinyl/leather depending on trim. That makes it durable for big families or shuttle work — but expect wear on seat bolsters, sliding‑door sills and scuff plates if the vehicle has seen taxi/rideshare use ([CarExpert 2020 Elite review on roomy but ageing cabin](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2020-hyundai-imax-elite-review)).  
  - Space and entry/exit are a strength — high roof, wide sliding doors and sensible step heights. Door check angles are generous but hinges and check‑straps on high‑usage sliding doors are common wear points (see sliding‑door recall above) ([REC‑000625](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-000625)).  
- Electrical behaviours and infotainment quirks  
  - Infotainment touchscreen faults have become a cross‑model issue in Hyundai/Kia late‑2010s to mid‑2020s hardware — symptoms reported include freezing, reboots and occasional blank screens. If the car has a mid‑life touchscreen, test it thoroughly for freezes and black screens; replacements are expensive if the unit is out of warranty ([Go‑Parts summary of 2020–2025 infotainment failures](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/infotainment-display-kia-ev6-hyundai-elantra-hyundai-kona-2020-2025), [Cuescreens on touchscreen delamination](https://www.cuescreens.com/blogs/news/fixing-hyundai-touchscreen-delamination-for-good)).  
  - Wireless charging and convenience tech can be fiddly — the owner manual explains conditions where pads won’t work, and owners commonly find case thickness/alignment or fuse/BMC issues cause false negatives ([Hyundai wireless charging manual text](https://ownersmanual.hyundai.com/docview/webhelp/Hyundai/0e169f84-6c7e-4286-a068-f3dbfd5bbb12/id5598fa5cc62.html)).  
  - Check for parasitic 12‑V battery drains if the vehicle has aftermarket gear (alarms, roof racks with lighting) — forum owners report occasional 12‑V quirks but not an epidemic. If the unit sits in a dealer yard, verify the charging system and battery health.

Mechanically:
- Brand engineering reputation & commercial DNA  
  - The iMax’s van roots mean durability and ease‑of‑service often beat flashy features. Hyundai’s D4CB diesel and associated drivetrains were built for heavy usage in light‑commercial roles and have extensive parts support across the brand’s LCV network ([Wikipedia Starex/H‑1 lineage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Starex)).  
- Security vulnerabilities & theft risk  
  - Older Hyundais (and some Kia models) were targeted by social‑media‑spread theft methods that exploited absent immobilisers; Hyundai/Kia ran an anti‑theft software upgrade campaign to retrofit protections on many affected vehicles — check if the car received the anti‑theft update and VIN eligibility ([NHTSA anti‑theft software update](https://www.nhtsa.gov/press-releases/hyundai-kia-campaign-prevent-vehicle-theft), Hyundai owner anti‑theft info page linked there).  
  - Modern theft vectors such as ‘headlight CAN injection’ attacks are real threats across many makes (attackers accessing exposed lamp wiring to inject CAN frames). Fit physical steering locks, hard‑wired immobiliser modules or dealer security updates where possible; park inside garages and consider aftermarket tracking as part of the TCO for older Hyundais ([Autoblog coverage on headlight CAN injection](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update), technical CAN injection explanation from Canis labs](https://www.can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf)).

Engines:
- *_**G4KG** (2.4‑litre petrol)_*  
  - What goes wrong: basic and resilient, but petrol versions carry higher running fuel cost and are less torquey than the diesel for fully laden work. Older 2.4s can show wear on intake systems and typical high‑mileage valve/valve‑seal issues if oil service intervals were ignored. Petrol auto variants sometimes exhibit gearbox‑related limp behaviour if the transmission was mistreated. (Spec references: parts list and engine code confirmation at [TotalEngines](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Hyundai/iMax), vehicle listings at [carsales](https://www.carsales.com.au/research/hyundai/imax/2020/active/)).  
  - Preventative maintenance: oil and filter every 10,000 km if using modern synthetic oil (10k is the practical Australian workshop cadence for high‑use family/commute duty); spark plugs, air filter and valve checks at 60–100k km depending on history. Confirm exact service schedule in the owner’s manual for that vehicle.  
  - Red flags pre‑purchase: inconsistent idle, blue smoke, excessive oil consumption, and documented missed services.

- *_**D4CB** (2.5‑litre turbo‑diesel CRDi)_*  
  - What goes wrong (common, real issues seen in Aussie service yards): injector seat washer leaks, turbocharger wear (VGT sticking or failure), EGR and soot build‑up, timing‑chain and tensioner wear on high‑mileage examples, and occasional high‑pressure fuel pump/injector failures. Independent Australian workshops list turbo failures, timing‑chain concerns and leaking injector seat washers as recurring jobs on the iLoad/iMax D4CB application ([Turbopros workshop summary](https://turbopros.com.au/blogs/news/hyundai-iload-imax-common-problems-and-solutions), D4CB engine guide details common faults](https://autopartswd.com/d4cb-engine-guide/)).  
  - Preventative maintenance:  
    - Oil & filter: every 10,000 km for hard use (taxis/shuttles) — 10k is conservative and protective for turbo diesels; owners on light private use sometimes stretch to 15k but that increases risk. If you want longevity: 10,000 km + top‑grade synthetic oil.  
    - Injector seat washers: inspect and consider preventative replacement near 100,000 km to avoid coolant/fuel leaks that can lead to head damage ([Turbopros advisory](https://turbopros.com.au/blogs/news/hyundai-iload-imax-common-problems-and-solutions)).  
    - Turbo and VGT: monitor boost/soot and a fast‑response service if power drops or black smoke appears; cleaning or replacement may be required on high‑km vans.  
    - EGR and intake cleaning: schedule at 80–120k km in heavy urban use.  
  - Red flags pre‑purchase: smoke on start/warm‑up, intermittent power loss, loud turbo whining, coolant loss with no visible leak (could point to injector seat washers), and heavy black smoke under load.

Transmission:
- Petrol autos and diesel automatics in the iMax family are conventional torque‑converter automatics (5‑speed in many diesel auto specs). Manuals exist (5 or 6‑speed depending on engine/market). Known failure/issue patterns:  
  - Symptoms: slipping under acceleration, delayed up/down shifts, harsh or jerky engagement, and limp‑home modes reported on owner forums ([CarsGuide gearbox problems](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/problems/transmission), forum reports of sudden loss of power and transmission issues](https://www.hyundaiforum.com/forum/new-member-area-4/imax-sudden-loss-power-transmission-issue-19681/)).  
  - Causes: wear of clutch packs and torque converters, valve body faults, and poor fluid maintenance accelerate failure. Diesel models used in heavy payload or towing accumulate heat cycles that shorten transmission life.  
  - Preventative care: insist on service history showing periodic transmission fluid and filter changes (if the model and year specify it). Where no history exists, budget for a transmission service and diagnostic scan pre‑purchase. Parts availability: transmissions for iMax/iLoad are listed by Australian suppliers for rebuilds and swaps ([Aus Auto Parts gearbox listings](https://www.ausautoparts.com.au/parts/hyundai-iload-imax-transmission-gearbox)).  
  - Repairs: torque‑converter replacement and valve‑body rebuilds are the common shop jobs; costs vary widely but expect significant bills if a full gearbox overhaul is required.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Hyundai iMax?

  - Commentary: That single NSW dealer listing (a 2020 iMax, ~75k km, $29,888) sets a useful local market anchor for buyers in NSW shopping dealer‑backed examples. Use it as the comparables reference when you haggle.

- Current used marketplace entry price up to ceiling price (Australia)  
  - Based on national listing sites and market aggregators, older iMax examples can be found from low‑teens (very old, high‑km) up to low‑30s AUD for late‑model, low‑km dealer examples. Your NSW dealer snapshot shows late‑model 2020 examples around $29,900 retail; private sale bargains will be lower but come with higher risk ([carsales iMax listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/hyundai/imax/people-mover-bodystyle/), [CarValue price trends](https://carvalue.app/Hyundai/iMax/all/)).

- Depreciation metrics  
  - Hyundais generally lose a fair slab of value in the first 3–5 years — brand studies suggest 45–50% depreciation at five years depending on model. The iMax, being an older platform and sold in a price‑sensitive commercial/people‑carrier niche, depreciates quicker than trendier SUVs; expect a sharper early drop than segment rivals that refreshed recently (Kia Carnival, Toyota Granvia) ([CarEdge depreciation notes](https://caredge.com/hyundai/depreciation), [CarDepreciation Hyundai rates](https://cardepreciation.org/hyundai/)).  
  - Practical baseline: if a new iMax originally sold near mid‑$40k–$50k (varying by trim and year), a 2020 dealer example at ~$30k aligns with roughly the expected 3–4‑year depreciation pattern, particularly with ~75k km on the clock.

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy & analysis (diesel vs petrol)  
  - Fuel use baseline (Australian cycle): the factory combined figure for the 2.5 CRDi auto is around 8.8 L/100 km (urban 11.2 / extra‑urban 7.3), with a 75 L tank per the official spec sheet — use this as your baseline when modelling fuel spend for the diesel ([Hyundai iMax spec sheet — 2.5 CRDi 8.8 L/100 km](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/models/imax/docs/Hyundai_iMax_Specifications_Sheet.pdf), CarsGuide fuel consumption page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/imax/fuel-consumption)). Real‑world owner fuel logs differ: Fuelly owner data shows variance depending on use and load — expect higher consumption in heavy urban shuttle duty ([Fuelly iMax page](https://www.fuelly.com/car/hyundai/imax)).  
  - TCO items to include in your calculations: fuel (L/100 km * km/year * fuel price), servicing (10k vs 15k intervals change oil/filter costs), repairs (turbo, injectors, transmission), insurance, rego, and depreciation. For high‑km or commercial use, the diesel’s better torque and lower long‑haul fuel cost usually offset its higher maintenance risk — but injectors/turbo repairs can be costly. Petrol versions are cheaper to maintain for the long term in mild use but more thirsty and less suited when towing or carrying 8 adults regularly. Use local fuel price assumptions (AUD $1.70–2.00/L as an example driver) and expected annual km to model break‑even. Tools like CostMyCar or AFDC calculators can formalise the math ([CostMyCar TCO tool](https://costmycar.com/), AFDC vehicle cost calculator](https://afdc.energy.gov/calc/)).  
  - Hybrid / EV alternative: the iMax wasn’t offered as a hybrid/EV in Australia. Comparing an iMax diesel to a hybrid Kia Carnival or an EV people‑mover is a separate exercise: hybrids usually require a premium that pays back on urban stop‑start duty only after high annual km; EVs carry charging infrastructures and higher upfront premiums. For conservative family buyers doing lots of highway KM with towing or full loads, a diesel iMax can still be economical, provided the vehicle has been well serviced and key diesel wear items addressed.

- Is it worth paying a premium for late‑model or Elite trim? Which year/trim is the sweet spot?  
  - If you want simple, rugged people‑moving without frills and lower purchase price, aim for 2016–2018 examples that have had recall work done and a documented service history. For better creature comforts (leather, additional convenience equipment) and the most up‑to‑date safety tweaks, a 2020 Elite with a full dealer service history is the sweet spot — but you’ll pay a premium near that $29–30k band (your NSW listing is a direct example). CarExpert and Drive noted the Elite for its roomy cabin and equipment but also called the platform ageing compared with modern rivals ([CarExpert 2020 Elite review](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2020-hyundai-imax-elite-review), [Drive review](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/hyundai-imax-review/)).  
  - Recommendation: for the Australian buyer who prioritises value and ruggedness, a 2016–2018 iMax with documented mechanical injector/turbo work and completed recalls represents the best compromise. If you want the most comfort and don’t mind paying dealer premiums for lower km and extra kit, a 2019–2020 Elite/Active with full Hyundai service history and recall compliance is the safer, albeit pricier, pick.

- Final buying recommendation (clear and blunt)  
  - Yes — but only with caveats. Buy a 2.5 CRDi diesel iMax if you regularly carry 6–8 people or tow/light commercial use: the diesel’s torque is what makes the iMax a sensible workhorse. However — treat every example like a vintage work truck: insist on full service history, proof the alternator/fusebox recall and sliding‑door recall work were completed, and do a compression/scan and a test for turbo/injector symptoms. If the dealer asks full price (~$29,888 in NSW for a 2020 with ~75k km), you should expect tidy service records and a clean recall history. If the history’s thin, walk — the likely bills (injectors, turbo, transmission) make a deal look poor fast. For most families wanting lower ongoing worry and more modern safety/comfort, the Kia Carnival or Toyota Granvia will be easier to live with and retain value better in the medium term.

Bottom line: the iMax does the job it was designed for — carry people reliably and cheaply — but you’re buying a van with passenger seats, not a modern luxury people‑mover. Check recalls, be paranoid about injector/turbo/transmission history on diesels, and don’t pay full retail unless the paperwork is spotless. If you want cheap space and don’t mind a bit of workshop reality, it’s a decent tool. If you crave tech, slick ADAS and plush residuals, walk to the Carnival or Granvia.
