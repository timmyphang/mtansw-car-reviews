#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation, production timeframe and internal codes
  - The HSV/“Holden” GTS is the long-running performance flagship sold under Holden Special Vehicles (HSV) badge — produced in HSV form from 1992 through to 2017. The later-generation GTS sits on GM’s **Zeta** architecture (GM Zeta platform for 2006–2017), while earlier GTS iterations were built on GM’s older V platform (1992–2006). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Special_Vehicles_GTS)
- Where it sits in the line-up
  - The GTS is HSV’s top‑tier performance sedan in normal years — the model above the regular Commodore and above lesser HSV trims. The absolute halo above some GTS runs was the limited‑run GTSR and the Warbird/W1 specials; below the GTS sit other HSV derivatives (clubsport, Clubsport R8, etc.). [HSV GTS page](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/) and the GTS history summary on [Carsales](https://www.carsales.com.au/editorial/details/the-history-of-the-gts-40687/).
- Shared global platform architecture and parts availability
  - The modern GTS is Commodore-derived, built on GM’s Zeta platform. That platform underpinned multiple cars — commodore derivatives used across the HSV family and global GM derivatives — which helps parts availability for fundamental structure, suspension and driveline components. For platform context see [Wikipedia’s HSV GTS page](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Special_Vehicles_GTS).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive layouts (summary)
  - Engines you’ll find in GTS cars (depending on era):
    - Supercharged 6.2‑litre V8 **LSA** (late‑era HSV GTS; headline output ~430 kW / 740 Nm in HSV marketing). [HSV product page](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/)
    - Naturally aspirated **LS2/LS3** small‑block V8s (earlier modern GTS/Commodore era)
    - Classic Holden and stroked Chevy V8s in earlier Monaro/GTS lineages (5.0L, 5.7L variants listed in historical specs). [Wikipedia history & spec pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Special_Vehicles_GTS)
  - Transmissions:
    - Tremec **TR‑6060** 6‑speed manual (MG9 spec) with oil cooler and twin‑plate clutch standard on many GTS examples. [HSV GTS page](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/)
    - Optional heavy‑duty 6‑speed automatic **6L90E** (GM 6‑speed automatics used across Commodore/Holdens). [HSV GTS page](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/)
  - Drive configuration: rear‑wheel drive only — this is proper muscle sedan territory.
- Marketplace rivals
  - When new the HSV GTS was pitched against high‑power European performance sedans and luxury performance packages — the BMW M5, Audi RS6 (and more broadly Mercedes‑AMG E63). Australian coverage and comparisons have made that point repeatedly. [LSX Mag compare piece](https://lsxmag.com/news/infographic-compares-holden-hsv-gts-to-the-competition/) and market pages like [CarsGuide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hsv/gts).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden GTS?

Exterior
- Corrosion and paint issues
  - Rust is a known recurring problem on older Holdens, and GTS examples are no exception — professional rust checks are recommended, especially on cars that have seen coastal storage or winter salt. AutoGuru lists rust repair as a common exterior job for Holden GTS examples. [AutoGuru rust repair](https://www.autoguru.com.au/repairs/rust-repair/holden/gts)
  - Owners report clearcoat delamination and paint flaking in areas exposed to the sun/age — threads on Just Commodores document paint flaking on roof rails and panels, particularly on older cars where factory protection has long expired. [Just Commodores thread](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/paint-flaking-off.295675/)
- Panel fit and body repair indicators
  - Check panel gaps, door alignment and boot shut lines — HSV conversions and aftermarket repairs are common, and uneven gaps often point to non‑original panels or crash repairs. General guidance on panel gaps and how to judge them is covered by collision‑repair guides. [Genuine Collision Center explainer](https://www.genuinecollisioncenter.com/blog/what-causes-panel-gaps-in-cars/)
- Trim & exterior fittings
  - Satin/gloss plastics and badges on HSV cars can show scratches and wear. Confirm centre caps, badges and AGR/HSV‑branded bits are original — replacements are available through HSV parts houses but cost can add up.
- ADAS & safety electronics
  - Later Commodore‑based cars adopted AEB and lane‑assist features as standard across tested variants; however, ADAS sensors can be fussy after repairs or if lenses are dirty. ANCAP’s Commodore safety report confirms AEB/LKA inclusion on tested platform variants. [ANCAP Commodore safety listing](https://www.ancap.com.au/safety-ratings/holden/commodore/21bc54)
  - Phantom braking/false AEB events are an industry issue (not GTS‑unique). If a car has had front bumper or sensor work, insist on a recalibration. Industry reporting on AEB phantom braking highlights software/sensor sensitivity as a continuing issue. [Headlight.News on phantom braking](https://headlight.news/2024/08/23/aeb-phantom-braking-is-causing-growing-number-of-crashes-and-recalls/)

Interior
- Seat and trim wear
  - GTS seats are bolstered and premium but the foam and upholstery wear under heavy duty (rideshare, track use). GTS seat‑foam collapse and repairs are discussed by owners — replacement/upholstery costs can be significant. [Just Commodores seat foam thread](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/gts-seat-foam.275227/)
- Infotainment and displays
  - Holden’s MyLink touchscreen units are prone to failures (blank screens, no touch response, flicker). Specialist vendors offer repair services for dead MyLink units — a common real‑world fault that can be fixed but is not cheap if you need a new OEM unit. [MyLink repair service listing](https://getelectronic.net/products/holden-lcd-touch-screen-mylink)
  - Screens can glitch from software, wiring or battery/voltage irregularities — basic soft reset steps can help, but persistent failures usually need module repair or replacement. [ImproveCarAudio on screen causes](https://improvecaraudio.com/why-is-my-car-screen-glitching/)
- Ergonomics and access
  - Entry/egress is typical large‑sedan (generous door aperture, mid‑height sill). Bolstered seats help on track but can be fiddly for older knees — check door check strap condition and hinge alignment during inspection.
- Electrical quirks
  - Expect some electrical gremlins on high‑km, older HSV cars: intermittent cruise control stalk faults and glitches have been reported on Commodore lineage cars (see long forum threads on VE cruise faults). Check stalk functions, heated seat elements, and look for parasitic draws during overnight park tests. [VE cruise thread](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/ve-cruise-control-fault-detailed-investigation.304398/)

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation & long‑term notes
  - The GTS is built from Commodore hardware with HSV performance upgrades. In broad terms the engineering is robust when serviced properly — but high power + track use increases wear. Regular service history is everything.
- Security vulnerabilities
  - Modern Holdens share GM/GMLAN CAN wiring patterns; the academic and practical CAN injection (CAN‑bus spoofing) attacks show a risk: an attacker can inject messages if they gain physical access to wiring to bypass immobilisers. This is an industry‑wide weakness, not HSV‑specific — but high‑value cars like GTS are attractive targets. Recommended owner mitigations include OBD‑port locks, physical steering locks and active vehicle tracking. [CAN Injection explainer](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/) and technical paper overview. [OBD/CAN hacks — Australian locksmith note](https://www.speedycarkeys.com.au/how-obd-ports-and-can-bus-hacks-are-used-by-thieves/)
- Aftermarket anti‑theft recommendations
  - Fit an OBD port lock, fit a high‑quality GPS telematics tracker with tamper alerts, and consider mechanical deterrents (wheel clamps / visible steering lock) if you park in high‑risk areas.

Engines
- The engines section below lists each common engine/variant you will meet in the market; each subsection italicised and grounded in documented owner reports or specialist write‑ups.

  - *LS1* (early modern LS family)
    - Known faults: valvetrain/lifter/cam issues when lubrication is compromised; oil leaks are a frequent complaint on older Holden V8s. Owners have reported cam/roller damage in highly tuned or poorly maintained LS1s. [Fastlane / VE forum stories & Oztrack summary](https://oztrack.com.au/ls-engine-trouble-information-hsv-commodore/) and forum accounts of specific failures.
    - Preventative maintenance: frequent oil checks; change oil every 10,000 km if using OEM‑grade oil and normal use, sooner (5–7,500 km) if hard‑used or track‑driven. Check valve‑train lifters and run a high‑quality oil filter.
  - *LS2 / LS3* (naturally aspirated small‑block V8s)
    - Known faults: typical LS‑family wear — lifter noise, oil leaks from gaskets, and cooling system items (thermostat housings, hoses). Oztrack lists common LS family wear items and servicing needs. [Oztrack LS info](https://oztrack.com.au/ls-engine-trouble-information-hsv-commodore/)
    - Preventative maintenance: oil/service interval guidance as above; inspect cooling system hoses and thermostat housing at 100,000 km and replace brittle components earlier.
  - *LSA* (supercharged 6.2L — late‑era GTS headline engine)
    - Known faults and stress points:
      - Supercharged engines demand meticulous oil and cooling maintenance. High cylinder pressures make the supercharged LSA sensitive to poor fuel quality, neglected oil changes and lower‑grade coolant. HSV documentation and marketing list the LSA as the 6.2L supercharged unit used in late GTS cars. [HSV GTS product overview](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/)
      - Typical failure points on supercharged LS derivatives include high‑pressure fuel pump wear if exposed to contamination, supercharger belt/tensioner wear, and thermally stressed intercooler plumbing on high‑km cars.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil changes every 7,500–10,000 km for road use; shorter intervals (5,000–7,500 km) for track use or high‑stress driving.
      - Inspect supercharger drive, tensioner and intercooler plumbing every major service (10,000–15,000 km) and replace belts/tensioners on recommended schedules or signs of wear.
      - Use high‑quality fuel (recommended RON), and monitor fuel trims and knock sensors — supercharged engines are less forgiving of poor fuel/ignition timing.
    - Reference for LS family faults and supercharged care: Oztrack LS notes and HSV technical specs. [Oztrack LS info](https://oztrack.com.au/ls-engine-trouble-information-hsv-commodore/) and HSV product page for outputs. [HSV GTS](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/)
  - *LS9* (limited halo engines in special models)
    - In limited W‑series and special cars (GTSR W1 etc.) the LS9 appeared — these are bespoke and high‑stress engines that need specialist maintenance. If you find one, expect collector‑grade costs and rare spares.

Transmission
- Manual — **Tremec TR‑6060**
  - Behavior: a heavy‑duty dog‑legbox in feel; strong and generally robust when driven reasonably. Common wear items are the clutch (twin‑plate on many GTS examples), release bearings and synchromesh rings after hard launches or track abuse. HSV factory spec shows the TR‑6060 on GTS cars with oil cooler and twin‑plate clutch. [HSV spec page](https://www.hsv.com.au/see/gts/)
  - Diagnostics: check for judder, grinding into gears, and clutch slip under load. Twin‑plate clutches give excellent initial bite but when worn are expensive to replace.
- Automatic — GM 6‑speed automatics (6L‑series, e.g., **6L90E**)
  - Behavior & known failure modes: these torque‑converter automatics are generally strong but GM 6‑series boxes have come under scrutiny for torque‑converter clutch and shudder problems on a range of Holden GM derivatives. Maurice Blackburn’s class action lists owner complaints of shuddering, harsh shifts and ATF leaks for 6L‑series transmissions used across Holdens. If you see an automatic GTS, test thoroughly for shudder, leak stains and rough engagement. [Maurice Blackburn class action](https://www.mauriceblackburn.com.au/class-actions/join-a-class-action/holden-transmission-class-action/) and GMAuthority reporting. [GMAuthority summary](https://gmauthority.com/blog/2025/01/gm-hit-with-class-action-lawsuit-over-defective-holden-vehicle-transmissions/)
  - Preventative/service: insist on documented ATF changes at regular intervals (factory/real‑world guidance suggests 60,000–100,000 km service windows for fluid change depending on use; for high‑power HSV cars consider changing ATF closer to 60k km and using heavy‑duty, correct‑spec fluid).

- Current used marketplace entry and ceiling price
  - As of the NSW snapshot you’ll be looking at about AUD $116k for the low‑end 2015 GTS up to roughly AUD $145k for showroom/low‑km special examples — the median sits around **AUD $130,495** with typical examples showing ~55,000 km. This is Australian dealer pricing for 2015 GTS examples (two active listings in the snapshot).
- Depreciation metrics & collector context
  - This isn’t your average family sedan — GTS cars occupy a performance/collector niche. Unlike ordinary four‑door Commodores, GTS examples have a stronger hold on value: specialised HSV models hold value better than standard mass‑market equivalents, with some classic Monaro/GTS examples fetching collector sums (see high auction prices for classic Monaro GTS models). [ABC News auction example](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2024-06-08/holden-monaro-gts-muscle-car-echuca-motor-museum-auction/103944118)
  - Realistically, a 3‑year depreciation curve for a late‑model GTS bought new would have been steeper than a German‑luxury rival because of smaller market scale; however, on the used market today these cars are trading strongly because of rarity and collector interest. Use carsales and classic markets to benchmark individual examples. [CarsGuide GTS showrooms](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hsv/gts) and classic aggregators like [Classic.com](https://www.classic.com/m/holden/) can help you track realised selling prices.
- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Fuel economy & running costs
    - Don’t kid yourself — a supercharged 6.2L GTS drinks fuel. Use the CarsGuide and Analyticauto consumption summaries for realistic per‑100 km rates, and add a margin for spirited driving. [CarsGuide fuel consumption entries](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hsv/gts/fuel-consumption) and [Analyticauto fuel stats](https://analyticauto.com/all/hsv/gts/fuel)
    - Estimate annual fuel cost: if you average 10 L/100 km in mixed driving (conservative for an LSA under normal use) and you do 15,000 km/yr at petrol AUD $1.70/L, fuel = ~AUD $2,550/yr. If you drive it hard or track it, double that figure easily.
  - Insurance and servicing
    - Expect higher insurance premiums due to high value and performance status. Servicing costs are higher than mainstream Commodore due to HSV‑specific parts, brakes (AP Racing hardware) and performance calibrations.
    - Consumables (tyres, brake pads, shoddy clutch if manual) are a real cost: 20‑inch tyres and high‑grip rubber are expensive; budget accordingly.
  - Depreciation vs premium powertrains
    - Hybrid/EV comparisons are academic here — GTS is a big‑power petrol V8 with no hybrid alternative in the HSV catalogue. Paying a premium for an EV/hybrid in the same performance bracket (if it existed) would only make sense if the purchase premium could be offset by lower running costs. For GTS buyers the decision is power and character vs economy. For city commuters an efficient sedan is the smarter financial choice; for the enthusiast after a V8 experience, TCO penalties are accepted as part of ownership.
- Buying recommendation — who should buy and which example
  - Buy one if:
    - You value a true Aussie V8 experience, want a limited‑volume HSV, and accept above‑average running and insurance costs.
    - You can verify full service history, and the car has not been abused on track or poorly modified.
  - Walk away if:
    - You want cheap daily transport or low running costs — this isn’t the car for daily austerity.
    - The dealer can’t show clear, documented service history, or the car has signs of heavy tune/track use without receipts.
  - Optimal financial and ownership sweet spot
    - Based on the NSW market snapshot, a late‑model 2015 GTS with ~50–80k km that’s had regular oil changes, factory service items and no track history is the practical sweet spot. The median listing (2015, 54,629 km, AUD $130,495) looks like a good reference point — negotiate on documented history and get a pre‑purchase inspection that checks transfusion items: supercharger belt and tensioner, ATF condition (if auto), clutch condition (if manual) and evidence of any front‑end repairs (ADAS sensors).
- Final verdict (short)
  - If you want the last of the Australian V8 muscle in full HSV form, and you can stomach running costs and are rigorous about service history, a GTS is a desirable, characterful buy — but only buy a GTS with receipts, a full mechanical inspection and an appreciation that these are not bargain daily cars. Given the NSW supply and prices, a clean 2015 with 40–70k km at the median price is the pragmatic sweet spot.
