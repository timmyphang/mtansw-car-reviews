#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation / timeframe / codes
  - The Hyundai Getz (single-generation TB series / TB‑FL facelift) was produced 2002–2011; the mid‑life facelift arrived in 2005 (commonly referred to as TB → TB‑FL). [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Getz) and period brochures document the single‑generation run and the 2005 facelift (styling and equipment tweaks) [Archive.org brochure](https://archive.org/download/hyundaigetzbrochure/Hyundai%20Getz%20Brochure.pdf).
- Where it sits in the lineup
  - The Getz was Hyundai’s small supermini — the cheap, practical hatch that sat below the i20/i30 sized cars in Hyundai’s global small‑car hierarchy of the 2000s. It was the “practical little mate” in a showroom otherwise trying to look a bit more grown up.
- Platform architecture & parts availability
  - The Getz uses Hyundai’s TB-era small‑car architecture (a compact front‑wheel‑drive platform shared among Hyundai small hatchbacks of that period). Extensive spec catalogues confirm the Getz’s TB family roots and global mechanical commonality across petrol and diesel variants (useful for parts interchange and aftermarket support) [Automobile‑Catalog](https://www.automobile-catalog.com/model/hyundai/getz.html), [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/hyundai-getz-model-1483).
- Engines, outputs, transmissions, drive configurations (market‑specific)
  - Petrol:
    - 1.1 MPI — ~62–65 PS (low‑power city car option) [Auto‑Data / CarInf].  
    - 1.3 / 1.4 MPI (commonly 1.3–1.4) — ~82–97 PS; the 1.4 is the most common Australian petrol choice. [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/hyundai-getz-model-1483)
    - 1.6 (G4ED) — ~106 PS; higher performance, but noted as an interference‑engine in some sources. [Hyundaiparts / what‑breaks summary]
  - Diesel:
    - 1.5 CRDi — ~82–110 PS depending on tune; the diesel was offered in many markets for better economy. [AutoData / CarInf]
  - Transmissions:
    - 5‑speed manual (most common), and several automatic choices (4‑speed torque‑converter automatics on some petrols; no modern CVT in this era Getz family listings). [Cars‑Data transmission list](https://www.cars-data.com/en/hyundai/getz/spec/transmission)
  - Drive:
    - Front‑wheel drive only for the production run.
- Marketplace rivals
  - In the small hatch segment the Getz competes with the Kia Rio, Suzuki Swift, Toyota Yaris/Echo, Mazda 2 and similar European small hatchbacks. Comparative resources position the Getz against the Kia Rio and other small hatches of the time [CarSpecsLab, Greenfleet road test]. [CarSpecsLab](https://www.carspecslab.com/hyundai/getz/rivals) and the Greenfleet review praise its practicality in that bracket [Greenfleet](https://greenfleet.net/road-tests/22012007/hyundai-getz).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Hyundai Getz?
Short answer: nothing dramatically catastrophic most of the time — but old age, cheap interior plastics, a few electronics gremlins and some known engine/transmission gotchas mean you do your homework or pay for it later.

- Exterior
  - Panel alignment & body condition
    - Getz body shells are simple and robust; alignment is normally fine but check for crumple repairs and uneven gaps on cars that have been in tight urban life. The mid‑2000s facelift (2005) changed bumpers/headlamp surrounds — use that to spot whether the shell is original [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Getz), [Parkers](https://www.parkers.co.uk/hyundai/getz/hatchback-2002/specs/).
  - Paint durability
    - Older Getz examples commonly show clearcoat and lacquer degradation — owners report peeling and bubbling especially on white/pearl finishes; online threads and news stories track owner frustration with peeling paint on some Hyundai models from that era [Drive](https://www.drive.com.au/news/hyundai-owners-angry-over-paint-peeling-problem/) and owner forum threads on lacquer peeling. Expect faded or crazed clear on 15+ year old panels unless the car has been resprayed.
  - Trim vulnerabilities
    - Cheap gloss plastics and trim pieces scratch and discolour; bumpers and lower plastic trims are prone to scuffs. Door handle paint finish and badges often show wear.
  - Body recalls & safety systems
    - No major high‑profile structural recall specific to Australian Getz examples in these search results, but always VIN‑check for outstanding recalls using national tools (VIN recaller searches are recommended — official Hyundai recall portals exist in markets) [Hyundai recall lookup reference](https://autoservice.hyundaiusa.com/campaignhome). Aggregators list a mix of minor campaigns in global records [Vindecoderz](https://www.vindecoderz.com/EN/Hyundai/GETZ/problems-recalls-complaints).
  - ADAS / safety electronics
    - The Getz is largely pre‑ADAS era: you won’t find lane‑keep or modern AEB. Cruise control can be temperamental on some cars — owner reports show sporadic cruise failures and ABS/ESP warnings appearing on older cars; owners recommend checking ABS sensors and wiring if warning lamps are lit [MyCarly cruise control thread](https://www.mycarly.com/community/getz-cruise-control-failure-6fa6909a-71bf-40bf-8a6b-c1444b447a6e/), and a forum post reported ESP/ABS faults linked to near‑accident situations on a late getz [Hyundai forum ABS/ESP story](https://www.hyundai-forums.com/threads/cautionary-tale-near-accident-esp-abs-problem.719839/).
    - Later firmware fixes are irrelevant here — Getz lacks advanced ADAS that would receive OTA updates.

- Interior
  - Cabin durability & heavy‑use duty
    - The Getz’s interior was aimed at budget durability — plain plastics, simple switchgear and basic cloth trim. For rideshare/taxi duty the Getz is workable but you’ll see:
      - Scuffed door sills and sagging seat bolsters on higher mileages.
      - Cheap steering‑wheel finishes that wear quickly.
    - Owner communities flag common wear on switches, window regulators and central locking after decades of use [Hyundai Forum](https://www.hyundaiforum.com/forum/hyundai-getz-36/), [Autodoc blog on common faults](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-hyundai-getz).
  - Ergonomics — entry/exit and space
    - Lowish door sills and a compact cabin make entry/exit easy for urban users; rear legroom is fine for the class but adults will feel it on long runs. Door check angles are standard — watch for worn door check straps which can make doors sag or not hold open.
  - Electrical behaviours & infotainment
    - 12V battery drain: older Getz owners report parasitic drains (central locking modules, aftermarket alarms or failing alternator diodes can be culprits). Diagnose with an ammeter test if a vehicle sits overnight and won’t start.
    - Infotainment: original radios are basic single‑DIN units. If a Getz has a modern touchscreen retrofit, treat it as aftermarket (check harness work). Forums discuss frozen screens and head‑unit resets for older Hyundais in a general sense [Cuescreens / infotainment freezing](https://www.cuescreens.com/blogs/news/hyundai-touchscreen-freezing-in-cold-weather) and DIY resets [Fatboysoffroad guide](https://fatboysoffroad.com/how-to-reset-hyundai-infotainment-system/).
    - Screen sizes / hardware: the factory Getz had no large factory touchscreen options in Australia — most models have basic radios, with larger audio options rare and largely market‑dependent [Parkers / brochure references].
    - Connectivity reliability (AUX, Bluetooth): if fitted via aftermarket head unit it's as reliable as the install. Factory units are simple and generally robust but feature‑limited.

- Mechanically
  - Brand engineering reputation
    - Hyundai’s small engines of the 2000s have a serviceable reputation — inexpensive parts, simple engineering and decent service network make the Getz cheap to keep. However, a few engine/transmission notes below deserve attention. General problems lists collated by community resources show a mixture of electrical and wear‑items rather than systemic failures [What‑Breaks overview](https://what-breaks.com/hyundai/getz-tb/).
  - Theft/security vulnerabilities
    - Immobiliser: the Getz has a factory immobiliser; the manual describes normal behaviour and indicator lights for the system [Hyundai owners manual immobliser ref](https://ownersmanual.hyundai.com/docview/webhelp/Hyundai/5375bf28-c9d4-4116-920b-b92a819711e6/id7595746de1d.html). However, small‑car immobilisers of this age are not immune to determined bypass; aftermarket "Immo Off" services exist for legitimate ECU work — this also demonstrates that immobiliser bypass tools are present in the wild [CKECU Immo Off mention](https://ckecu.com/tag/hyundai-getz-immo-off/).  
    - CAN‑injection style thefts are a modern attack vector against vehicles whose networks trust certain modules — while the Getz’s simpler architecture and age mean it is less likely to be attacked like recent cars, the broader technical literature shows CAN‑bus trust is a vulnerability to be aware of for vehicle security in general [Ken Tindell CAN injection write‑up](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/), [Schneier summary](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2023/04/car-thieves-hacking-the-can-bus.html).
  - Recommended anti‑theft measures
    - Physical steering wheel lock, SORN‑grade visible immobiliser sticker, and a hardwired alarm/cut relay for fuel pump are cheap, effective deterrents. Fit a proven aftermarket engine‑immobiliser or a wheel‑to‑chassis lock if you live in a high‑theft area.

- Engines
  - Note: every engine entry below is italicised and tagged with bold engine codes where available — problems and preventative actions follow.
  - *G4HG / 1.1 MPI (≈62–65 PS)*
    - Low power, very cheap to run. Typical failure points: worn ignition coils and aged starter components (owner reports). Good for low‑kilometre urban use.
    - Preventative maintenance: regular plugs/ignition checks; verify service history for starter/alternator swaps.
    - Sources: general specs [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/hyundai-getz-model-1483), owner forums [Hyundai Forum](https://www.hyundaiforum.com/forum/hyundai-getz-36/).
  - *G4EE / G4EA / 1.3–1.4 MPI (≈82–97 PS)*
    - The workhorse petrols: generally robust but watch for:
      - Ignition coils and spark plug wear causing misfires and starting complaints [Autodoc common faults](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-hyundai-getz).
      - Cam/timing belt wear — these engines are timing‑belt units; maintain cambelt per service book to avoid catastrophic failure. The community emphasises timing‑belt health and scheduled replacement [Hyundaiparts writeups / what‑breaks lists](https://what-breaks.com/hyundai/getz-tb/).
    - Preventative maintenance: regular oil changes, spark plugs and ignition checks; replace timing belt at manufacturer interval (or earlier on high‑mileage cars).
  - *G4ED / 1.6 MPI (≈106 PS) — bold caution*
    - The *1.6 G4ED* is flagged in community and technical write‑ups as an interference engine — a snapped cambelt can allow pistons to strike valves, producing expensive engine damage. Owners and guides explicitly warn of this risk on the 1.6 Getz [Hyundaiparts / what‑breaks summary].
    - Preventative maintenance: insist on documented timing‑belt replacements; if service history is missing, budget for cambelt + water pump and tensioner immediately. Consider a compression/leak‑down test on purchase.
    - Sources: what‑breaks / hyundaispares summary [what‑breaks](https://what-breaks.com/hyundai/getz-tb/), Hyundaiparts summaries.
  - *D4FA / 1.5 CRDi diesel (≈82–110 PS)*
    - Diesel advantages: markedly better fuel consumption (official ~4.8 L/100 km according to AutoData), torque for highway overtakes. Common diesel points:
      - High‑pressure fuel system and injector issues in older diesels can be costly if neglected.
      - Diesel particulate systems and EGR components can clog on low‑use cars.
    - Preventative maintenance: regular fuel‑filter changes, periodic long runs to clear DPF/EGR residue (if fitted), and service of HPFP/injectors as needed. Real world owners report gas savings but recommend attentive maintenance [AutoData fuel figures](https://www.auto-data.net/en/hyundai-getz-model-1483), [Fuelly real world usage](https://www.fuelly.com/car/hyundai/getz).
    - Sources: AutoData fuel figures and what‑breaks diesel notes.

- Transmission
  - Manuals
    - 5‑speed manuals are simple and durable; check for clutch slip and gearbox whine and ensure the gearshift feels positive. Most community reports show manuals are the lowest‑cost long‑term option.
  - Torque‑converter automatics (4‑speed & older 4‑speed AT)
    - Known symptoms:
      - Cold‑shift harshness (won’t upshift easily from 1→2 until warm), slipping or hesitation reported in some owner threads — a possible sign of wear in the torque converter or valve body, or old trans fluid. See MyCarly and Hyundai‑forum threads describing owners’ transmission lights and slipping symptoms [MyCarly torque converter post](https://www.mycarly.com/community/getz-torque-converter-issue-b5fd9208-cb76-4ddc-acf1-9976b8b06126/), and community repair shops documentation [ASR Gearbox Repairs](https://www.asrgearboxrepairs.co.uk/hyundai-getz-common-gearbox-problems/).
      - Age‑related wear: fluid breakdown, worn clutch packs or solenoids in older automatics — get a transmission service history or budget for a fluid + filter swap and inspection.
    - Preventative maintenance: exchange fluid/filter at sensible intervals (if unknown, do it immediately after purchase). If cold‑shift issues persist, get a transmission diagnostic before buying.

- Entry price to ceiling price (NSW dealer data)
  - Entry: $6,990 — likely a higher‑mileage or lower‑spec manual/cheapie.  
  - Ceiling: $7,990 — likely a tidy late‑plate example with lower km, private sale help or dealer warranty. Median $7,490 is the sweet spot to aim at in dealer negotiations.

- Depreciation metrics
  - The Getz today is an end‑of‑life small car: values are low and fairly flat. Historically Hyundai small cars depreciate like other mass‑market hatches; brand‑level reports show Hyundais lose significant value in early years but stabilise in the secondhand market [CarEdge depreciation context](https://caredge.com/hyundai/depreciation). The Getz’s biggest depreciation has already happened — you’re buying a car that’s largely bottomed‑out in value. Expect modest further declines, mainly driven by condition and mileage rather than age.

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Running costs you must budget for:
    - Fuel: official figures show petrol 1.4 ≈7.0 L/100 km and diesel 1.5 CRDi ≈4.8 L/100 km [AutoData fuel figures](https://www.auto-data.net/en/hyundai-getz-model-1483). Real world owner logs (Fuelly) align roughly but often show petrol running a bit higher in mixed driving [Fuelly](https://www.fuelly.com/car/hyundai/getz).
      - A practical calculation trick: multiply (fuel consumption difference in L/100 km) × (annual km/100) to see litres saved per year by diesel. Example: diesel saves ~2.2 L/100 km → on 15,000 km/year that's ~330 L saved. Multiply by local fuel price to see annual savings.
    - Maintenance & parts: cheap parts and a common engine family mean parts are readily available and inexpensive compared with European small cars. Community resources and parts catalogues indicate good parts availability [Automobile‑Catalog](https://www.automobile-catalog.com/model/hyundai/getz.html).
    - Repairs to watch: timing‑belt jobs (especially the 1.6), fuel injector or HPFP on diesel, ignition coils/plugs on petrols, and possible automatic gearbox works on older autos.
    - Insurance & registration: as a small cheap car, premiums tend to be low — check insurers for ACT/NSW specifics.
  - Should you pay a premium for diesel?
    - Math‑first approach: diesel is more economical per km (≈2.2 L/100 km better on paper). If a diesel example costs a premium, calculate payback: (price premium) / (litres saved per year × fuel price) = years to break even. If the diesel premium is large (>3–5k), payback can be many years. Given Getz prices are cheap (NSW median $7,490) you’d often be better off buying a cheap petrol manual unless you do very high km each year or the diesel example is similarly priced.
    - Also consider maintenance risk: older diesels can be more expensive to repair if injectors/HPFP fail; factor that into expected TCO.
    - Sources: AutoData fuel figures and Fuelly owner logs for realistic consumption [AutoData](https://www.auto-data.net/en/hyundai-getz-model-1483), [Fuelly](https://www.fuelly.com/car/hyundai/getz).
  - TCO summary advice:
    - If you do <15,000 km/year, a well‑maintained 1.4 petrol manual is probably the cheapest total cost route.
    - If you do >20,000–25,000 km/year and find a clean 1.5 CRDi at a small premium with service history, diesel may pay back over a few years — but only if HPFP/injector history looks healthy.

- Buy / not‑buy recommendation and optimal sweet spot
  - Buy if:
    - You need a cheap, nimble city hatch with low purchase outlay and low parts cost.
    - You find a 2006–2010 TB‑FL facelift car with documented service history (esp. cambelt history), tidy interior, no ABS/ESP warning lights and a manual gearbox. The TB‑FL facelift (post‑2005) is preferable for slightly fresher styling and minor kit improvements [Wikipedia / Parkers facelift notes](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyundai_Getz).  
  - Avoid if:
    - The car has no cambelt documentation (especially the 1.6 engine) — treat as a red flag.
    - The automatic shows slipping, harsh cold shifting or has a tranny light on — get a pre‑purchase trans diagnostic.
    - The body has extensive clearcoat peeling or rust; a respray can cost more than the car.
    - There are persistent electronic faults (ABS/ESP lights, immobiliser issues) without resolved repair history.
  - Specific sweet spot trim / year
    - Aim for a 2006–2010 TB‑FL with:
      - 1.4 petrol manual for the best balance of cost, economy (~7.0 L/100 km) and low repair risk. Confirm timing‑belt history.
      - If a diesel is priced the same and comes with a full fuel‑system service history, consider that for high annual kms — otherwise skip the diesel premium.
    - NSW snapshot shows 2009–2010 examples around $7k–$8k — that’s the market to aim at if you want minimal immediate spend and about 80k on the clock (median in your dataset 80,061 km).
    - Negotiation targets:
      - If the vehicle matches the “sweet spot” criteria and the dealer asks $7,490 median — try to negotiate toward the low‑end listing ($6,990) unless service history justifies the premium.

Final reality check (peer to peer)
- If you want a cheap, small, economical urban runner that’s simple, parts‑cheap and easy to park — the Getz is still a very solid choice in 2026. If you want modern safety tech, low NVH or long high‑speed motorway comfort, move up the used market to i20 / Mazda2 / Toyota Yaris territory.
- Caveats: insist on cambelt receipts (especially the 1.6), a transmission health check on automatics, and a full VIN/recall check before you sign. Use the NSW prices above as a negotiation anchor.
