#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation, timeframe & codes:
  - The Impreza sitting in Australian used‑car yards today mostly comes from Subaru’s fifth‑generation family (launched 2016 onward; the Impreza was the first model to ride on the Subaru Global Platform). See the platform overview here: [Subaru Global Platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Global_Platform).
- Product hierarchy:
  - Where it sits: the Impreza is Subaru’s compact car — below the Forester / Outback SUVs and beneath performance WRX / STI variants in the range. Subaru pitches the Impreza as the AWD compact hatch/sedan alternative to mainstream small cars.
- Shared platform and parts availability:
  - Platform: Subaru Global Platform (SGP). That means the Impreza shares basic structures and many suspension, brake and body‑mount parts with later Forester, XV/Crosstrek and other Subaru models built on SGP — handy for parts availability and independent‑shop repairs. See SGP details: [Subaru Global Platform (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Global_Platform).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions & drive layouts (what you’ll actually see in NSW yards)
  - Engines (broad families seen on Australian Impreza listings across 2012–2021): horizontally‑opposed Subaru boxer fours from the EJ → FB families, plus FA‑family motors in hot variants (WRX/STI are separate). Typical displacements in the mainstream Impreza line: 1.5–2.5L range (most common in Australia: 2.0L FB20 and earlier EJ25/2.5 in some older examples). See the engine families overview: [List of Subaru engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Subaru_engines).
  - Transmissions: 5‑ or 6‑speed manuals on older/performance versions, conventional automatics on some older units and Subaru’s Lineartronic CVT on the majority of mainstream 2012‑2021 Impreza models. Aggregated transmission data: [Cars‑Data transmission list](https://www.cars-data.com/en/subaru/impreza/spec/transmission).
  - Drivetrain: Symmetrical All‑Wheel Drive is standard on modern Imprezas — that’s one of the car’s unique selling points compared to front‑drive rivals.
- Marketplace rivals
  - Main competitors in the compact segment: Honda Civic, Toyota Corolla, Mazda3 and small crossovers such as the Nissan Kicks or Subaru’s own XV/Crosstrek (for buyers who want higher ride height). Comparisons: [KBB Impreza vs Civic](https://www.kbb.com/comparison/subaru-impreza-vs-honda-civic/) and [CarConnection competitor list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/subaru_impreza-sedan_choices).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Subaru Impreza?
Exterior
- Panel fit & trim:
  - Owners report occasional panel gaps (rear trunk/boot lips and rear door gaps have been discussed in owner forums for 2017+ cars). These aren’t common structural failures but can be annoying for water intrusion and rattles; many fixes are trim adjustments or clip replacements — check the rear trunk area for gaps. See a forum example: [rear trunk panel gap (Impreza5)](https://www.impreza5.com/threads/gap-in-rear-trunk-panel.14187/).
- Paint & clearcoat:
  - Subaru owners have reported clearcoat peeling/fading and minor paint durability complaints at a non‑zero rate — the trend crops up enough in owner‑surveys to merit inspection of the car’s upper surfaces and door leading edges for UV/clearcoat damage. A good owner write‑up on Subaru paint issues: [EngineCrux paint problem summary](https://enginecrux.com/understanding-subaru-paint-problems-and-solutions/).
- Trim vulnerabilities:
  - Gloss black plastics and thin interior claddings can scratch or rattle with heavy use — check rear cargo side‑panels and luggage area trims.
- ADAS (EyeSight) and safety system wrinkles:
  - EyeSight (camera‑based ADAS) is a headline safety feature on many Imprezas, but there have been reliability and functionality complaints (phantom/abrupt braking, lane‑keep misbehaviour). These issues were serious enough to be included in a class action settlement and law‑firm advisories — owners should verify open campaigns and operation. If EyeSight warnings persist, the common culprits are windshield damage, camera misalignment or sensor wiring. See:
    - Official EyeSight overview: [Subaru EyeSight](https://www.subaru.com/eyesight.html)
    - Coverage of litigation and complaints: [EyeSight settlement page](https://www.eyesightsettlement.com/) and [CarComplaints summary](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2026/subaru-eyesight-lawsuit.shtml).
  - Practical check: start the car and test adaptive cruise, lane‑keep and AEB in a safe, low‑speed environment — don’t rely on a dealership demo. If there are EyeSight‑disabled codes like P2583, dealers will flag them: [P2583 EyeSight Disabled write‑up](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p2583-subaru-impreza-2017-2024).
Interior
- Durability for rideshare / heavy use:
  - The Impreza interior is build‑quality decent but not premium. Seat fabric and trim can show wear on high‑kilometre cars used for rideshare. Low‑sill heights make ingress/egress easy for passengers, but plastic scuffs on door sills and rear cargo panels are common.
- Ergonomics & entry/exit:
  - Door sills and seats are positioned for easy entry — an advantage if you ferry kids or elderly passengers. Check door check angles (doors should open and hold cleanly without worn detents).
- Electrical behaviours:
  - Infotainment freezing/reboots and frozen displays have been documented across multiple model years; Subaru issued technical bulletins and dealers have software updates or unit replacements for affected cars. Relevant bulletins and troubleshooting are public: [TSB 15‑228‑18R — display freeze/blank](https://www.tsbsearch.com/Subaru/15-228-18) and the NHTSA service bulletin on repro steps: [NHTSA service bulletin PDF](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2022/MC-10209915-0001.pdf).
  - Wireless charging modules (on some late‑model Imprezas) have intermittent behaviour and heating complaints — there are diagnostic procedures and accessory guides that dealers follow: [Wireless charger diagnostic note](https://dot.report/bulletins/11027088).
- 12V drain & accessory quirks:
  - Expect the normal 12V modules and convenience electronics to show age‑related faults on higher‑km cars (parasitic draws from aftermarket gear are common). If the car has had accessories installed, get a bench test of the 12V system before purchase.
Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation — practical summary:
  - Subaru’s engineering focus is ruggedness, AWD traction and low centre of gravity via boxer engines. The Global Platform improved rigidity and crash dynamics since 2016 models; the downside is more complexity in ADAS and CVT electronics in newer cars. See SGP benefits: [Subaru Global Platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Global_Platform).
- Security vulnerabilities:
  - Like many modern cars, the Impreza isn’t immune to “CAN injection” theft techniques where thieves access wiring (often around lights) and inject messages on the vehicle bus to spoof key validation. This technique has been reported across other brands and is a class of attack to be aware of with late‑model cars. Sources on CAN injection: [Autoblog report on headlight CAN injection](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update/) and the deep‑tech writeup by Dr Ken Tindell: [CAN injection — technical note](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/).
- Aftermarket anti‑theft measures (practical, cheap vs expensive):
  - Don’t rely on the stock alarm alone in high‑theft areas. Recommended layered defences:
    - Visible physical deterrent (Club style steering wheel lock).
    - ECU immobiliser or kill‑switch that interrupts fuel pump relay.
    - Hardwired alarm with tilt/shock sensors and battery backup.
    - Park the car in a locked garage or on CCTV‑visible driveway when possible.
  - For high‑value older Imprezas with aftermarket smart‑keys, get the immobiliser code checked at a Subaru dealer or reputable locksmith: basic but effective.
Engines
- Notes on engine families you’ll encounter in Australian Imprezas: below are italicised subsections for each engine code/family that appeared in our research and the common failure patterns you should look for. Service interval guidance: Subaru owners in Australia commonly follow 10,000 km service cycles for oil/inspection items (some reports and owner habits drift to 15,000 km on longer‑life oil — if you want longevity, stick to 10,000 km oil changes and follow Subaru’s service schedule in the owner’s manual).
  - *EJ25* (older naturally aspirated 2.5L EJ family)
    - Failure points:
      - Head gasket leaks and external coolant/oil weeps are a well‑documented issue on EJ25s in Subaru’s history. Symptoms: white exhaust smoke, coolant loss, oil in coolant, or overheating. A deep tech primer: [EJ25 head gasket problems (UnderhoodService)](https://underhoodservice.com/subaru-head-gaskets/).
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Keep coolant system healthy, watch for oil/coolant cross‑contamination, and have cylinder‑head leakdown/compression checked if buying a high‑km EJ car.
    - Service interval:
      - Oil every 10,000 km (prefer shorter if the car has oil‑consumption history); cooling system flush and inspection per Subaru schedule (10k–30k km checkpoints).
  - *FB20 / FB25* (the FB series 2.0L / 2.5L boxer engines that replaced EJ in many mainstream Imprezas)
    - Failure points:
      - Early FB engines had owner‑reported oil consumption problems — bearings and piston ring sealing were implicated in some VIN ranges; search data shows owner complaint aggregators and recall/TSB action on certain cars: [FB oil‑consumption owner complaints summary](https://problemsbyvin.com/engines/subaru-fb-oil-consumption/) and [CherishYourCar coverage](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/subaru-oil-consumption-recall/).
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Monitor oil level between services; perform an oil consumption test (measure oil use over 1,000–2,000 km) before buying. Keep service to 10,000 km oil changes using the correct grade and quality oil.
    - Service interval:
      - 10,000 km oil/inspection is recommended; valve cover gaskets and PCV system checks every 40–60k km.
  - *FA20DIT* (turbo FA20 used in WRX and other hot Subarus — not the mainstream Impreza unless you’re looking at WRX)
    - Failure points:
      - Turbocharged direct‑injection FA motors are susceptible to intake valve carbon build‑up (direct injection), and higher‑load engine bearings/connect‑rod stress on tuned cars. Sources summarise FA20 owner problems: [FA20 problem overview (8020automotive)](https://8020automotive.com/the-4-most-common-subaru-fa20-engine-problems/).
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Regular oil service (10,000 km or less) with high‑quality oil, inlet cleaning if symptoms of intake restriction appear, and avoid aggressive tuning without engine‑upgrades.
    - Service interval:
      - 10,000 km oil changes; more frequent inspection if the car’s been modified or used hard.
Transmission
- CVT — Subaru Lineartronic (the common modern choice)
  - Known behaviour:
    - The Lineartronic CVT is smooth in normal driving but can exhibit low‑speed shudder, delayed engagement, or whining. These symptoms are most frequently reported in models from 2010–2018 ranges but show up in later cars too if service history is poor. Aggregated owner complaints and mechanic writeups are widely available: [ProblemsByVin — Lineartronic complaints](https://problemsbyvin.com/transmissions/subaru-lineartronic-cvt/) and practical shop guidance on causes and fixes: [Gears Magazine CVT analysis](https://gearsmagazine.com/magazine/worth-another-look-subaru-lineartronic-failures-faults-and-fixes/).
  - Failure modes and costs:
    - Valve body solenoids, torque converter shudder/clutch wear, chain and pulley wear. Replacement or rebuilds can be costly — shop quotes vary widely; insist on a full transmission scan and fluid service history. Subaru extended some CVT coverage in North America via a warranty bulletin: [NHTSA bulletin — extended CVT warranty note (example)](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2025/MC-11021247-0001.pdf).
  - Service recommendations:
    - CVT fluid changes at dealer intervals (use correct Subaru Lineartronic fluid), check for AT OIL TEMP warnings and any drivability shudder.
- Manuals & conventional automatics:
  - Manuals are generally robust but check clutch feel and gear engagement — these can be expensive to replace if abused. Conventional automatics are less common on post‑2016 Imprezas; check for delayed engagement if present.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Subaru Impreza?
- NSW dealer market snapshot (live data you provided)
  - Active NSW listings: 15
  - Price range: AUD $9,950 – $23,999
  - Median price: AUD $17,888
  - Year spread: 2012 – 2021
  - Median odometer: 70,973 km
  - Transmission mix (listings): Automatic 11, CVT 2, Manual 1
  - Use this as your negotiation anchor — the median $17.9k is what an average, mid‑life Impreza in NSW is trading for in dealer yards right now.
- Depreciation metrics (what you can expect)
  - Longer‑term depreciation: aggregated data from valuation sites suggest Impreza holds value a touch above segment average but not as well as Toyotas. Example metrics:
    - iSeeCars says ~29.1% depreciation over 5 years for Impreza in general sample sets: [iSeeCars Impreza resale & depreciation](https://www.iseecars.com/car/subaru/impreza/resale-value).
    - CarEdge estimates roughly 31% over five years: [CarEdge depreciation summary](https://caredge.com/subaru/impreza/depreciation).
  - Practical takeaway: buy one that’s already taken the big hit (3‑6 years old, 60k–120k km) — you avoid early depreciation and still get modern safety features.
- TCO calculation strategy (how to decide if it’s economical)
  - Step 1 — Base fuel consumption:
    - A modern 2.0L Impreza’s real world combined fuel use will typically sit around 7.5–9.0 L/100km depending on conditions; for a US EPA comparable the 2025 2.0L combined is ~30 mpg = 7.8 L/100km (EPA to L/100km conversion: 235.214 / mpg). Source (EPA/US spec for reference): [FuelEconomy — 2025 Impreza](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/Find.do?action=sbs&id=48507).
  - Step 2 — Annual fuel litres:
    - Example: at 15,000 km/year and 7.8 L/100km you’ll use ~1,170 L/year.
  - Step 3 — Annual fuel cost (example):
    - At AUD $1.80/L that’s ~AUD $2,106/year in fuel. (Swap in your local price to update).
  - Step 4 — Service, insurance & maintenance:
    - Expect routine service (~$300–$600 every 10k km at a non‑dealer shop for oil/inspection), brakes/tyres depending on wear. Factor a CVT risk premium if the car has an older CVT and incomplete service history.
  - Step 5 — Hybrids/EVs vs petrol Impreza:
    - Subaru’s mild‑hybrid e‑BOXER versions existed in some markets but are being wound down; overall, with suburban Australian driving (moderate km/year) the small fuel savings from mild‑hybrid Impreza historically have been incremental and often don’t cover the higher purchase premium before many years — compare lifetime fuel cost, servicing differences and battery/complexity risk. For a quantitative decision, compare the petrol Impreza’s annual fuel bill above with the hybrid’s lower fuel bill, then amortise any purchase premium over your ownership term. (Edmunds has a US 5‑year TCO reference to illustrate categories: [Edmunds True Cost to Own example](https://www.edmunds.com/subaru/impreza/2025/cost-to-own/).)
- Risk adjustments & negotiation levers
  - If the car has CVT + no fluid/service history: demand a price cut or dealer‑backed warranty. CVT symptoms or Trace codes = red flag.
  - If EyeSight is non‑functional or showing warnings: insist on dealer diagnosis and proof of completed ADAS calibration (windshield replacement often requires recalibration). Use EyeSight Settlement resources to check whether your car’s year is in scope: [EyeSight settlement](https://www.eyesightsettlement.com/).
  - If engine is an EJ25 with high km: negotiate for compression tests and evidence the head gaskets haven’t been sealing issues.
- Final buying recommendation — what model year/trim to aim for
  - Best financial sweet spot (NSW market): hunt in the 2016–2019 model years — these capture the SGP benefits (stiffer chassis, improved NVH and safety) while having taken most steep depreciation. With the NSW listing range and median km (~71k km), expect to pay roughly AUD $13k–$19k for well‑spec’d 2016–2019 Premium or Sport hatchbacks in good nick.
  - Trim pick:
    - If you want simple reliability and value: a 2.0i Premium (or equivalent higher‑option grade) with full service history, recent timing/belt/fluids and a clean EyeSight operation is the pragmatic pick.
    - Avoid: Early‑EJ cars with unknown history, CVT cars without documented fluid changes, and any Impreza with persistent EyeSight faults or an unexplained infotainment blanking error.
  - Cash vs finance:
    - If buying from a dealer near the NSW median, use the median price ($17,888) as your target starting point for negotiation — recent market shows $9,950 at the low end (high‑km, older cars) and $23,999 at the top (low‑km late models). Use mechanical checks and service records to push the price down if mileage or CVT/ADAS issues exist.

Bottom line (short and blunt)
- The Impreza is a practical, AWD compact with a sensible chassis and good safety hardware — exactly the sort of car Aussies buy when they want grip in wet weather without the SUV bulk. Buy one with documented service (10,000 km oil changes), a clean EyeSight history and a CVT that has never shuddered — and you’ll have a dependable daily for the next 5–7 years. Skip the older EJ‑heavy, high‑km bargains unless you’re mechanically confident or price is your only driver.
