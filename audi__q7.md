#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation, production timeframe and codes
  - We're primarily talking about the second‑generation Audi Q7 (Typ 4M / 4M8; facelifted through to 2024/2025 and updated toward 2026/2027 markets). Audi’s public materials and spec pages frame the Q7 as a three‑row luxury SUV that’s evolved since the original 2005 launch — the 4M is the generation most of the NSW market listings (2013–2023 range in your snapshot) will be drawn from. See Audi’s global model pages and the model timeline for the generational changes. [Audi global Q7 page](https://www.audi.com/en/audi-q7-64) · [Wikipedia Q7 overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Audi_Q7)

- Product hierarchy (what sits above and below)
  - The Q7 sits above the Q5 in Audi’s SUV ladder (bigger, three‑row capability) and sits alongside the more style‑led Q8 (which trades some practical space for coupey presence). Think of Q7 as the family‑first Audi SUV, Q8 the image option, Q5 the smaller family car. (See Audi model positioning and third‑generation press context.) [Audi Q7 press release summary](https://www.audi.com/en/press-releases/third-generation-of-the-successful-suv-new-audi-q7-combines-versatility-and-performance-18004)

- Shared global platform architecture & parts availability
  - The Q7 uses the Volkswagen Group MLB platform (MLB/MLB Evo family) — the same basic underpinnings that support high‑end stablemates like the Porsche Cayenne, Bentley Bentayga, Lamborghini Urus and VW Touareg. This matters: components (suspension bits, brakes, some electrics) are shared across the VW‑Group luxury range, which helps parts availability — but also means some repairs are expensive because the parts are premium. [Wikipedia — MLB platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volkswagen_Group_MLB_platform)

- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive layouts (what you’ll see on Aussie classifieds)
  - Petrol

Recent V6s are around the ~250–340 kW/HP neighborhood depending on tune (e.g., 3.0 V6 ~335 hp reported in spec sheets). Most run Audi’s quattro AWD and **8‑speed Tiptronic** automatic. #### 2. What goes wrong with a used Audi Q7?

Exterior:
- Panel alignment & paint
  - Aftermarket collision repairs can leave inconsistent panel gaps and poor paint matching. Owner threads and collision‑repair reports flag disappointing repair quality — these big SUVs are expensive to repair correctly, and cheap panels show it. Check seams around doors, bonnet and tailgate carefully for colour/clear‑coat mismatch and uneven gaps. [AudiWorld collision repair thread summary](https://www.audiworld.com/forums/q7-mkii-discussion-211/collision-repair-disappointing-results-3058486/) · [Go‑Parts exterior trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-audi-q7-audi-q8-audi-sq7-2015-2026)
- Trim vulnerabilities
  - Plastic arch mouldings, gloss black trims and door sills can scratch, fade or loosen. Guides show common trim fatigue on 2015–2026 Q7s — a cosmetic pain but expensive if you want a concours finish. [Go‑Parts trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-audi-q7-audi-q8-audi-sq7-2015-2026)
- Paint durability
  - Clear‑coat delamination reports are scattered in owner forums and repair roundups. If you’re buying a car that’s been resprayed, insist on documented repairs and compare panels in direct sunlight.
- Recalls & safety systems
  - ADAS sensors (radar/camera) are a recurring headache. Forward radar and camera faults manifest as “ACC unavailable” or pre‑sense warnings. Some model years have TSBs and recalls; calibration and replacement of front sensors requires dealer tooling and can be pricey. Don’t buy a Q7 that flags ADAS faults and continues to report them — get the dealer VIN check and open‑recall history. [NHTSA model detail / recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle/2024/AUDI/Q7) · [Radar sensor failure guide (Go‑Parts)](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/video-camera-audi-q7-audi-q8-audi-a6-2017-2026) · [TSB on driver assist faults](https://carandproblems.com/bulletins/audi/q7/11016535-audi-q7-driver-assist-system-fault)

Interior:
- Durability under heavy use
  - Door panels and interior trim rattle or loosen, especially in high‑use environments (rideshare, school run). Guides call out door‑trim clips and inner panels as common sources of rattles. Check every trim join, cupholder and storage lid with a physical test drive and listen for wind/rattle at cruising speeds. [Door panel guide Q7/Q8](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/door-interior-trim-panel-audi-q7-audi-q8-audi-sq7-2017-2024)
- Entry/exit ergonomics
  - It’s a tall car but door sills are not punishing; step‑in is reasonable for a three‑row SUV. Check rear seat ingress/egress if you regularly carry kids/elderly — the third row in many Q7s is tight for adults.
- Electrical behaviours & infotainment
  - Infotainment hardware varied across years: earlier mid‑range cars may have smaller MMI screens and fewer features; Premium Plus/Prestige trims upgraded to the full Virtual Cockpit and bigger passenger displays in later years. Some models show 12V battery drain symptoms after aftermarket fitment or in cars with many electronic convenience features — if a car has recurring flat batteries, it usually points to parasitic draws, ageing 12V, or poor module sleep behaviour. Ask for battery replacement history and any aftermarket alarms or stereo work. [J.D. Power model history & trim shifts](https://www.jdpower.com/cars/history/audi/q7) · (owner reports aggregated in RepairIQ/OwnerKeep for electrical faults) [RepairIQ summary](https://www.repairiqhq.com/audi-q7-common-problems/)

Mechanically:
- Engineering reputation & general notes
  - The Q7 is well‑engineered but complex: air suspension, multilink rear, adaptive dampers and extensive electronics mean maintenance costs sit above mainstream SUVs. The brand’s engineering is solid — but complexity bites when wear or faults occur.
- Security vulnerabilities (CAN & keyless)
  - Modern Audis (VW‑group cars) are a target for CAN‑based intrusion attacks. Research shows criminals can access exposed wiring (headlight area, for example), inject CAN frames and impersonate immobiliser messages. That’s not an Audi‑only problem — it’s a platform architecture risk that affects many modern luxury cars. Practical mitigations: steering‑wheel locks, VIN‑tagged wheel bolts, secure parking, and consider an aftermarket CAN‑bus intrusion detection or a mechanical immobiliser if you live in a high‑theft postcode. [CAN injection technical overview](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/) · [media coverage of headlight CAN thefts](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update)
- High‑priority aftermarket anti‑theft fixes
  - Fit a visible mechanical lock (Club), park in secure or lit areas, use VA/insured immobiliser options, and ask a dealer to lock out keyless‑start features if they can or fit an aftermarket RFID/shield pouch for keys.

Engines:
(Each engine variant below is discussed as we found it in the research — italic headings for each variant.)

- *45/50 TFSI (2.0‑litre four‑cylinder petrol)*
  - Common notes: respectable economy and lower complexity than the V6. Potential issues centre on high‑pressure fuel pump wear and ignition/coils (typical for direct‑injection turbo petrols). Keep to regular oil and service intervals; misfires and check‑engine lights can come from coils and plugs. Source: aggregated owner/repair writeups. [MotorAndWheels Q7 issues](https://motorandwheels.com/audi-q7-problems-known-issues/) · [EnginePatrol common faults recap](https://enginepatrol.com/audi-q7-common-problems/)
  - Preventative schedule (practical Aussie guidance): oil & filter every 10,000 km (or annually), spark checks and coil inspection at ~60,000–80,000 km.

- *55 TFSI (3.0‑litre V6 petrol mild‑hybrid) — bold important component: **3.0 TFSI V6***  
  - Failure points: owner reports and spec pages note misfire and erratic check‑engine light histories in some V6s; high‑pressure fuel systems and ignition coils are common fail items. The mild‑hybrid system adds complexity (start/stop hardware, 48V bits) and increases diagnosis costs. [Edmunds 2026 spec reference](https://www.edmunds.com/audi/q7/2026/features-specs/) · [MotorAndWheels known issues](https://motorandwheels.com/audi-q7-problems-known-issues/)
  - Preventative schedule: oil & filter every 10,000 km; inspect high‑pressure fuel system and ignition components at 60k intervals or sooner if misfires appear. Follow dealer software updates for PCM/engine‑management; some misfire cases responded to PCM reflashes.

- *45/50/55 TDI (3.0‑litre diesel V6) — bold: **3.0 TDI***
  - Failure points: timing chain and tensioner wear is a documented issue on several Audi/VW V6 diesels (rattling at ~120k–150k km with risk of catastrophic failure if ignored). AdBlue system faults, DPF regeneration failures and turboactuator problems also appear on diesel Q7s. [AutoDoc diesel weak points](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-audi-q7) · [MyEngineSpecs Q7 engine problems summary](https://myenginespecs.com/audi/audi-q7-engine-problems-key-issues-and-solutions/)
  - Preventative schedule: oil & filter every 10,000 km (diesel), AdBlue checks and DPF diagnostics periodically, and timing chain inspection/review once you pass 120,000 km or at the first sign of a rattle. Factor in higher cost for diesel repairs and AdBlue/DPF components.

- *55 TFSI e (PHEV) — bold: **Q7 TFSI e 3.0 PHEV / 22 kWh battery***  
  - Failure points: PHEV items introduce battery cooling, inverter and battery management components to the mix. Pack cooling or HV issues are uncommon but expensive; real‑world electric range depends heavily on charging habits — quoted WLTP ~85–90 km is optimistic in real mixed driving. The PHEV’s complexity raises long‑term maintenance considerations around battery health and replacement risk. [Audi Q7 TFSI e official page](https://www.audi.com/en/audi-q7-tfsi-e-quattro-until-2026-15963) · [Audi media data](https://www.audi-mediacenter.com/en/q7-suv-tfsi-e-899/technical-data)
  - Preventative schedule: follow Audi dealer HV system checks at scheduled intervals, keep the battery charged for regular conditioning, and perform HV system diagnostics if you see energy loss or charging faults. Oil service at 10,000 km still applies for the ICE side.

Transmission:
- **ZF 8HP Tiptronic (8‑speed torque‑converter automatic)** — bold: **ZF 8HP**
  - Behaviour & known failure modes: generally smooth and efficient when serviced, but the Q7 community reports torque‑converter shudder, hard shifts and mechatronic/solenoid pack faults in some model years. Many fixes start with software reflashes; some cases need valve body, mechatronic or torque converter replacement — all of which are costly. Fluid and filter change history matters a lot — no service history is a red flag. [CarComplaints Q7 transmission page](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Audi/Q7/transmission/) · [ZF aftermarket info](https://www.zf.com/global/en/vehicle-service/aftermarket.html) · [Edmunds owner threads on shift quality](https://www.edmunds.com/audi/q7/owner-reviews/)
  - Practical checks before purchase:
    - Do a long test drive covering multiple shift points (stop/start, freeway kick‑down, motorway cruising). Feel for shudder, hesitation or hard 1→2/2→3 shifts.
    - Get a dealer transmission‑health scan; ask whether the car had the known TSBs or software updates applied.
    - Demand service receipts for transmission fluid/filter changes — ideal to see a 60,000 km-ish fluid change on older cars. If none, budget for fluid and filter service immediately.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Audi Q7?

- NSW dealer market snapshot (your live data)
  - Active dealer listings: 5
  - Price range: AUD $11,990 – $89,950
  - Median price: AUD $39,990
  - Year range: 2013 – 2023
  - Median odometer: 68,463 km
  - Transmission mix: Automatic: 4, Sports Automatic: 1
  - Use that median and the top‑end spread to anchor expectations — there’s everything from cheap project Q7s through to near‑new flagship examples.

- Depreciation & resale behaviour
  - Audi Q7s are notorious for steep depreciation. Public data aggregators put five‑year depreciation in the mid‑50s per cent (~57% over five years), worse than the luxury midsize segment average. That’s the double‑edged sword: you can buy bargains, but resale value can be poor versus some rivals. [iSeeCars depreciation summary](https://www.iseecars.com/car/audi-q7/resale-value) · [CarEdge depreciation comparisons](https://caredge.com/audi/q7/depreciation)
  - Practical read: If you want the cheapest monthly payment, buy used; if you want to keep it and run it for 7–10 years, accept the depreciation and focus on mechanical condition.

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Components you must budget for (per annum, Австраlia experience):
    - Servicing (Audi dealer or specialist): expect premiums vs non‑luxury SUVs — factor $700–$1,200 per scheduled service at dealer rates depending on items and region.
    - Tyres & brake pads: big SUV, big tyres — don’t assume cheap Michelin alternatives are fitted.
    - Repairs: air suspension, DPF, turbocharger, timing chain work and transmission repairs are expensive and occur on higher‑mileage cars.
    - Insurance: luxury‑class premiums.
  - PHEV vs petrol TCO: the PHEV (55 TFSI e) can be brilliant if you:
    - Can charge daily, do the majority of trips on electric range, and have low‑cost electricity.
    - If you drive lots of long highway trips without charging, the PHEV becomes heavier and thirstier than petrol equivalents.
    - Use Audi’s quoted battery capacity (22 kWh usable) and WLTP claims as starting points, then model your own city/highway mix. For a worked‑example primer, see Audi’s PHEV technical data and Edmunds cost‑to‑own breakdowns. [Audi PHEV data](https://www.audi.com/en/audi-q7-tfsi-e-quattro-until-2026-15963) · [Edmunds 2025 True Cost to Own](https://www.edmunds.com/audi/q7/2025/cost-to-own/)
  - Practical TCO takeaway:
    - If you can reliably charge at home/work and do <50 km/day commute cycles, the PHEV can cut fuel costs and city running dramatically.
    - If you can’t charge regularly, a well‑serviced petrol V6 on a cheaper purchase price will likely cost less over five years than a PHEV that never sees meaningful electric miles.

- Depreciation metrics and a buy/sell strategy
  - Expect around ~50–60% depreciation over 5 years from new on average — which makes late‑model used Q7s very tempting as “almost new” tech at steep discounts. Use that to buy the nicest example you can afford and plan to keep 5–7 years to amortise the purchase and large fixed costs.

- The final buy recommendation (clear and blunt)
  - If you want the Q7 and you want value: aim for a facelifted 2017–2020 Q7 with comprehensive service history and under ~120,000 km. Those years took on key interior tech, and by 2017 Audi had ironed out some of the earlier platform teething problems. [J.D. Power model history & trim migration](https://www.jdpower.com/cars/history/audi/q7) · [CarBuzz generational timeline](https://carbuzz.com/cars/audi/q7/generations/)
  - If your budget is tight: the low‑price AUD $11,990 end of the NSW snapshot likely represents a project car (high km, unknown history, possible mechanical/electrical faults). Walk away unless you’ve got mechanical backup and a solid inspection.
  - If you want tech and safety that reliably works: prefer higher‑trim cars where ADAS modules were updated and where dealers can demonstrate TSBs/recalls have been applied. Avoid cars that show persistent ADAS warnings on the dash (adaptive cruise, pre‑sense) unless the dealer provides proof of repair/calibration. [Go‑Parts radar sensor guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/radar-sensor-audi-a4-audi-q7-audi-rs5-2017-2024) · [TSB reference](https://carandproblems.com/bulletins/audi/q7/11016535-audi-q7-driver-assist-system-fault)
  - Best financial sweet spot: a 2017–2019 Premium Plus / Prestige grade with 60k–120k km and full service history. These usually balance tech, comfort and a price that hasn’t completely bottomed out — you avoid older timing‑chain risk in diesel V6s by favouring well‑maintained petrol V6s, and you get nicer interior fitment and updated software.

Practical pre‑purchase checklist (your must‑have inspections)
- Full dealer VIN/recall check (show me the stamped service booklet and completed TSBs).
- Transmission health test (long test drive, no shudder, dealer scan).
- ADAS check — clear history and no persistent camera/radar faults.
- Engine health: compression/misfire diagnostic if check‑engine is intermittent; listen for timing chain rattle (diesels).
- Confirm PHEV battery health and charging records if you’re buying a TFSI e.
- Confirm recent cam/timing/chain service history on higher‑km diesels.

Bottom line: The Q7 is an absolute peach of an Audi when it’s been loved — exceptional space, comfort and presence. But used Q7s demand respect: steep depreciation makes them bargain‑priced, but the flip side is expensive mechanical/electrical bills if you buy the wrong car. Do the homework, pay for a thorough pre‑purchase inspection, prioritise documented service history and prefer the 2017–2019 facelifted cars for the best balance of features, fewer surprise bills and sensible value.
