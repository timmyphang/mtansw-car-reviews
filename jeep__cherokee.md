#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation covered: sixth‑generation Cherokee lineage overview (the KL-era compact crossover sold 2014–2023 in most markets, with the model code often referred to as **KL**; Jeep restarted Cherokee production with a new KM generation for 2026). Production timeframe you’ll see on the NSW market snapshot is predominately 2014–2020 examples. See Jeep’s model pages and history for the generational split. ([Jeep Cherokee specs](https://www.jeep.com/cherokee/specs.cherokee.html), [Wikipedia Cherokee KL & KM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeep_Cherokee), [CarBuzz generations](https://carbuzz.com/cars/jeep/cherokee/generations/))

- Product hierarchy: the Cherokee sits between the smaller Compass and the larger Grand Cherokee in Jeep’s lineup — think: Compass = entry, Cherokee = mainstream compact/midsize crossover, Grand Cherokee = premium/larger family SUV. Jeep markets the reborn 2026 Cherokee upmarket of the Compass but below Grand Cherokee. ([Jeep official model pages](https://www.jeep.com/cherokee.html), [CNBC preview](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/05/29/jeep-cherokee-suv-hybrid.html))

- Shared platform and parts footprint: the KL-era Cherokee shares common Stellantis/Fiat‑Chrysler mechanicals and electronics with other FCA midsize crossovers—engines like the **2.4L Tigershark** and Pentastar V6s, and the ZF 9‑speed automatic were used across many models. The newer 2026 KM uses STLA-derived transverse architecture. Shared parts across multiple Stellantis models helps parts availability — but it’s a double‑edged sword if a design has an endemic fault. (See platform histories and engine listings: [Platform history overview](https://theoffroading.com/what-platform-is-the-jeep-cherokee-built-on/), [AutoFiles engine list](https://autofiles.com/engine/jeep/cherokee/))

- Market-specific drivetrain mix (what you’ll find in Australia, especially in used stock):
  - Petrol I4: **2.4L Tigershark (MultiAir II)** — common on 2014–2020 local imports.
  - Petrol V6: **3.2/3.6 Pentastar V6** options on higher trims or earlier years.
  - Turbocharged petrols: later years and international markets saw 2.0L turbo variants; the 2026 return is hybrid‑centric with a 1.6L turbo hybrid and eCVT/e‑CVT style transmission.
  - Transmission: mostly automatic in the NSW snapshot (all listed units were automatic).
  - Drive configurations: FWD and AWD/4x4 variants existed; the 2026 KM hybrid pushes AWD as standard on some markets. ([AutoFiles engines](https://autofiles.com/engine/jeep/cherokee/), [Jeep 2026 specs](https://www.jeep.com/cherokee/specs.cherokee.html))

- Typical rivals in Australia: Toyota RAV4, Honda CR‑V, Mazda CX‑5, Hyundai Tucson and Subaru Forester — the Cherokee competes on size and capability but not always on reliability or resale. Comparison pieces put Cherokee against these segment benchmarks. ([Edmunds comparison](https://www.edmunds.com/car-news/toyota-rav4-honda-crv-jeep-cherokee-hybrid-spec-comparison-feature.html), [The Car Connection competitors list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/jeep_cherokee_choices))

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Jeep Cherokee?
Here’s where the sales pitch stops and the real talk starts. I break down common failure modes and quirks you’ll actually encounter in the Australian used market.

Exterior
- Panel fit and trim: the KL Cherokee is notorious for loose fender flares, peeling B‑pillar trim and UV‑faded appliqués. Owners report broken plastic clips and mismatching trim across facelift years (2019 facelift changed many parts; not always interchangeable). Expect to spend for clip kits or replacement panels if the previous owner was careless. ([Go‑Parts exterior trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-jeep-cherokee-2014-2023), [Go‑Parts center pillar trim](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/center-pillar-jeep-cherokee-2014-2023))
- Paint durability and corrosion: owner complaint databases flag paint and water‑ingress reports concentrated in early KL years (2014–2016). Scraped rock chips and bubbling aren’t common like a classic rusted ute, but look closely around wheelarches and lower doors on older cars. ([CarComplaints body/paint problems](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Jeep/Cherokee/body_paint/index.shtml))
- Body recalls: Jeep/Fiat recall history for Cherokee is significant enough that you should check VINs for outstanding campaigns (Mopar VIN recall lookup recommended). Also look for large campaigns affecting powertrain/drive units. ([Mopar recall lookup](https://www.mopar.com/jeep/en-us/my-vehicle/recalls/search.html), [CBS on PTU recall](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/jeep-cherokee-recall-ptu-failure-stellantis-2025/))
- ADAS/sensors: forward camera and radar faults are common after collisions or even just from sensor contamination. Recalibration post‑repair is necessary and can be expensive; owners report phantom braking from ACC/AEBS. TSBs show ADAS warnings after battery servicing and advise recalibration. ([Go‑Parts ADAS forward camera faults](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/video-camera-jeep-cherokee-2014-2023), [TSB S2408000086 listing](https://www.tsbsearch.com/Jeep/S2408000086), [CarProblemZoo AEBS complaints](https://www.carproblemzoo.com/jeep/cherokee/automatic-emergency-braking-problems.php))

Interior
- Durability: dash delamination and leather/wrap wrinkle issues are reported on Jeep models — Grand Cherokee threads are messy and owners of KLs report similar soft‑touch failures on long hot Aussie summers. If the car has been used for rideshare or commercial work, check seat wear, scuffs on sill plates and repeated USB port failures. ([Forum threads on dash delamination](https://www.jeepforum.com/threads/dashboard-delamination.4499914/))
- Entry/egress: door sills are mid‑height — not as high as a ladder frame 4x4 but higher than a typical hatch. Door check angles are generous; older cars sometimes have worn door check stops — that’s a cheap fix.
- Electricals and infotainment: Uconnect screens on many Stellantis vehicles suffer freezing, blank screens and reboot loops. Dealers have issued software updates/TSBs on related models; some fixes are software, others require control unit replacement. Expect intermittent USB/Bluetooth dropouts on tired systems. ([BuyMoparWarranty on screen freezing and TSBs](https://www.buymoparwarranty.com/blog/jeep-grand-cherokee-screen-freezing-and-blackouts-what-tsbs-reveal/), [dot.report TSB listing](https://dot.report/bulletins/11021823))
- 12V battery drains: owners report battery drain after accessories and aftermarket audio installs; given ADAS and telematics draws, check the battery health and ask for a recent battery test. If the vehicle has had repeated jump starts, that’s a red flag for latent electrical gremlins.

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation: Jeep’s household reliability score is mixed. The Cherokee/KL has a higher-than-average complaint rate for engine, transmission and electrical items in owner‑report databases. It’s not a death sentence — many last long with sensible care — but plan for higher maintenance than a Japanese compact SUV. ([CarComplaints aggregates](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Jeep/Cherokee/), [AutoReliabilityIndex](https://autoreliabilityindex.com/jeep/cherokee/2024))
- Security vulnerabilities: the Cherokee family has been the poster child for vehicle cybersecurity risks — remember the 2015 Uconnect remote access exploit that allowed researchers to shut down a moving Cherokee? That hack prompted recalls and software patches, but it’s a stark reminder that older connected head units can be an attack vector. Also be aware of CAN‑injection theft techniques used by organised thieves to bypass immobilisers on many modern cars. If you buy a KL Cherokee, assume the head unit needs to be patched and physical anti‑theft measures are sensible. ([Wired deep dive on the 2015 hack](https://www.rogerford.org/privacy21f/wired-hackers.pdf), [Kaspersky explanation](https://www.kaspersky.com/blog/blackhat-jeep-cherokee-hack-explained/9493/), [CAN injection explainer](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/))

- Recommended aftermarket counter‑measures:
  - Fit a quality mechanical immobiliser or steering wheel lock (cheap and visible theft deterrent).
  - Get the latest dealer firmware applied to Uconnect and TCU/TCM where available.
  - Fit a VIN‑tagged kill‑switch on a fuel pump relay or an inertial‑type shock sensor tied to an alarm that trips when wiring is physically tampered.
  - Park smart: inside garage or well‑lit area; wheel‑lock/locks in high risk suburbs.

Engines
(italicised subsection for each engine variant mentioned in the search results — short, precise failure points and recommended service cadence)

- *2.4L Tigershark (MultiAir II)*  
  - Known issues: excessive oil consumption, valve/valvetrain and cylinder‑head related faults, and leaks. Owners report oil‑burning that can lead to premature engine rebuild if not monitored. The MultiAir variable valve system can be sensitive to poor oil maintenance. ([MotorReviewer on Tigershark problems](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=184), [SlashGear/MultiAir coverage](https://slashgear.com))  
  - Failure points to watch: oil consumption, cam/chain tensioner wear, head gasket or coolant leaks in higher‑mileage units.  
  - Preventative schedule & checks: treat like a modern petrol — oil every 10,000 km at minimum (or sooner if using long idling/urban driving), regular oil‑level checks between services, and a compression/leakdown check on high‑km examples. If buying a car >120,000 km, insist on service records proving consistent oil top‑ups and no recent oil‑consumption complaints.

- *2.0L GME‑T4 / “Hurricane” 2.0 turbo*  
  - Known issues: turbo charging system stresses and high‑pressure fuel system sensitivity in some FCA engines. Not as common in Aussie KLs but shows up in related models. ([AutoFiles engine list](https://autofiles.com/engine/jeep/cherokee/))  
  - Watch for: turbo oil feed restrictions, intercooler oil contamination, and boost‑related misfires. Check for smoke under hard acceleration and boost pressure consistency.

- *3.2L / 3.6L Pentastar V6*  
  - Known issues: generally robust compared with Tigershark but watch for higher fuel and servicing costs, and coolant/thermostat failures in some years. Pentastar is a fundamentally well‑regarded engine within the Stellantis range. ([AutoFiles engines](https://autofiles.com/engine/jeep/cherokee/), [What‑Breaks guide](https://what-breaks.com/jeep/cherokee-kl))  
  - Maintain: oil changes every 10,000–15,000 km depending on oil spec and driving, and follow coolant service intervals.

- *1.6L turbo hybrid (2026 KM)*  
  - New arrival: the KM revived Cherokee is hybrid‑first with an eCVT/e‑CVT and electric assist; not yet relevant to 2014–2020 used stock but important if you’re planning longer ownership or want the hybrid premium. The hybrid promises around 37 mpg (≈6.4 L/100km) in US testing. ([Jeep 2026 specs](https://www.jeep.com/cherokee/specs.cherokee.html), [Autoblog first drive](https://www.autoblog.com/reviews/2026-jeep-cherokee-hybrid-review))

Transmission
- ZF 9‑speed automatic (9HP): the KL Cherokee used the ZF 9‑speed in many combinations and it’s the single biggest mechanical headline for buyers. Symptoms commonly reported: shuddering, delayed or harsh shifts, torque converter clutch fault codes **P0740 / P0741**, and in some cars valve‑body or TCM software corrections needed. Repairs can be expensive (valve body or torque converter replacement) and some failures are software or TCM calibration related — dealers sometimes fix by reflash, other times by heavy mechanical work. ([CherishYourCar on 9‑speed failures](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/jeep-cherokee-transmission-problems/), [Go‑Parts P0741/P0740 articles](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0741-jeep-cherokee-2014-2023), [YouCanic transmission troubleshooting](https://www.youcanic.com/jeep-transmission-problems/))

- Practical checks: during a test drive, watch for:
  - Shuddering under light throttle or low‑speed cruise (classic TCC failure sign).
  - High, hunting RPM at highway cruise — could be torque converter slipping.
  - Check transmission fluid condition and service history (some workshop guides recommend earlier fluid changes for these boxes if used hard). If in doubt, get a professional transmission diagnostic with live TCM data.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Jeep Cherokee?
This is the money talk. I use the NSW dealer snapshot plus reliability context to give you the real answer.

- NSW dealer market snapshot (live): Active listings: 6. Price range: AUD $9,990 – $24,888. Median price: AUD $11,990. Year range: 2014 – 2020. Median odometer: 67,017 km. Transmission mix: Automatic only in the scrape. (MTA NSW scraper snapshot supplied in your brief)

- Typical purchase ceiling and entry: expect to pay roughly:
  - Entry level (high‑km, base trims, cosmetic issues): from around AUD $9,500–$12,500.
  - Reasonable family spec (mid‑trim, 60–120k km, tidy): AUD $12,500–$18,000.
  - Low km or better spec (limited, leather, V6, or near‑service history): closer to AUD $18–25k — which matches your top NSW listing. Use the NSW snapshot as your negotiation starting point.

- Depreciation and resale: Jeep Cherokees have historically weaker resale than Japanese rivals. iSeeCars notes about a 49.5% five‑year depreciation for Cherokees — worse than segment avg — and JeepInside/AutoPadre show Cherokees lose more value than RAV4/CR‑V in five years. Expect steeper early depreciation but deeper discounts on older stock. ([iSeeCars depreciation](https://www.iseecars.com/car/jeep-cherokee/resale-value), [JeepInside depreciation commentary](https://jeepinside.com/do-jeep-cherokees-hold-their-value/))

- TCO calculation strategy (how to think about total cost):
  1. Start with purchase price + immediate maintenance: assume a “safety spend” of AUD $1,000–$3,000 on older KLs to bring them up to date (timing-related services, tyres, brakes, battery, potential software updates).
  2. Add scheduled servicing: budget higher than a Corolla — plan AUD $600–$1,000 per year for dealer‑level servicing and higher per‑repair costs if engine/transmission work is needed.
  3. Fuel: calculate using realistic combined consumption for the engine variant (Tigershark 2.4 commonly returns higher fuel numbers than hybrid; the new 1.6 hybrid promises ~37 mpg ≈ 6.4 L/100km). Use your annual km to estimate fuel spend. ([JeepInside gas ratings summary](https://jeepinside.com/does-jeep-cherokee-have-a-hybrid/), [Autoblog on hybrid consumption](https://www.autoblog.com/reviews/2026-jeep-cherokee-hybrid-review))
  4. Insurance and registration: budget slightly higher if you live in an urban theft hotspot because Cherokees have seen targeted theft/attack vectors historically. Factor in a mechanical warranty or roadside cover if you can get it.
  5. Unexpected repairs: transmission or engine repair can be a four‑figure hit; set aside an emergency fund (AUD $3–5k) if buying privately without warranty.

- Hybrid vs petrol: does paying a premium for hybrid make sense?
  - The 2026 KM hybrid claims around 37 mpg (≈6.4 L/100km) per first drives and Jeep specs. Older petrol Cherokees, depending on engine and driving, are commonly in the 9–11 L/100km neighbourhood on mixed driving (owner median figures vary). ([Jeep 2026 specs](https://www.jeep.com/cherokee/specs.cherokee.html), [Autoblog fuel numbers](https://www.autoblog.com/reviews/2026-jeep-cherokee-hybrid-review), [JeepInside fuel ranges](https://jeepinside.com/does-jeep-cherokee-have-a-hybrid/))
  - Example (quick back‑of‑envelope): at 15,000 km/year:
    - Hybrid @ 6.4 L/100km = 960 L/year. If petrol = AUD $1.80/L → fuel = AUD $1,728/yr.
    - Petrol @ 10.0 L/100km = 1,500 L/year → fuel = AUD $2,700/yr.
    - Annual saving ~ AUD $972. If the hybrid premium is AUD $5,000 at purchase, fuel payback ~5+ years — and you still have to consider battery warranty, hybrid service costs and potential higher initial insurance. (Use your own fuel price and km to fine tune — methodology based on fuel economy comparisons and AFDC/Fueleconomy-style calculators for lifecycle cost comparisons. [US AFDC calculator](https://afdc.energy.gov/calc/), [FuelEconomy.gov comparison methodologies](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/hybridCompare.jsp))
  - Bottom line: hybrid can be worth it for high annual km drivers (commuters, country runs) — less so if you do low annual km and plan to flip the car in 2–3 years, because the purchase premium won’t be recouped by fuel savings alone. Also consider battery/complexity risk with a model new to market.

- Depreciation metrics you should expect:
  - Expect a larger drop versus Japanese rivals: Cherokees often lose ~45–55% in five years by multiple valuation sources. That’s why good buys are often low‑km, well‑serviced 2017–2019 examples that have already taken the depreciation hit. ([iSeeCars](https://www.iseecars.com/car/jeep-cherokee/resale-value), [JeepInside depreciation writeup](https://jeepinside.com/do-jeep-cherokees-hold-their-value/))

- Final buying recommendation (clear verdict):
  - If you want cheap mid‑size SUV capability and don’t mind tinkering or paying more for potential repairs: go for a well‑documented, mid‑range KL with full service history and a pre‑purchase inspection (PPI). In the NSW market snapshot the median price ~AUD $11,990 for 2014–2020 examples is realistic — but factor in the safety spend and higher running costs.
  - Avoid buying:
    - Any KL with ongoing transmission shudder, unexplained ECU/ADAS faults, or a history of oil‑bloating on the Tigershark without proof of corrective repairs.
    - Cars with outstanding recalls not attended to — demand the VIN recall history be clear and recently serviced at a dealer where practical. ([Mopar recall search](https://www.mopar.com/jeep/en-us/my-vehicle/recalls/search.html), [NHTSA recall lookup](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls))
  - Best bang‑for‑buck picks:
    - Sweet spot model year/trim: 2016–2018 mid‑trim Latitude/Limited with the **3.2 Pentastar** if you want mechanical robustness and can accept fuel penalty — or a well‑maintained 2.4 Tigershark with a detailed oil/service ledger if you want a cheaper running cost and lower purchase price.
    - If you can stretch to a newer hybrid KM (when locally available and if you keep cars long‑term), it’s the smarter long‑term fuel play for high km drivers — but buy one with factory warranties still intact.

Final, blunt takeaway: the Cherokee is a capable, characterful SUV that’ll get you further off the beaten track than most rivals, but in the used market you’re buying into a vehicle with known electronic, transmission and engine black spots — not to mention a target for clever thieves. Buy one cheap with a good PPI and service history, or pay up for a low‑km V6 or a factory‑maintained example. If you’re buying to flip in two years, pick a Toyota or Honda instead — they’ll keep their value and cause less late‑night wrenching.
