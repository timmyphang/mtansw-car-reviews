#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: Y400 (second-generation Rexton introduced 2017; facelift from 2021 and continuing into 2023). Production timeframe and lineage are covered on the Rexton’s Wikipedia page — the late‑2017 model with a 2021 facelift is the Rexton you’ll see in Australia and Europe today ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ssangyong_Rexton)).
- Internal alphanumeric codes: commonly referenced as the Y400 generation (sometimes marketed under KGM Rexton after SsangYong’s restructuring).
- Product hierarchy: the Rexton is KGM/SsangYong’s large, ladder‑frame, seven‑seat flagship SUV — it sits above the smaller SsangYong Musso pickup/SUV derivatives and below any marque move into genuine luxury SUVs. KGM positions the Rexton as its top practical family/tow vehicle ([KGM Rexton specs](https://www.kgm-motors.co.uk/new-cars/rexton/)).
- Platform architecture and parts-sharing: the Rexton is a traditional body‑on‑frame (ladder‑frame) large SUV. That under‑pinned architecture means many drivetrain and chassis parts are shared across SsangYong’s large‑SUV/pickup family — helpful for parts availability compared with bespoke monocoque rivals ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ssangyong_Rexton); [KGM specs](https://www.kgm-motors.co.uk/new-cars/rexton/)).
- Market‑specific powertrains (Australia/recent global list):
  - 2.2‑litre turbo‑diesel e‑XDi / OM672 series — approx ~202 PS (~199 bhp) and 441 Nm; typically paired with an 8‑speed automatic and AWD/selectable 4x4 ([Auto‑Data 2021 facelift](https://www.auto-data.net/en/ssangyong-rexton-ii-facelift-2021-2.2-e-xdi-202hp-4wd-e-tronic-7-seat-42660); [autoevolution](https://www.autoevolution.com/cars/ssangyong-rexton-2021.html)).
  - 2.0‑litre petrol turbo (selected markets) — newer small‑displacement petrol turbo options exist in some regions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ssangyong_Rexton)).
  - Older/other engines in global line-up include Mercedes‑derived diesels and petrols (2.7 XDi, 2.9, 3.2 etc. across earlier model years) — see the engine index for code mapping ([TotalEngines](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Ssangyong/Rexton)).
  - Drive: Rear‑wheel based with on‑demand AWD (4x4) selectable and low‑range on many models; 7‑seat layout, ladder frame chassis ([Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/ssangyong-rexton-model-1821)).
- Transmission types:
  - 8‑speed automatic in recent diesels (E‑Tronic), older pairings include Mercedes 7G‑Tronic adoption on some derivatives and Aisin manuals/automatics on earlier models ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ssangyong_Rexton); [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/ssangyong-rexton-ii-2.2-e-xdi-181hp-automatic-32445)).
- Explicit marketplace rivals:
  - Body‑on‑frame, seven‑seat/large SUV rivals: Mitsubishi Pajero Sport, Ford Everest, Isuzu MU‑X, Toyota Fortuner/Prado range in the Australian market. Comparisons and segment placement are discussed in Australian reviews ([CarExpert comparisons](https://www.carexpert.com.au/ssangyong/rexton/competitors?jw_start=303); [CarsGuide Rexton vs Prado](https://www.carsguide.com.au/ssangyong/rexton/vs/toyota-landcruiser-prado)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used SsangYong Rexton?

Exterior
- Panel fit & finish: Owners and aftermarket reviewers repeatedly call out fit-and-finish as “not premium” — expect uneven panel gaps and softer trim clips compared with Toyota or Hyundai class rivals. Owners report trim breakages on interior and exterior plastic bits over time ([carsales owner reviews](https://www.carsales.com.au/owner-reviews/ssangyong/rexton/)).
- Paint durability: Several owner threads point to paint and finish quality not matching the premium competition — scratches and scuff marks on gloss trims are common after everyday use.
- Trim vulnerabilities:
  - Gloss black plastic and matte chrome/trim pieces are prone to scratching and clip failure after a few years of heavy use.
- Body recalls / safety items:
  - Australian recall (important): a 2025 national recall (REC‑006327) covers Musso and Rexton (2020–2024) for a manufacturing defect where the engine cooling‑fan fuse may fail and cause engine overheating and sudden loss of motive power — check your VIN before purchase ([vehiclerecalls.gov.au REC‑006327](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-006327); and media coverage [CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/ssangyong-musso-and-rexton-recalled)).
- ADAS / safety system quirks:
  - Adaptive cruise control (ACC) and other driver‑assist systems: owners report calibration sensitivity — delayed or overly cautious responses in dense traffic and occasional ACC/AEB warnings. Community threads indicate software updates sometimes help but are not a universal cure; be prepared for sensor recalibration if warning lights appear ([ssang‑yong‑club ACC threads](https://en.ssang-yong-club.com/forum-category/rexton-13); [Auto Insider problem lists](https://www.autoinsider.co.uk/problems/ssangyong/rexton)).
  - Practical tip: always request the most recent infotainment/ADAS firmware log and ask the dealer about software/recall remedies.

Interior
- Durability under heavy usage: The cabin is spacious and well‑appointed for the segment, but owners regularly note rattles and trim loosening in high‑kilometre or rideshare/towing use — not unknown for ladder‑frame SUVs but worth factoring if you plan commercial usage ([carsales owner reviews](https://www.carsales.com.au/owner-reviews/ssangyong/rexton/)).
- Entry/exit and ergonomics:
  - Strengths: high ride height and wide door openings make ingress/egress easy for children and older passengers; second‑row offers good legroom for the class.
  - Weaknesses: door check angles and sill protection are average; repeated heavy use can result in scuffed door sills and worn rubber seals.
- Electrical behaviours:
  - 12V battery drain: forum reports of parasitic drains and battery‑related faults are present in owner discussions — check battery health and ask for a DAS (drain) check if the car has been sitting ([ssang‑yong forums](https://www.ssangyongclub.co.uk/forum/index.php); owners often post similar symptoms).
  - Infotainment hardware & software:
    - Screen sizes vary by trim; most late‑model Rextons have large centre touchscreens with Apple CarPlay/Android Auto; however, users report lagging or freezing touchscreens. There are community guides for checking firmware and updating via USB to resolve some issues ([YouTube firmware how‑to](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXmQ0Dh0VFc); [NavShop map updates](https://ssangyong.navshop.com/)).
    - Connectivity reliability: Bluetooth drops and slow pairing symptoms show up in owner threads — check functions during test drive (phone calling, streaming, map nav).
- Practical check list for buyers:
  - Boot and seat rails (folding 3rd row) for smooth operation; look for water ingress at rear lights/seals; test every USB/Aux port.

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation: The Rexton trades refinement for ruggedness — ladder‑frame, big torque diesels and towing capability are the selling points. However, the brand’s historical quality control and electrical reliability are a mixed bag compared to Japanese rivals ([CarExpert review & comparisons](https://www.carexpert.com.au/kgm/rexton/2025)).
- Security vulnerabilities:
  - CAN‑bus and keyless bypass: modern vehicles with CAN bus architectures can be targeted by skilled thieves via CAN injection attacks — this is not unique to SsangYong, and research papers and security blogs document such attacks and mitigation techniques. The Rexton’s immobiliser exists, but immobiliser/immobiliser fault threads are present on community forums — do not assume invulnerability ([The Register CAN bus article](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/); immobiliser primer [StartMyCar](https://www.startmycar.com/ssangyong/guides/immobilizer-anti-theft-system)).
  - Suggested anti‑theft measures:
    - Physical: Sold‑out but effective — steering wheel lock, wheel nut locks, solid trailer hitch locks for towing.
    - Digital/vehicle hardening: OBD port locks, aftermarket alarm with CAN‑bus intrusion detection, key‑fob Faraday pouch, and ensure any dealer firmware updates applied.
- High‑priority checks pre‑purchase:
  - Full transmission service history (see transmission section).
  - Proof of any ADAS recalibrations after bodywork or sensor servicing.
  - VIN recall check (see KGM recall page) — ask for dealer proof of recall compliance ([KGM safety recalls](https://kgm.com.au/owners-and-service/safety-recalls)).

Engines
- Below are engine code subsections that cover the engines you’re likely to encounter in the Rexton line. Each subsection is italicised (engine code) and references known failure points, owner‑reported issues and recommended maintenance intervals.

  - *OM672.960 — 2.2‑litre e‑XDi diesel (modern 2020s Rexton)*
    - Key facts: modern in‑house 2.2 turbodiesel producing ~200 PS and ~441 Nm; commonly paired with 8‑speed automatic and AWD ([Auto‑Data 2021 facelift](https://www.auto-data.net/en/ssangyong-rexton-ii-facelift-2021-2.2-e-xdi-202hp-4wd-e-tronic-7-seat-42660); [autoevolution](https://www.autoevolution.com/cars/ssangyong-rexton-2021.html)).
    - Failure points & quirks:
      - Turbocharger wear and soot‑related EGR clogging reported in owner threads — leads to loss of power or limp mode if neglected ([Autodoc common faults](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-ssangyong-rexton)).
      - Fuel‑system: high‑pressure pump and injector wear on diesel engines globally — monitor fuel pressure and service filter changes.
    - Preventative schedule:
      - Oil & filter: recommend 10,000 km intervals with high‑grade synthetic diesel oil (factory schedules can vary, but many owners and workshops use 10k‑15k km service windows — check the individual vehicle’s service manual and stick to the shorter interval if used in town/towing).
      - Diesel particulate filter (DPF) and EGR checks: inspect every 30,000 km, force regeneration if frequent short trips are common.
      - Turbo inspection: at ~80,000 km consider turbo charge system check for shaft play and oil feed issues if symptoms appear.

  - *OM671.960 / M174.950 — 2.0‑litre petrol/diesel variants (regional)*
    - Notes: 2.0 turbo petrol/diesel exist in some markets. Expect typical turbocharged petrol/diesel wear patterns — oil quality and timely servicing are the killers.
    - Failure points: turbocharger and high‑pressure fuel pump vulnerability on turbo units if oil change intervals are ignored.
    - Preventative schedule: oil & filter every 10,000 km in Australian use; spark plugs/petrol ignition and coil checks per manual.

  - *OM665.925/935 — 2.7 XDi (older Mercedes‑derived diesel)*
    - Notes: found in older Rexton generations; a Mercedes‑derived unit but reworked by SsangYong historically ([Wikipedia engine lineage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ssangyong_Rexton); [TotalEngines list](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Ssangyong/Rexton)).
    - Failure points: timing‑related components and turbo issues can be costly if neglected; look for oil leaks and DPF/EGR clogging.
    - Preventative schedule: stick to factory schedule; many workshops recommend 10,000 km oil services and DPF/EGR inspection around 60–100k km.

  - *OM662.925 — 2.9 and M162.995 — 3.2 petrol (older models)*
    - Notes: earlier large petrol engines used in first‑gen Rextons; fewer present in Australian used market today.
    - Failure points: head gasket/overheating reports rare but check service history and evidence of cooling system works.
    - Preventative schedule: 10–15,000 km oil/service intervals depending on use.

- General engine advice:
  - Diesel owners: if you do lots of short trips, expect DPF/EGR maintenance sooner. Heavy towing accelerates turbo & transmission wear — up your vigilance.
  - Always ask for a mechanical inspection and a spectroscopy oil analysis if the car is high‑km and you want to sniff out early bearing or turbo wear.

Transmission
- Types fitted across the range:
  - Recent diesels: 8‑speed automatic “E‑Tronic” (some sources differ on exact badge but Auto‑Data documents 8‑speed pairings for facelifted Rextons) ([Auto‑Data facelift 2021](https://www.auto-data.net/en/ssangyong-rexton-ii-facelift-2021-2.2-e-xdi-202hp-4wd-e-tronic-7-seat-42660)).
  - Older boxes: Mercedes‑derived 7‑speed, Aisin 6‑speed or earlier torque‑converter automatics in older Rextons ([Wikipedia transmission notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ssangyong_Rexton)).
- Known failure modes:
  - Recurring reports of jerky gear changes, delayed engagement and complete gearbox failures commonly in the 80,000–120,000 km window — often traced to worn hydraulic components, valve bodies or faulty control units ([Autodoc transmission faults summary](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-ssangyong-rexton); [Auto Insider gearbox threads](https://www.autoinsider.co.uk/problems/ssangyong/rexton/part/gearbox)).
  - Overheated transmission when towing: owners report gearbox temperature warnings; ensure correct transmission oil type and cooling when towing heavy loads.
- Service recommendations:
  - Transmission fluid & filter change every 60,000 km is a practical target for torque‑converter automatics — sooner if towing/harsh driving. If no documented fluid change history, budget for a service (and possibly a diagnostic scan) on purchase.
  - During test drive, watch for slip on cold start, delayed engagement, harsh downshifts or warning lights — these are red flags.
- Mechanical behavior:
  - Expect torque‑converter character: smooth when healthy, but as wear begins, symptoms include slipping, hard changes, whining, and loss of gears. Early diagnosis and hydraulic/valve body repair can be costly but cheaper than a full rebuild.

Depreciation metrics
- Hard numbers: the Rexton depreciates faster than Toyota/Ford equivalents — the Rexton’s value proposition is initial price advantage but weaker brand value retention. Direct head‑to‑head price comparisons show the Rexton undercuts a Prado by a wide margin new and used (CarsGuide price comparisons — Rexton $36,990–$53,990 vs Prado $73k+), which explains sharper percentage drops vs Japanese rivals ([CarsGuide comparison](https://www.carsguide.com.au/ssangyong/rexton/vs/toyota-landcruiser-prado)).
- Baseline rule: expect a steeper-than‑segment depreciation in the first 3–5 years versus segment benchmark (Toyota/Ford) — exact % varies by trim and market, but plan on paying less up front and accepting higher relative depreciation if you’re trading after a few years.

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy — how to run the numbers for your situation
- What to include: purchase price, depreciation, fuel, servicing & consumables, insurance, registration, tyres, and likely repair “surprises” (electronics/transmission work). For Rexton specifically, budget a higher reserve for potential transmission work after 80–120k km and for software/infotainment/ADAS fixes.
- Fuel economy baseline for calculations:
  - Official/real world: CarsGuide reports combined starting from ~8.7 L/100 km for modern Rexton figures and WLTP data is available for precise variants ([CarsGuide fuel notes](https://www.carsguide.com.au/ssangyong/rexton/engine); [WLTP model fuel guide](https://www.wltpinfo.com/model/ssangyong/rexton_model_year_2023/Diesel.html)).
  - Real‑world owners on Fuelly show variability depending on load/towing — long‑distance highway driving will be kinder than heavy urban/towing use ([Fuelly Rexton logs](https://www.fuelly.com/car/ssangyong/rexton)).
- Simple worked example (your inputs):
  - Choose annual km (e.g., 15,000 km/year).
  - Fuel consumption (use 8.7 L/100 km as combined baseline).
  - Fuel price (use your local average; for demonstration, plug in any current petrol/diesel price).
  - Annual fuel cost = (15,000 / 100) * 8.7 * price_per_litre.
  - Add servicing (annual average depending on mileage; diesel services at 10k–15k km intervals), insurance, and a maintenance reserve (I recommend $1,500–$3,000/year reserve if you expect transmissions/electronics work over ownership).
- Hybrid/EV premium analysis (is paying extra worth it?):
  - For the Rexton specifically there is no widely available factory hybrid/EV variant in the Australian ladder‑frame seven‑seat class at the same price — your choices are either the diesel Rexton or stepping up to a very different vehicle architecture (hybrid monocoque Sorento, Prado hybrid, or EV).
  - The math: hybrids/EVs make sense if your annual running is high (lots of city kilometres where hybrids excel) or if fuel prices are extremely high — for long‑distance towing and lots of highway kilos, a torquey diesel body‑on‑frame like the Rexton can be more efficient and robust than a hybrid not designed for heavy tow duties ([CarsGuide fuel & engine commentary](https://www.carsguide.com.au/ssangyong/rexton/engine); CarExpert towing comparison context [CarExpert comparison](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-comparisons/2024-mitsubishi-pajero-sport-v-ssangyong-rexton-comparison)).
  - Conclusion: unless you need urban‑only commuting savings or low‑emissions compliance, a dedicated hybrid/EV alternative rarely delivers a guaranteed net saving when you include higher purchase premiums, potential battery replacement risk, and the Rexton’s towing/trailer needs. Plug values into the TCO template above and include expected premium for the hybrid’s purchase price to see your break‑even year.

Buying recommendation — should you buy one?
- Short answer: Yes — but only if you buy with your eyes open. The Rexton is a bargain for buyers who want seven seats, ladder‑frame robustness and towing capability for a fraction of what a Toyota or Ford costs new. You get a roomy cabin, good standard equipment at the price point and a proper diesel torque curve for towing.
- When to pass: If you want the quietest cabin, best long‑term resale or dealership support networks that rival Toyota’s footprint — skip it. If you do low‑km city commuting only and want the absolute lowest running costs via hybrid tech, consider other powertrains.
- Specific NSW market pick (based on your snapshot): the 2023 Rexton listings sitting at AUD $36,990–$38,850 (median $37,920) with ~25,200 km are reasonably priced for near‑new examples. Ask for:
  - Full service history with proof of transmission fluid changes and turbo/EGR services.
  - Proof that the 2025 cooling‑fan‑fuse recall (if applicable to the VIN) has been completed — check via the Australian recall page ([vehiclerecalls.gov.au REC‑006327](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-006327)) and KGM recall completion proof ([KGM safety recalls](https://kgm.com.au/owners-and-service/safety-recalls)).
  - A dealer diagnostic report for ADAS firmware versions and infotainment updates (YouTube guides show owners can check and update firmware if needed — ask the dealer for logs) ([YouTube firmware how‑to](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MXmQ0Dh0VFc); [NavShop](https://ssangyong.navshop.com/)).
- Optimal financial sweet spot:
  - If you want the best blend of warranty, equipment and lower risk — a 2022–2023 higher‑spec Rexton with documented dealer servicing and under 50,000 km is the sweet spot in Australia. These units typically offer modern drivetrains (the 2.2 diesel + 8‑speed) and recent software/recall fixes without the new‑car premium.
  - If budget is tight and you don’t mind DIY maintenance or independent workshops, a well‑sorted 2018–2020 Rexton with complete service history can deliver the value proposition — but insist on a pre‑purchase transmission and engine inspection.

Final blunt take (peer to peer)
- If you need a cheap, competent, ladder‑frame seven‑seat tow vehicle that doesn’t scream “I bought a Prado” at the club, the Rexton makes a lot of sense. But don’t buy emotionally — buy with a checklist: service history, transmission service proof, ADAS firmware status, and recall completion. Expect to trade a swankier badge and better residuals for immediate value and capability. If you’re buying one of the NSW cars in that $37k band, you’re getting a near‑new specimen that’s worth a hard inspection and an independent scan. Bring a strong mechanical mate and a decent warranty add‑on if you can.
