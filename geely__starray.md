#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation & timeframe: The Geely Starray (global nameplate) arrived mid‑2020s — Australian arrivals are 2025–2027 model years in dealer stock — marketed primarily as the Starray EM‑i (plug‑in hybrid) in Australia alongside turbo‑petrol options in other markets. See Geely's model page for official positioning and drivetrain notes: [Geely STARRAY](https://www.geely.com/en/models/starray).
- Internal/alphanumeric codes & platform: The Starray sits on Geely’s compact modular underpinnings; reviewers repeatedly note it shares architecture and parts commonality with the EX5 family (B‑segment/compact modular families derived from Geely group platforms) which helps parts availability across the group in export markets. See platform references: Geely product page and platform overviews ([Geely STARRAY](https://www.geely.com/en/models/starray), BMA summary at [Wikipedia — B‑segment Modular Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-segment_Modular_Architecture)) and EX5 references in reviews ([What Car?](https://www.whatcar.com/geely/starray/estate/review/n28670)).
- Product hierarchy: In Geely’s range the Starray is pitched above compact crossovers (smaller BMA cars) and below larger Volvo‑sourced premium offerings within the group; the Starray targets near‑mainstream families who want tech and space for less than mainstream Japanese/Korean rivals.
- Shared global platform & cross‑models: The BMA / compact modular family and Geely’s EX5 lineage support the Starray in export markets — that shared architecture shows up across multiple Geely models and helps parts sourcing (sensors, suspension arms, electrified components) in export markets. See platform notes and EX5 linkage: [BMA wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B-segment_Modular_Architecture), [What Car?](https://www.whatcar.com/geely/starray/estate/review/n28670).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions & drivetrains:
  - *1.5T petrol* — roughly 174–181 hp (≈130–134 kW), ~270 Nm; front‑wheel drive; paired to a 7‑speed wet dual‑clutch **7DCT** in global markets ([Auto‑Data Starray 1.5T](https://www.auto-data.net/en/geely-starray-1.5t-174hp-dct-52971)).
  - *2.0T petrol* — roughly 218 hp (≈160 kW), ~325 Nm; front‑wheel drive; also mated to a 7‑speed **7DCT** ([Auto‑Data Starray 2.0T](https://www.auto-data.net/en/geely-starray-2.0t-218hp-dct-52972)).
  - *EM‑i PHEV* — plug‑in hybrid variants with battery capacities quoted variably in export spec sheets (examples 18.4–29.8 kWh in some markets, larger 29.8 kWh units reported), electric ranges claimed up to ~80–84 km WLTP depending on pack; combined driving using petrol/electric depends on trim and battery size. See Geely spec sheets and Carwow/CarExpert coverage ([Geely spec sheet — EM‑i preliminary PDF](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/61bef282dd6ffd4be0f07332/68cb5e25b38962959c22ffe5_geely-starray-em-i-specification-sheet-september-2025-preliminary.pdf), [Carwow Starray specs](https://www.carwow.co.uk/geely/starray/specifications), [CarExpert EM‑i review](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-review)).
  - Transmission: Geely specifies a new generation wet dual‑clutch **7DCT** for the range; Geely staff claim high efficiency and quick shifts ([Geely STARRAY technical note](https://www.geely.com/en/models/starray)).
- Marketplace rivals: The Starray is cross‑shopped against the Toyota RAV4, Mazda CX‑5, Hyundai/Kia mid‑size crossovers and a raft of Chinese challengers (BYD Atto, Haval H6, MG HS, Chery Tiggo 8, etc.). See Australia‑market comparators and head‑to‑heads ([CarsGuide comparisons](https://www.carsguide.com.au/geely/starray-em-i/vs), [CarExpert review](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-review)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Geely Starray?
Below I’ve split problem areas the way you’ll actually encounter them at a dealer or private inspection — from scratches on the paint to software quirks that frustrate owners.

Exterior
- Panel and trim fit:
  - Early owner reports and reviews point to generally good first impressions of trim and finish, but a handful of fit‑and‑finish niggles (panel gaps, door alignment) appear in owner forums and country‑specific MOT/inspection aggregators — not catastrophic, but worth a close walkaround. See Drive and What Car? tester notes on build ([Drive review](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-inspire-review/), [What Car?](https://www.whatcar.com/geely/starray/estate/review/n28670)).
  - Trim vulnerabilities: gloss black plastics and decorative trim panels are typical scratching hotspots on new cars — owners report the usual chips and scuffs. Expect to budget for minor trim repairs if the unit has been used in urban duty.
- Paint & body durability:
  - No systemic nationwide paint delamination recall appears in public datasets for the Starray at time of writing, but check any VIN in government recall portals before purchase (e.g., NHTSA recall search for exported models) — always verify with dealer and service history ([NHTSA recall checker](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls)).
- ADAS & safety electronics:
  - ADAS calibration and tuning are the headline "watch" items. Multiple independent road tests and long‑term pieces flagged lane‑keep, adaptive cruise and driver‑assistance calibration being rough out of the box; reviewers and owners note Geely pushed OTA updates to improve things. If you're evaluating a used Starray, confirm whether the vehicle has received the major OTA/firmware patches. Sources: GoAuto flagged ADAS tuning issues ([GoAuto review](https://www.goauto.com.au/car-reviews/geely/starray/2025-geely-starray-em-i-review/2025-09-24/97431.html)), MotoringNZ noted calibration problems ([MotoringNZ road test](https://www.motoringnz.com/tested/2026/2/19/geely-starray-em-i-road-test-review-solid-but-no-supernova)), and updates were rolled out per automotive tech news ([TechDrivePlay on OTA update](https://techdriveplay.com/2026/03/04/geely-starray-em-i-gets-its-first-major-ota-update/)).
  - Practical check: test every ADAS function on public roads — lane‑keep, AEB urban/pedestrian, adaptive cruise — and get the dealer to show the OTA history or service records proving the vehicles have been updated.
  - Euro NCAP delivered a 5‑star rating for the EM‑i model on tested equipment, but real‑world usability and tuning still need verification on the car you inspect ([Euro NCAP assessment](https://www.euroncap.com/assessments/geely/starray%2Bem-i/1211/)).

Interior
- Durability and heavy use:
  - The cabin is widely praised for perceived quality and equipment levels, but users flag heavy touchscreen dependence and a reduced number of physical controls — that increases wear points: persistent screen freezing or lag can be more than an annoyance if the car is used for rideshare duty. Parkers and other reviewers note lag and reliance on touchscreen interfaces ([Parkers interior notes](https://www.parkers.co.uk/geely/starray/review/interior/)).
  - Leatherette/trim wear on high‑touch surfaces (armrests, steering wheel) follows the usual pattern — inspect seam stitching and the driver seat bolsters on any used example.
- Entry/exit & packaging:
  - The Starray scores well on cabin space and practicality in reviews; door sills and aperture are family‑friendly — but verify door check angles and sill height if you regularly ferry kids in and out (child seat installation access).
- Electrical & infotainment:
  - Infotainment reliability is a recurring theme: reports of frozen screens or wireless CarPlay problems were recorded in early ownership reports and test drives — owners often solved or mitigated these with firmware updates or reboots. See troubleshooting and owner reports ([BitAuto Q&A on freezes](https://www.bitauto.com/ask/100128783491/), MyPitShop review noting broken wireless CarPlay in one test unit ([MyPitShop review](https://mypitshop.com/geely-starray-em-i-review/))).
  - Wireless charging pad placement and performance has drawn owner tweaks and DIY fixes on sister models (EX5); check phone charging reliability during your test drive ([YouTube owner fix example](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HNkx2skc9KM)).
  - 12V battery drain — no large‑scale plague of phantom drains documented in the sources; still, as with any highly‑electronic modern car, expect parasitic draws from telematics and always inspect service logs for repeated jump‑starts in dealer history aggregations (see owner aggregator pages and reviews: [CarsGuide reviews](https://www.carsguide.com.au/geely/starray/reviews), [Folt buyer’s guide](https://folt.co.uk/cars/geely/starray)).
- Practical test checklist:
  - Ensure the head‑unit has the latest firmware/OTA applied, test wireless CarPlay/Android Auto, check wireless charging and all USB ports, cycle heated seats/AC while stationary (watch for infotainment hangs).

Mechanically
- Engineering reputation & build:
  - Geely’s engineering has stepped up in recent years (Volvo/Geely group investment matters here), and reviews praise the hardware for what it offers at the price. But it's still a relative newcomer to the Australian mainstream; long‑term mechanical reliability is less proven than Toyota/Honda. See hands‑on evaluations and value positioning ([Autoblog first drives](https://www.autoblog.com/reviews/2025-geely-starray-review-hands-on-with-an-off-limits-chinese-suv), [CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-review)).
- Security vulnerabilities:
  - Modern connected cars can be attack surfaces. Industry‑wide techniques like CAN injection can affect many makes if architectures aren't hardened — while there’s no public Geely‑specific CAN exploit disclosure in these search results, buyers should assume increasing risk and protect their cars like any modern connected vehicle. Read up on CAN injection explanations and mitigation approaches ([The Register on CAN bus theft](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/), technical paper on CAN Injection attacks ([can-cia PDF](https://can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf)).
  - Practical steps: insist on dealer activation of anti‑theft software patches, fit a mechanical steering‑wheel lock for longer‑term parking, and consider a secondary immobiliser with professional install.
- Aftermarket anti‑theft measures to ask about or fit:
  - Faraday key pouch for the keyfob at home (kills relay attack surface).
  - Professional CAN‑bus hardening or alarm systems that isolate the OBD port.
  - Physical wheel or pedal locks for suburban parking or when storing the car.

Engines
- Note: I list each engine variant discussed in reviews and specs. For every engine I italicise the variant and then give the failure points and maintenance notes that appear reliably in the public reporting.

  - *1.5T (approx. 174–181 hp, 270 Nm)*  
    - Failure/weak points: turbocharged small‑capacity engines carry the usual risks — turboactuator wear, high‑pressure fuel pump stress and turbo oil‑cooling circuit sensitivity if servicing is neglected. Owner forums for Geely models and early Starray owners report higher perceived fuel consumption and occasional NVH/harshness rather than outright mechanical collapse, but turbo oil and boost‑related components demand attention. Sources: Auto‑Data spec summary and owner forum aggregators ([Auto‑Data 1.5T](https://www.auto-data.net/en/geely-starray-1.5t-174hp-dct-52971), Motorsift aggregated owner issues ([Motorsift Starray page](https://www.motorsift.co.nz/vehicle/geely-starray-emi-2023-2026-4466)).
    - Preventative maintenance: be conservative — oil and filter at least every 10,000 km if running urban or hybrid cycles, use manufacturer‑spec synthetic oil, and insist on documented turbo/PCV system checks at every service. If you do a lot of short trips, service intervals should be the shorter end of any recommended range.
  - *2.0T (approx. 218 hp, 325 Nm)*  
    - Failure/weak points: similar turbocharged engine caveats — higher torque means higher thermal and transmission loads. No widespread engine‑destructive faults documented in the sample sources, but the higher torque places more load on the wet DCT and clutch packs (see transmission notes). Source: Auto‑Data ([Auto‑Data 2.0T](https://www.auto-data.net/en/geely-starray-2.0t-218hp-dct-52972)).
    - Preventative maintenance: follow the same conservative oil change regime; inspect engine mounts and cooling system frequently if you do towing or heavy loads.
  - *EM‑i PHEV powertrain (various battery sizes; electric motor + petrol engine)*  
    - Failure/weak points: PHEV systems add complexity — battery thermal management, high‑voltage wiring/connectors and power electronics are the new watch items. No mass battery recall or catastrophic failures for the Starray EM‑i are listed in the sources, but the ecosystem is new and requires watching. Reported strengths include long electric range claims (WLTP figures up to ~84 miles / ~80–84 km on larger packs). See spec sheets and PHEV range reporting ([Geely specification sheet PDF](https://cdn.prod.website-files.com/61bef282dd6ffd4be0f07332/68cb5e25b38962959c22ffe5_geely-starray-em-i-specification-sheet-september-2025-preliminary.pdf), [Carwow battery/range notes](https://www.carwow.co.uk/geely/starray/specifications), [CarsGuide fuel economy](https://www.carsguide.com.au/geely/starray-em-i/fuel-consumption)).
    - Preventative maintenance: battery pack cooling and software updates matter. Make sure the car’s high‑voltage battery has health documentation and that the battery was charged/used regularly (PHEVs left idle long periods can have degraded conditioning). Check for OTA patch history — some owners reported improved usability post‑OTA ([TechDrivePlay OTA report](https://techdriveplay.com/2026/03/04/geely-starray-em-i-gets-its-first-major-ota-update/), YouTube owner reports).
    - Service intervals: high‑voltage systems typically require inspection rather than frequent replacement; follow Geely Australia’s schedule (ask the dealer). For the petrol engine part of a PHEV, follow the short‑trip conservative oil change cadence (10,000 km suggested practice).
- Service strategy: If you want a specific maintenance cadence for inspection and fluid changes, treat the Starray like any modern turbo/PHEV — oil/filter at 10,000 km if you do urban runs, full coolant and brake fluid checks every 30,000–40,000 km, DCT fluid inspection and replacement intervals per dealer schedule (see transmission section).

Transmission
- The headline: a **wet 7‑speed dual‑clutch transmission (7DCT)** is standard on many Starray variants and is promoted by Geely as efficient and quick‑shifting ([Geely STARRAY official tech note](https://www.geely.com/en/models/starray)). Wet DCTs are a sensible choice for higher torque and stop‑start city driving when well‑cooled.
- Known failure modes & behaviour:
  - Reports from owner forums and regional sites flag transmission harshness/jerkiness and occasional grinding or slipping sensations on some units — early‑life calibration and fluid condition appear to be the root causes in many anecdotes. Sources: BitAuto and owner forum threads ([BitAuto disadvantages list](https://www.bitauto.com/wiki/10015162859/), gari.pk transmission problems summary).
  - Advice from forums: inspect the transmission fluid smell/colour and ensure records show regular fluid/service per Geely’s schedule; some owners report burning smells or gear change judders that were resolved by fluid changes or software updates.
  - Wet DCTs are more tolerant of heat than dry DCTs, but they still need correct fluid and cooling. If you intend to tow or drive hard, request documented transmission service history and a transmission scan for logged fault codes.
- Mechanical behaviour: in day‑to‑day driving reviewers often describe prompt shifts when new and quiet cruising; where owners have issues it tends to be in low‑speed clutch engagement (city creep) and during repeated low‑speed, high‑load manoeuvres. See Auto Express/AutoBlog road test impressions and owner reports ([Auto Express](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/geely/starray), [Autoblog first drive](https://www.autoblog.com/reviews/2025-geely-starray-review-hands-on-with-an-off-limits-chinese-suv)).

#### 3. Should you buy a used Geely Starray?
- NSW dealer market snapshot (live): Active listings 46 | Price range: AUD $35,888 – $45,410 | Median price: AUD $38,884 | Year range: 2025–2027 | Median odometer: 10 km | Transmission mix: Automatic 45. This is near‑new territory — many units are demo/near‑new stock, which tells you dealers are trading volume on relatively fresh cars.
- Current used marketplace entry/ceiling price (NSW dealer data): expect to pay about AUD $35,900 at the low end to AUD $45,400 at the top; median is roughly AUD $38,884 (see the NSW dealer snapshot provided).
- Depreciation & residuals:
  - Short answer: unknown but watchful. The Starray arrives into a market where near‑new stock dominates used supply, so immediate depreciation has already been realised by demonstrator pricing and dealer incentives in some markets. Compared to rock‑steady Toyotas and Hyundais, expect a larger percentage drop in years 1–3 unless Geely builds demonstrable long‑term reliability and dealer support. Use valuation tools (KBB, CarsGuide, local dealer listings) to track monthly moves. See used price comparators: Autotrader/Arnold Clark example pages for cross‑market used stock ([Arnold Clark used listings](https://www.arnoldclark.com/used-cars/geely/starray), [Autotrader listings](https://www.autotrader.co.uk/cars/geely/starray)).
  - Practical metric: If you want a hard number, use the standard rule — newcomers often drop 20–30% faster than segment leaders in the first three years. Treat that as a conservative planning assumption and check local trade valuations.
- TCO calculation strategy (how to actually compare ownership costs):
  - Running cost inputs you can rely on from published figures:
    - ICE figures: *1.5T* ~6.3 L/100 km (Auto‑Data), *2.0T* ~6.7 L/100 km (Auto‑Data) — use these for fuel spend scenarios in pure petrol usage ([Auto‑Data 1.5T](https://www.auto-data.net/en/geely-starray-1.5t-174hp-dct-52971), [Auto‑Data 2.0T](https://www.auto-data.net/en/geely-starray-2.0t-218hp-dct-52972)).
    - PHEV figures: combined WLTP reported as low as 2.4 L/100 km with an EV‑only range ~83 km in Australian testing frames; real‑world benefit depends on daily charging behaviour ([CarsGuide fuel consumption](https://www.carsguide.com.au/geely/starray-em-i/fuel-consumption), [Carwow battery/range](https://www.carwow.co.uk/geely/starray/specifications)).
  - TCO tools & steps:
    1. Choose your annual km: 10,000 km (urban mix), 20,000 km (commuter/heavy).
    2. For petrol models, multiply the L/100 km by your annual km and local fuel price to get annual petrol spend.
    3. For the EM‑i PHEV, estimate the share of km done electrically. If you can charge daily and your typical commute is ≤80 km round trip, the PHEV will be mostly electric and fuel use will be minimal — plug that into the 2.4 L/100 km combined claim and add residential electricity cost per kWh for charging cycles (use the published WLTP battery size and EV consumption figures to estimate kWh/100 km).
    4. Add service, insurance (likely a touch higher for a newcomer brand), registration and expected depreciation. For service costs, ask local Geely dealers for a 5‑year service pack estimate.
  - Is the PHEV worth the premium? If most of your driving is short daily commutes with reliable overnight charging, the EM‑i will reduce fuel significantly and probably deliver TCO advantages over a petrol‑only unit — but only if you consistently exploit its electric range. If you do long highway runs and rarely charge, the PHEV’s complexity and potential battery degradation risks mean petrol variants are the simpler economic choice. Sources: CarsGuide and CarExpert PHEV coverage and WLTP claims ([CarsGuide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/geely/starray-em-i/fuel-consumption), [CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-review)).
- Warranty & dealer network:
  - Before committing, confirm Geely Australia’s current warranty terms and the dealer servicing footprint in NSW — one of the biggest risks with buying a newcomer is dealer coverage and parts lead time. Ask for full service history and OTA patch records. CarExpert and CarsGuide show Geely’s aggressive value play but also note buyers must accept the newer service network reality ([CarExpert review](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-review), [CarsGuide comparisons](https://www.carsguide.com.au/geely/starray-em-i/vs)).
- Final buying recommendation (pragmatic, not evangelising):
  - If you want tech and space for the money and accept a bit more early‑adopter risk: buy a near‑new **2026 EM‑i** mid‑trim (Complete) or the Inspire if you want the largest battery/longest EV range. Those trims balance kit, range and likely resale value in the short term. CarExpert and Australian coverage show the EM‑i as the headline model for value seekers ([CarExpert EM‑i review](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2026-geely-starray-em-i-review)).
  - If you prioritise the lowest mechanical risk and easiest resale: wait for a 2–3‑year old example with full accredited service history and clear OTA update records, buy from a dealer who will warranty and demonstrate all ADAS/OTA updates applied. Given NSW listings are heavily near‑new, you can pick up demo stock with minimal km for a significant saving compared with new list price — but insist on proof of software updates and service.
  - Avoid private buys with missing software/service records. No matter how tidy the interior, missing OTA/service history is where cost and headaches hide.
- The bottom line in plain English:
  - The Starray is an attractive, very well‑equipped offering that undercuts many rivals on kit and EV capability (if you choose EM‑i). But it’s still the new kid on the block in Australia — excellent on features, with proven early fixes rolling out via OTA, but not yet earning the iron‑clad long‑term resale and dealer cover of mainstream Japanese brands. Buy one if you want value and gadgetry and you can live with a slightly higher sniff of early‑adopter risk; otherwise, wait for the three‑year mark when the initial kinks will almost certainly be worked out and used prices stabilise.
- Optimal buy: a 2026 EM‑i mid‑trim (Complete) or Inspire if you need the extra EV range; insist on full OTA/service records. Expect to pay around the NSW market median (~AUD $38,884) for a low‑km dealer unit; bargains will show up near the low end of the $35,888–$45,410 band but check update/service proofs first.
