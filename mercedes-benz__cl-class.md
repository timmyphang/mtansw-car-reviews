#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: C216 (often called W216 in owner threads) — production timeframe for the last-generation CL‑Class: **2006–2014**. [Wikipedia C216](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_CL-Class_(C216))
- Internal codes and siblings:
  - CL‑Class = Mercedes’ two‑door flagship grand tourer, closely related to the S‑Class. The C216/CL‑Class sits above the CLS/CLS‑Class and below the (now merged) S‑Class Coupe lineage that replaced the CL in 2014. [Mercedes CL-Class Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_CL-Class)
- Platform architecture and shared models:
  - The C216 CL‑Class uses the W221 S‑Class platform — that’s important because parts, suspension components and diagnostic strategies are widely shared with late‑2000s S‑Class cars. Parts availability tends to be reasonable relative to rarer models because the W221 family was sold in big numbers. [Wikipedia C216](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_CL-Class_(C216))
  - Other cars on the same S‑Class family (platform/architecture) include contemporaneous W221 S‑Class models; AMG variants share hardware with E/ML/G‑class AMG families in parts bins for big V8s/V12s.
- Engines, outputs, transmissions and drivetrains sold in Australia (market summary)
  - Petrol V8 and V12 engines across the line. Common engine codes seen on C216 listings and technical writeups include: **M273**, **M278**, **M156** (AMG), **M157** (AMG twin‑turbo), **M275** (V12 twin‑turbo). [Wikipedia C216](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_CL-Class_(C216))
  - Typical market variants sold globally (and commonly imported/sold in Australia): CL500 / CL550 (V8), CL600 / CL65 (V12), CL63 AMG and CL65/CL60 AMG performance variants. Transmissions: Mercedes 7‑speed 7G‑Tronic automatics (722.9 family) and older 5‑speed units on some early AMG V12s. Drivetrain: predominantly rear‑wheel drive (RWD); 4MATIC variants rare on CL.
  - Fuel consumption ballpark (real world): expect ~12.0 L/100 km combined for the regular V8s (factory combined for CL500 cited ~12.1 L/100 km) — AMGs and V12s will be materially thirstier. [Motoreu CL500](https://motoreu.com/mercedes-cl-500-mpg-fuel-consumption-technical-specifications-166003), [Edmunds fuel figures](https://www.edmunds.com/mercedes-benz/cl-class/2009/review/)
- Marketplace rivals in Australia:
  - BMW 6/8‑Series, Audi A7/8 and large GTs like Porsche Panamera or older Bentley Continental examples (price/segment overlap). Direct competitor set for buyers cross‑shopping used luxury GTs includes BMW 6 Series and Audi A7/6/8 family. [iSeeCars vs BMW 6 Series](https://www.iseecars.com/compare/mercedes__benz-cl__class-vs-bmw-6_series)

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mercedes‑Benz CL‑Class?
This is the nuts‑and‑bolts section. If you’re buying one of these as a daily or a weekend grand tourer, read it twice.

Exterior
- Panel gaps and finish:
  - Expect variable factory panel gaps and occasional uneven fitment — owners frequently note bonnet/wing and door gaps that look “off” compared with newer cars. This is documented in owner forums for the family (W216/C216 threads). [MBWorld forum thread on panel gaps](https://mbworld.org/forums/c218/914283-these-panel-gaps-normal.html)
- Paint and clear coat:
  - Older CLs (2006–2010) with harsh sun exposure commonly show clear‑coat delamination or peeling on tops and boot lids. It’s a cosmetic fight you can win only with respray or serious correction. Community reports on clear‑coat failures are long‑running. [BenzWorld clear coat reports](https://www.benzworld.org/threads/clear-coat-peeling-off.1614732/)
- Exterior trim / mouldings:
  - Gloss and rubber trim can fade/crack on older cars. Replacement parts exist (OEM and aftermarket) but labour for bumper/door removal to fit new trims is non‑trivial. Parts catalogue references exist (Pelican Parts pages for W216). [W216 exterior trim parts](https://www.pelicanparts.com/cat/r_c216/bdyext_moulding-and-trim)
- ADAS and sensor problems:
  - Distronic/active cruise systems and radar sensors show sensitivity to contamination and bumper damage. Faults like “radar sensor dirty” or Distronic deactivated are common; cleaning and re‑calibration are often required after bumper work or when sensors are obstructed. Firmware/software fixes helped some late‑build cars, but sensors and associated module replacements remain costly. [MercedesAssistance radar sensor clean](https://en.mercedesassistance.com/mercedes-radar-sensor-dirty-how-to-fix/), [MercedesMedic Distronic discussion](https://www.mercedesmedic.com/mercedes-radar-sensor-problems-solutions/)
  - Practical check at inspection: walk the bumper for puddle/stone chip repairs, ensure sensors aren’t misaligned, look for aftermarket tow‑bar or bumper repairs (cheap repairs often throw ADAS out of calibration).

Interior
- Build and wear:
  - The CL was built to impress — but leather, stitched trims and dash plastics in cars used hard (rideshare, courier, hail) show wear: seat bolsters collapse, dash hairline cracks appear on older examples. Owners frequently report cracked dash surfaces on older models. [MercedesSource dash cracks](https://mercedessource.com/problems/interior/cracks-the-dash)
- COMAND infotainment and screens:
  - COMAND units on W216/C216 models can freeze (boot stuck on logo), suffer from soft‑button failure or intermittent black screens. Resets sometimes work; many owners pay for head unit reprogramming or replacement. Look at screen size/trim differences — higher trims had larger COMAND screens and more features. [BenzWorld COMAND freeze thread](https://www.benzworld.org/threads/comand-frozen-on-mb-logo-but-audio-working.2126970/), [Go‑Parts garage infotainment failures](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/infotainment-display-mercedes-benz-b-class-mercedes-benz-cla-class-mercedes-benz-gla-class-2015-2019)
- Electrical quirks and battery behaviour:
  - Older CLs can show parasitic 12V draws from modules (comfort electronics, SAM modules, telematics). Intermittent battery drain manifests if a vehicle spends long periods unused. If a car has had multiple module replacements, ask for invoice history — diagnostics are expensive when modules talk nonsense.
- Practical inspection points:
  - Test all seat motors, soft close doors (if equipped), roof/convertible fittings (if retrofit), sunroof seals, and every infotainment feature — sometimes a single module failure kills multiple functions.

Mechanically
- General engineering reputation:
  - Mercedes built the CL as an engineering tour‑de‑force. The W221/S‑platform engineering is sophisticated and robust — until complexity bites. Air suspension (if fitted), Active Body Control on earlier CLs and elaborate electronics can be expensive to fix.
- High‑priority security vulnerabilities:
  - The industry has documented “CAN injection” headlight/bumper wiring exploits that let thieves inject CAN frames and impersonate smart keys to open and start cars. Mercedes models are part of the broader set of vulnerable vehicles — attackers have used headlight access points to inject frames and bypass immobilisers. This is real and documented. [CAN injection paper / writeup](https://can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf), [summary writeup on Autoblog](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update)
  - Mitigations: physical steering wheel locks, OBD‑port locks, securing headlight wiring (grommets, inner‑bumper shields), aftermarket immobilisers or CAN intrusion detection systems. See also Mercedes guidance on CAN bus troubleshooting for where messages live. [Mercedes Assistance CAN bus troubleshooting](https://en.mercedesassistance.com/can-bus-troubleshooting/)
- Recommended aftermarket anti‑theft:
  - Fit a steering‑wheel lock, an OBD locking device, and consider an alarm/immobiliser that cuts fuel/ignition; if budget allows, fit a CAN intrusion monitor (specialists sell these) or an aftermarket tracker with immobilise feature.

Engines
- Below are the engine variants commonly seen on C216/CL‑Class examples. Each engine subsection is italicised per instruction and focuses on documented weak points and suggested service cadence.

* **M273 (naturally aspirated V8)**  
  - Typical fitment: early C216/C215 V8 models (pre‑2010 V8 architecture). Known service items include valve cover gaskets and age‑related oil leaks; PCV and inlet tract deposits on high‑mileage cars. No widely documented catastrophic design recall for M273 on the C216 specifically in the search results, but general V8 wear is expected.  
  - Preventative maintenance: change oil and filter every 10,000 km for peace of mind (older performance engines benefit from tighter oil intervals), check valve clearances and replace gaskets as soon as leaks appear.

* **M278 (newer twin‑turbo V8 family)**  
  - Appears on later powertrains as Mercedes modernised the V8 range; turbocharged architecture invites higher servicing demands — turbo oil feed/return health, intercooler condition and smoke/oil consumption checks.  
  - Preventative maintenance: oil change every 10,000 km, turbo health check (soot/whine), intercooler pressure/leak test during major services.

* **M156 (6.2‑litre AMG V8, naturally aspirated)**  
  - AMG‑derived high‑rev NA V8 — strong power but higher owner‑service costs: valve‑train, camshaft wear and occasional oil consumption reported in high‑mileage, highly driven examples. AMG cars often had stronger clutches/gearbox cooling demands.  
  - Preventative maintenance: 10,000 km oil services with high‑spec oil; inspect cam and valve train during major services; expect higher consumables cost.

* **M157 (AMG twin‑turbo 5.5 V8)**  
  - Pack a lot of torque; turbochargers and high‑pressure fuel systems add complexity. Watch for turbo/oil leaks, high‑pressure fuel pump wear and boost‑related oil contamination. Turbocharger servicing history and intercooler cleanliness are essential checks.  
  - Preventative maintenance: oil every 10,000 km, attention to turbo oil feed lines and intercooler plumbing, and replacement of fuel filter at shorter intervals if used hard.

* **M275 (twin‑turbo V12: CL600/CL65 territory)**  
  - The V12 is glorious — also ruinously expensive to maintain. Twin‑turbo V12s can suffer expensive failures (turbochargers, intercoolers, complex ancillaries). Early‑generation V12s sometimes relied on 5‑speed automatics that were stressed by big torque.  
  - Preventative maintenance: strict oil service every 10,000 km, frequent coolant checks and cooling system refreshes, and insist on full service documentation before purchase.

- Service interval note: For older luxury engines (especially turbo or AMG tune), err on the side of a 10,000 km oil change interval rather than stretching to 15,000 km — it reduces the risk of turbo/cam wear problems and keeps heritage electronics happy.

Transmission
- 7‑speed 7G‑Tronic (722.9 family) is the common auto in pretty much all late‑C216 CLs. It is sophisticated and generally smooth — until it’s not.
  - Known failure modes:
    - Rough or jerky shifts, slipping or failure to go into gear from neutral. A documented repeating cause is the **TCM conductor plate** failure and solenoid pack issues — owner communities and specialist writeups show this as a recurring failure item. [ECU Testing on 7G conductor plate](https://www.ecutesting.com/common-faults/mercedes/7g-tronic-transmission-problems/), [MercedesMedic 722.9 problems overview](https://www.mercedesmedic.com/722-9-7g-tronic-automatic-transmission-problems-solutions/)
    - Torque converter shudder/lock‑up clutch problems — symptoms include judder under load and a feeling of slipping; symptoms can mimic full gearbox failure but sometimes trace to torque converter internals or solenoids. [Torque converter diagnostic guide](https://cartreatments.com/bad-torque-converter-symptoms-and-replacement-cost/)
  - Practical inspection:
    - Test‑drive across multiple gears and loads — a warmed‑up trans should shift cleanly. Look for slip under acceleration and watch the car for delayed engagement when moving off from stop.
  - Repair notes:
    - Transmission control unit (TCU) and conductor plate repairs are specialist jobs; full rebuilds or replacements are very expensive. Independent transmission specialists can offer reconditioning options cheaper than dealer.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mercedes‑Benz CL‑Class?
Short answer: yes — if you accept that you’re buying an engineering flagship with corresponding complexity, maintenance/repair costs, and theft risk mitigations. No — if you need a cheap, worry‑free daily.

Marketplace pricing context & depreciation
- Broader used market: online aggregates show CL‑Class asking prices range wildly depending on year/condition — from low thousands for project cars to >$100k for pristine AMG/V12 examples. Sites show broad listings across markets (CarGurus, AutoTrader, KBB, Edmunds). [CarGurus CL listings](https://www.cargurus.com/Cars/l-Used-Mercedes-Benz-CL-Class-d71), [AutoTrader CL listings](https://www.autotrader.com/cars-for-sale/mercedes-benz/cl-class)
- Classic/collector market: classic market aggregators show average sale values in the low tens of thousands (classic.com aggregates around US$ range ~USD $23k median by one snapshot) reflecting a long‑tail market. [Classic.com CL market](https://www.classic.com/m/mercedes-benz/cl/c216/)
- Depreciation metrics:
  - Mercedes passenger cars generally retain about 40–50% of value after five years according to broad brand data — but the CL is already a specialist older flagship, so typical depreciation has already occurred; expect deeper percentage variance vs segment rivals because AMGs and low‑owner‑count V12s are niche. [CarDepreciation Mercedes data](https://cardepreciation.org/mercedes-benz/)
  - Practical takeaway: you’re buying a car that has done the heavy depreciation already — but you must budget for maintenance that can exceed the purchase price if big items fail (transmission, turbos, V12 repairs).

Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to think about real numbers
- Fuel consumption:
  - Expect ~12 L/100 km combined for a regular CL500/CL550 in real‑world driving; AMGs and V12s are significantly thirstier (single‑figure mpg differences don’t do justice — expect mid‑teens to low‑teens L/100 km for heavy hitters). [Motoreu CL500 combined 12.1 L/100 km](https://motoreu.com/mercedes-cl-500-mpg-fuel-consumption-technical-specifications-166003), [Edmunds cost‑to‑drive figures](https://www.edmunds.com/mercedes-benz/cl-class/2009/review/)
- Insurance, servicing and parts:
  - Expect higher-than‑average insurance for V8/V12 grand tourers. Dealer servicing and replacement modules (radar/COMAND/TCM) are expensive. Independent Mercedes specialists are more cost‑effective for routine work but plan dealership work for ADAS recalibration and software/recall fixes.
- Depreciation vs repair risk:
  - Because the purchase price is already well down from new, paying a little more for a documented, well‑serviced CL with a complete service history is often cheaper long term than a cheaper “as‑is” car that needs a TCU, turbo or major leak fix.
- Hybrid/EV maths:
  - There are no factory hybrid CL models in the C216 line. That makes the question of paying a premium for electrified technology moot — your options are to buy a more modern S‑Class coupe/S‑Class EV alternative. For the C216, the fuel math is simple: expect high fuel costs and schedule parts/servicing into your ownership budget. Sources note no hybrid CL in this generation. [Wikipedia CL-Class overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercedes-Benz_CL-Class)

Buying recommendation — who should buy and which car to pick
- If you want a showpiece weekend GT with presence and are prepared to spend on maintenance and theft prevention:
  - Target: CL500/CL550 (non‑AMG V8) — the best value for the mix of comfort, performance and realistic running costs compared with AMG or V12 cars. The V8 is less terrifying in repair cost than the V12 and cheaper to insure/maintain.
  - Model years: buy a late‑build example (2010–2013) where possible — later cars often have incremental software fixes and updated electronics; however, these command higher prices. Use the cheapest early‑build CL500 (2007–2009) only if the car has a full history and a pre‑purchase inspection (PPI) that clears the gearbox and electronics.
- Avoid unless you have big spare cash:
  - CL63/CL65 AMG and V12 CL600 — intoxicating but expensive to maintain and insure; only buy if you have documented recent work (turbo service, transmission rebuild) and a specialist warranty.
- Specific checklist before signing:
  - Full service history (dealer or independent specialist invoices) covering oil changes every ~10,000 km for turbo/AMG cars.
  - Evidence of TCM/TCU or transmission work if shifting has had faults; test drive to warm temperature and check for shudder/jerkiness.
  - ADAS health: confirm DISTRONIC/radar modules are functional and note if bumper work necessitated recalibration.
  - COMAND screen operation, codecs and every infotainment function tested.
  - Clear title, NSW/VIC PPSR check, VIN verification and explanation for unusually low odometer readings (your NSW listing shows 2,007 km — confirm if odometer was reset or if it’s an imported, low‑use car).
- Final verdict:
  - Buy a CL‑Class if you want a proper grand tourer and can afford the occasional six‑figure repair in the worst case. For most buyers in Australia who want the Mercedes GT coupe experience without daily anxiety, a well‑documented CL500/CL550 from a later year is the sweet spot. Avoid V12 and high‑boost AMG cars unless you have a specialist wallet.
