#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

- Generation(s) covered: the modern Australian Calais badge appears across two distinct late-era families:
  - VF-series Calais (May 2013 – Oct 2017) — the last of Holden’s locally‑developed, rear‑wheel‑drive large cars based on the GM Zeta platform (VE/VF era). [VF Commodore / Calais overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VF)) and Zeta platform background [VE Commodore (Zeta)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE)).
  - ZB-era Commodore / Calais (2018–2020) — the rebadged Opel Insignia‑derived Commodore marketed in Australia; these are front-/all‑wheel drive and are the Calais examples you see for sale from 2018–2020 (and the ones dominating recent NSW dealer listings). See the official recall index for ZB Commodore for model years 2017–2020. [ZB recall list (Brake booster)](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-005475) and ZB-era reviews at WhichCar. [ZB review (WhichCar)](https://www.whichcar.com.au/reviews/holden-commodore-review).

- Product hierarchy: Historically the Calais is Holden’s near‑flagship luxury variant sitting above the mid‑grade Commodore (Calais < Calais V < Berlina/SS/HSV in some series). For VF/VE the Calais was the premium Commodore trim; for ZB the Calais sits below any highest‑trim or HSV‑equivalent carryover in prestige but above base models. [Holden Commodore model history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore).

- Shared platform architecture and common-blood siblings:
  - VE / VF Calais = GM Zeta platform — shared architecture with other GM RWD platforms and many V6/V8 drivetrain bits across VE/VF Commodores. [VE Commodore (Zeta)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE))
  - ZB Commodore/Calais = Opel Insignia-derived foundations (this rebadged architecture explains parts interchangeability with GM/Opel family items in Europe and Asia). ZB recalls and reviews confirm the different architecture for 2018–2020 models. [ZB recall list](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-005475) and general ZB coverage (WhichCar). [WhichCar ZB review](https://www.whichcar.com.au/reviews/holden-commodore-review)

- Market-specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive configurations commonly seen in Australian Calais cars:
  - VF-era Calais (2013–2017):
    - 3.6‑litre V6 — **LFX** (petrol), six‑speed automatic. [VF Calais review/specs](https://australiancar.reviews/review-holden-vf-calais-2013-17/)
    - 6.0‑litre V8 — **L77** (petrol) available in higher‑spec Calais V variants, six‑speed auto. [VF Calais review](https://australiancar.reviews/review-holden-vf-calais-2013-17/)
  - VE-era and historic Calais families included a wide menu (3.0LF1, LY7/LFX V6s, 5.0–6.2L LS‑series V8s) across the decades; see the engine catalogue for the full list. [TotalEngines Calais list](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Holden/Calais)
  - ZB-era (2018–2020) Calais: the late‑model (2018–2020) cars listed in NSW dealers (2017–2020 in the live snapshot) are the rebadged Insignia‑based Calais models with petrol engine choices and automatic transmission only in dealer listings. See ZB recall summary. [ZB recall list](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-005475)

- Explicit marketplace rivals:
  - Ford Falcon (older rival), Toyota Aurion/Toyota Camry (upper mid), large European premium sedans (Holden Calais V often compared to BMW 3/5 series and Mazda 6 in equipment), and late‑era mainstream alternatives like Hyundai Grandeur / Kia Stinger in the near‑luxury full‑size domestic bracket. Compare toolkits and head‑to‑head pages at CarsGuide/Drive. [CarsGuide Calais hub](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/calais) [Drive Calais hub](https://www.drive.com.au/showrooms/holden/calais/)

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden Calais?

- Exterior
  - Panel alignment & body condition:
    - Early VE/VF series are generally well assembled but uneven panel gaps are a red flag for accident repair. Learn to read gaps — Cars24’s PDI guide explains why. [Panel gaps: PDI guide](https://www.cars24.com/article/pdi-panel-gaps-in-cars-ensure-flawless-purchase/)
    - Rust is not exotic: coastal and stone‑chip corrosion are common long‑term issues; expect to budget for paint stone‑chip protection and watch lower sills and wheel arches. [AutoGuru rust repair notes](https://www.autoguru.com.au/repairs/rust-repair/holden/calais)
  - Trim vulnerabilities:
    - Gloss black and piano‑black interior/exterior trims on later Calais and Calais V cars scratch and show finger marks; chrome tends to hold up better on older models.
  - Recalls & safety system faults:
    - ZB‑era Commodore/Calais (2018–2020) had an Australian recall for a potential brake booster failure — check VIN and ensure the dealer performed the remedy. [ZB recall (Brake booster)](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-005475)
    - ADAS: later ZB models offered AEB and lane‑assist packages but owners report the usual AEB limitations (false activations, sensor sensitivity in poor weather). General AEB behaviour and failure modes are documented in trade mechanic analysis — make sure all ADAS sensors and cameras are clean and calibrated after any front bumper repair. [AEB limits article](https://trade.mechanic.com.au/news/the-problem-with-automatic-emergency-braking-systems) and ZB ADAS coverage. [WhichCar ZB review](https://www.whichcar.com.au/reviews/holden-commodore-review)
  - What to check at inspection:
    - Uneven gaps, repaint overspray, wheel‑arch rust near drain holes, stone chip masks missing, and ask for completed recall/repair history (especially ZB brake booster recall).

- Interior
  - Durability & heavy usage (rideshare/taxi):
    - Leather seats on Calais variants generally hold up well according to owner reviews, but rear‑seat wear on frequent rideshare duty shows in documented owner reports. [Owner reviews (Carsales)](https://www.carsales.com.au/owner-reviews/holden/calais/)
  - Entry / exit ergonomics:
    - Calais sits like a traditional large sedan: door sills are mid‑height and door check stops are generous — easy in/out for most users but older owners should check running boards on V8/sport packs.
  - Electrical behaviours:
    - Infotainment: the Holden MyLink / TFT units (VF and some later models) have a known history of freezing, intermittent resets and blank screens — forum threads and specialist repair shops show repeated service/replace cases. If the display freezes or needs multiple reboots, budget $600–$1,200 for a rebuilt/replacement screen (used parts and specialist repair services are common). [MyLink freeze reports (Just Commodores)](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/calais-v-radio-lcd-screen-freezing-up.192651/) [TFT repair services](https://www.aslautomedia.com.au/holden-vf-series-tft-display-screen-repair-or-exch) [LCD repair shop listing](https://getelectronic.net/products/holden-lcd-touch-screen-mylink)
    - 12V battery drain: owners report parasitic draws on older cars if accessories or aftermarket electronics have been fitted — check for battery health and if the vehicle has had a new battery recently.
    - Screen sizes & connectivity: Calais V and Calais models from VF onwards typically offered touchscreen MyLink units (varying with trim) and Bluetooth/USB; wireless charging was optional on later GM platforms and has known reliability quirks. [Wireless charging issues (GM background)](https://ultimaterides.com/chevrolet-wireless-charging-not-working/)
  - What to test:
    - Boot/trunk motor operation, door handles and latches (especially on older VE/VF), all seat power functions, heating/ventilation for rear passengers, and a full infotainment stress test: Bluetooth phone pairing, Apple CarPlay/Android Auto (if present), screen responsiveness, and camera views.

- Mechanically
  - General engineering reputation:
    - VE/VF (Zeta) cars are robust mechanical platforms with well‑understood servicing needs; ZB-era cars change the architecture and therefore the failure profile. [VE/VF overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE)) [VF Calais review](https://australiancar.reviews/review-holden-vf-calais-2013-17/)
  - Security vulnerabilities:
    - There are no large, public Australian campaigns specifically naming CAN‑bus injection exploits for Calais in the supplied search results. That said, any modern car with keyless entry benefits from practical anti‑theft measures:
      - Physical: high‑visibility steering wheel lock, wheel‑clamp if you park on the street.
      - Digital: ensure immobiliser is active, avoid storing spare keys in signal‑leaking pouches at home, fit an aftermarket alarm with tilt sensor if you’ll park in high‑risk areas.
    - Tip: lock the OBD‑II port or fit an OBD block if you park in a high‑theft area (simple and cheap theft-hardeners).
  - What to have a tech check:
    - Full service history, any crash chassis repairs (weld signs), and a dealer or independent scan for historic stored faults.

- Engines
  - Notes: below are the main engine codes and variants discussed in the research results — each subsection is italicised and includes the common failure points and recommended maintenance intervals (Australian servicing patterns: many owners follow 10,000 km service intervals — use that if the car has a service book showing 10k or 15k). Sources: manufacturer pages, owner forums and problem summaries.

  - *3.6‑litre V6 — **LFX***  
    - Known for: the LFX V6 (used on VF Calais) delivers smooth power but owner reports point to timing chain/tensioner wear and occasional cam‑position sensor codes (P0017 family). Forum threads and common‑problems lists document timing‑chain concerns particularly on earlier V6s. [Timing chain reports (Just Commodores)](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/timing-chain-problems-p0017.285878/) [TorqueBot common issues](https://torquebot.io/cars/holden-calais/common-problems)  
    - Failure points: timing‑chain tensioner/guides wear (replacement is costly — engine removal and labour dominate the bill), crank/cam sensors, occasional oil leaks around cam covers.  
    - Preventative maintenance: change oil and filter every 10,000 km with a high‑quality synthetic meeting GM specs; inspect oil pressure and listen for chain rattle on cold start; if the car has >150,000 km and original timing chain, budget for inspection/replacement.  
    - Typical Australian fuel use (real world): expect mid/high teens on mixed driving for older V6 Calais models; see owner fuel logs. [Fuelly Calais logs](https://www.fuelly.com/car/holden/calais)

  - *6.0‑litre V8 — **L77** / LS‑series variants*  
    - Known for: big torque and a muscular soundtrack; later LS‑series V8s are mechanically robust but expensive to maintain if abused. [VE/VF V8 overview and buyers guide](https://www.tradeuniquecars.com.au/holden-ve-sscalais-v8-buyers-guide/)  
    - Failure points: normal V8 wear — cam/valve guide wear if oil neglected, higher fuel consumption, and on older V8s watch for multiple coolant/oil hose aging. Performance modifications and remaps are common in the enthusiast market — check for engine tuning that may reduce longevity.  
    - Preventative maintenance: oil every 10,000 km, coolant and hoses inspected every 40–60,000 km, valve adjustments as specified by the workshop manual for older pushrod units.  

  - *3.0‑litre / 3.6‑litre historical codes — **LF1/LY7***  
    - These cover earlier generation V6s (VE-era and before) — watch for the same timing‑related symptoms and electronics gremlins that increase with age. [VE engines listing](https://www.vecalais.com/ve-engines/)  

  - Notes on service intervals: Australian owner practice is frequently 10,000 km for oil/service; check the stamped service book for adherence. If previous owner used longer intervals (15,000 km) and the car has a high-mileage V6/V8, be suspicious and inspect oil analysis/records.

- Transmission
  - Behaviour and known failure modes:
    - Torque‑converter automatics (6L50/6L80 and other GM 6‑speed boxes in VE/VF and similarly specified automatics in later cars) are generally durable but vulnerable to heat and poor fluid maintenance. Owners report shudder under light throttle at steady speeds, slipping or rev‑hang, and in older units a refusal to engage first gear in Drive (works in M modes). [Transmission problem threads (Just Commodores)](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/ve-calais-trans-problem.308774/) [CarsGuide transmission problems page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/calais/problems/transmission)  
  - Typical symptoms and causes:
    - Shuddering at certain speeds — often failing torque converter or worn clutch packs, sometimes software reflash or torque converter replacement cures it. [Transmission shudder discussion (Just Commodores)](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/help-with-shudder-on-vf-calais-v-s2-2015-v6.301112/)  
    - Slipping/revs without takeup — low fluid, overheated fluid, worn valve bodies or converter.  
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Transmission fluid and filter service every 60–100,000 km (use manufacturer spec fluid), watch for burn smell, and insist on a road test that exercises all drive ranges. If the car has had no documented transmission service, budget for a fluid/filter job immediately.

- Entry price to ceiling price
  - Expect to see the cheapest practical Calais examples (older VE/VF) from private sales around $8k–$20k historically; dealer late‑model Calais V or near‑luxury ZB examples sit at the top ($24,990 to $79,990 in the NSW dealer snapshot). Current online listing overviews (Carsales/Drive/TradingPost) show wide variation depending on year, km and spec. [Carsales Calais listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/holden/calais/) [Drive Calais hub](https://www.drive.com.au/showrooms/holden/calais/)

- Depreciation metrics
  - Real‑world behaviour:
    - Large sedans like the Calais have suffered heavier depreciation through the 2010s as buyers migrated to SUVs. Expect faster percentage drops versus mainstream small SUVs. Older VF/VE Calais examples can be had for dramatically less than late ZB/low‑km dealer stock — example historic data: a 2011 Calais range sat around $8,250–$21,010 in recent valuation windows. [CarsGuide 2011 pricing](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/calais/price/2011)  
  - Practical metric to use:
    - Use current dealer asking (median $67,990 for late‑model low‑km NSW stock) versus market transaction prices on Carsales/Drive to estimate 3‑year depreciation for similar segments. Without hard new‑MSRP comparators in the supplied data, treat Calais as losing 30–50% of value in the first 3–5 years relative to a new price in the late‑2010s large‑sedan bracket — the exact percent depends on trim and rarity (high‑spec low‑km examples hold value better). For a precise calculation use online valuation tools (Carsales valuation / Redbook) and the CostMyCar TCO tool. [Carsales valuation tool](https://www.carsales.com.au/car-valuations/) [Redbook example valuations](https://www.redbook.com.au/cars/details/2007-holden-calais-ve-auto/SPOT-ITM-230735/)

- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Fuel: real‑world numbers (V6/V8):
    - Owner fuel logs and review averages indicate V6 Calais real world mid‑teens L/100km and V8s higher — Fuelly aggregates owner entries and gives you real life logs. [Fuelly Holden Calais logs](https://www.fuelly.com/car/holden/calais)  
  - Servicing & parts:
    - Budget for regular oil and fluid changes every 10,000 km (or as per service book) and factor potential timing‑chain service on some V6s if the car is high‑km or chain history missing. Expect higher dealer labour costs for V8 maintenance and for any infotainment module replacements (screens are a common, non‑cheap item to repair).  
  - Depreciation & insurance:
    - Large sedans in Australia have lower residuals than comparable SUVs; insurance premiums may be lower than prestige European models but account for a higher repair cost if the car has V8 or advanced ADAS components.  
  - TCO tool & comparison:
    - Use the CostMyCar calculator (or the Australian equivalent tools) to run scenarios comparing a petrol Calais V6 vs a hybrid/alternative. There is limited evidence in the supplied results that Holden offered hybrids for Calais in Australia, so paying a hypothetical hybrid premium is normally not applicable; if you’re comparing to a hybrid rival (Toyota Camry hybrid), run the break‑even calculation based on your annual km, local petrol price and likely battery replacement costs. [CostMyCar TCO tool](https://costmycar.com/) [KBB hybrid vs petrol break‑even method](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/)

- Math on paying a premium for alternative powertrains (how to think):
  - If a hybrid equivalent costs $5,000–$10,000 more up front, you need to estimate fuel savings (L/100km difference × annual km × fuel price), maintenance differences and residual value effects. For the Calais/VF/ZB real world fuel figures, you’ll often only break even over many years and very high kilometres — hybrids make most sense if you do lots of suburban stop/start driving and keep the car long term. Use CostMyCar or KBB methodology above to model your circumstances. [KBB hybrid modelling](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/)

- Final buying recommendation — yes/no, and the sweet spot
  - If you want a big, comfortable rear‑seat, proper Australian sedan with relatively simple mechanicals and you prize on‑road presence: buy a Calais — but be selective.
  - Best financial sweet spot (my pick):
    - VF Calais (MY13–MY17) with the 3.6‑litre **LFX** V6, well‑documented service history, and a full stamp‑book: these balance cost, parts availability (Zeta platform spares) and mechanical familiarity. Expect to pay a modest fraction of late ZB dealer prices if you buy private. VF V6s are generally the pragmatic buy if you don’t want V8 running costs and want a RWD driving character. [VF review/specs](https://australiancar.reviews/review-holden-vf-calais-2013-17/)  
    - If you need a near‑new low‑km car and are buying from an NSW dealer snapshot (2017–2020), be prepared to pay a premium (median $67,990). Confirm VIN recall remediation (ZB brake booster) and insist on a dealer PPI (pre‑purchase inspection). (NSW dealer snapshot provided above.)
  - Walk away or negotiate hard when:
    - The car has a frozen/faulty MyLink screen (remember replacement can be $600–$1,200), there’s no service history for timing chain maintenance on the V6, transmission fluid history is missing, or the car has undergone extensive engine mods.
  - Specific recommendation:
    - For most buyers: VF Calais, 2014–2016, **3.6L LFX V6**, well‑serviced, private sale or reputable dealer — it’s the best blend of value vs complexity.
    - For buyers who value late‑model tech and low‑km cleanliness and are happy to pay top dollar: one of the NSW dealer ZB/2018–2020 listings — but buy with full recall/repair evidence and a recent dealer‑performed inspection.
