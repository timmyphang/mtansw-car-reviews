#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

- Generation & codes: This review covers the Holden Colorado sold in Australia across its two main eras — the first-generation RC (2008 launch) and the later RG series (revealed 2011, sold from 2012 through to the late‑2010s; Holden badging lasted until 2020). See the model overview on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Colorado) for the chronology.
- Product hierarchy: The Colorado sat as Holden’s mid‑size ute offering — above smaller (light commercial) models and below full‑size workhorses. In market terms it competed directly with the Toyota HiLux, Ford Ranger and Isuzu D‑Max.
- Shared platform / parts availability: The Colorado is essentially a Thai‑built GM pickup, a rebadged Chevrolet Colorado with shared engineering to the Isuzu D‑Max family in earlier iterations — meaning a decent pool of global parts and specialist knowledge. (See the platform note on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Colorado) and production detail on GM Thailand references.)
- Models using same foundation: Chevrolet Colorado (sibling), early-generation ties to Isuzu Rodeo/D‑Max derivatives; the RG Colorado was produced in Thailand and sold globally in Chevy/GM and Holden guises ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Colorado)).
- Engines, outputs, transmissions and drive layouts (Australian market highlights):
  - Petrol historically: 2.4‑litre four‑cylinder (early/RC era) and the 3.6‑litre Alloytec V6 in some markets (RC-era).
  - Diesel (the important one for Aussie buyers): the 2.8‑litre turbo diesel Duramax (2776 cc — commonly listed as LWN in application tables) across RG models. Early models also carried 2.5L / 3.0L Isuzu diesels in some specs ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Colorado); [TotalEngines list for LWN](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Holden/Colorado/LWN)).
  - Transmissions: manuals and torque‑converter automatics — historically 5‑speed manuals and 4‑speed autos in early RC models, with later diesel pairings updated across the RG run (see the platform/spec notes on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Colorado)).
  - Drive configs: 4x2 and 4x4 dual‑cab and space cab variations were common.
- Marketplace rivals: Toyota HiLux, Ford Ranger, Isuzu D‑Max (primary), Nissan Navara — the familiar ute ring.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden Colorado?

Exterior:
- Panel fit & paint:
  - Panel alignment is generally reasonable given the Colorado’s commercial roots, but used examples show typical ute knocks: scuffs to rear guards, tailgate dents and bumper abrasions. Watch for dodgy resprays where tray or canopy fitment has been done without care.
  - Trim wear: Gloss black plastics and fake‑chrome trim on some higher trims scratch easily and show up under close inspection — common owner reports flagged on community pages and repair services.
  - Paint durability: Australia’s sun and salt will show on older RG examples, particularly on lower‑grade LS/LT models that lack extra protection. Expect stone chips on bullbar/bonnet leading edges.
  - Recalls & safety‑related exterior issues: historical RG recalls include things like missing seatbelt anchor bolts on certain Space Cab VIN ranges — search the consolidated recall lists for specifics and VIN ranges ([australiancar.reviews recall summary](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-rg-colorado-cab-chassis-2012-20/); national recall lookup via [vehiclerecalls.gov.au](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/)).
- ADAS & safety sensors:
  - If you’re looking at late‑model GM twins (post‑2018/2019 onwards in other markets), be aware of software sensitivity issues around AEB/front camera modules — GM issued reprogramming remedies for certain newer Chevrolet Colorados when unwanted AEB events were possible ([NHTSA recall A232424660](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2024/RCRIT-24V133-0199.pdf)). The Holden RG sold in Australia through to 2020 is less ADAS‑dense than the latest Chevy trucks, but sensor reliability and camera calibration are still worth checking after any body repair (see GM Driver Assistance Systems guide on proper recalibration: [GM Parts tech guide](https://www.gmparts.com/content/dam/gmparts/na/us/en/index/technical-resources/technical-documents/gm-driver-assistance-systems-aid.pdf)).

Interior:
- Cabin wear & use‑case reality:
  - The Colorado’s interior is hard‑wearing but not immune. Rideshare/taxi style heavy use accelerates wear to seat bolsters (cloth on LS/LT trims), plastic scuffs on door cards, and fading controls. Higher trims (LTZ/Z71) with leather surfaces fare better but watch for sagging leather on high‑kilometre examples.
  - Entry/exit: dual‑cab models have decent sill heights for the category — not a low‑slung SUV, but not a full‑height truck either. Door check angles vary by model year; check the rear‑door swallow on space cabs (some owners have reported weaker catches).
- Electricals & infotainment:
  - MyLink head unit reliability is the single most‑reported annoyer: frozen screens, unresponsive touch, flicker and phantom touches are common enough that specialist repair services exist ([MyLink touchscreen repair references](https://getelectronic.net/products/holden-lcd-touch-screen-mylink); [Ultimate Electronics listing for repair](https://ultimateelectronics.com.au/index.php/holden/colorado)).
  - Screen sizes: earlier LS/LT models had a 7‑inch MyLink while LTZ/Z71 often came with the 8‑inch unit and sat‑nav — verify physical screen size and functionality before buying ([Carsales “what you need to know” summary](https://www.carsales.com.au/editorial/details/holden-colorado-what-you-need-to-know-105779/); [CarsGuide trim breakdown](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/colorado)).
  - Connectivity: Apple CarPlay / Android Auto are present on mid/up trims but can be flaky with older firmware — owners often opt to replace the factory head unit for better wireless/fast pairing (see aftermarket swap guides and owner threads: [CJ Industries MyLink replacement guide](https://www.cjindustriesaustralia.com/blogs/head-unit-upgrade-guides/replacing-the-factory-mylink-head-unit-in-a-holden-colorado-how-to-decide-which-head-unit-to-choose)).
  - 12V battery drain: older examples can show parasitic draws after aftermarket accessories (canopies, alarms, fridges) are added. Battery and charging system checks are essential on test drive.
  - Wireless charging issues: a newer GM bulletin for 2023–2025 Chev Colorado cites intermittent wireless charger faults — not directly the Holden RG lineup, but an indicator that charging/tray electronics across the Colorado family can be temperamental ([NHTSA TSB on wireless charger 2025 bulletin](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2025/MC-11019461-0001.pdf)).

Mechanically:
- Brand engineering reputation: The Colorado (RG) sourced from GM Thailand performed well as a work ute — rugged platform but with diesel aftertreatment complexity and a few niggling electricals. Expect a robust chassis and good towing hardware; the tradeoff is the complexity in late‑life emission systems.
- Security vulnerabilities:
  - Modern keyless/immobiliser attacks (relay, OBD and more advanced CAN injection) affect many vehicles. Technical coverage of CAN bus injection explains how attackers can inject messages and potentially bypass immobilisers if they access wiring (headlight area hacks have been publicised). For a practical counter, consider a CAN‑aware aftermarket immobiliser and physical deterrents ([CAN injection technical explainer](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/); news coverage of the problem: [SecurityWeek / Autoblog articles linked in search results]).
  - Australian aftermarket solution recommendation: IGLA markets a CANBUS‑capable immobiliser designed to mitigate modern theft methods — highly recommended for periods when the vehicle is left unattended in higher‑risk areas ([IGLA Australia](https://igla.com.au/)).
  - Practical buys: a good quality OBD‑port blocker, steering column lock as passive deterrent, and an aftermarket alarm/immobiliser that integrates with the CAN bus are sensible.
- Electrical oddities:
  - Cruise control and ABS sensor interplay has been reported (cruise intermittently disengages or refuses to engage) — check for outstanding fault codes and watch the dash for RELATED ABS or traction‑light history ([owner forum reports and CarsGuide problems page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/colorado/problems)).

Engines:
- Note on engine codes: the sources most commonly reference the RG diesel as the 2.8‑litre Duramax (often catalogued as LWN in parts lists). Older RC models also offered petrol and Isuzu diesel options. I’ll tackle each engine variant mentioned in the search results; each subsection is italicised for clarity.

  *LWN / 2.8‑litre Duramax turbo diesel (2776 cc)*  
  - Typical fitment: RG Colorado 2012–2020 across 4x2/4x4 and auto/manual variants ([TotalEngines LWN listing](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Holden/Colorado/LWN)).  
  - Common failure points:
    - Emissions hardware: EGR valves blocking and DPF (diesel particulate filter) regeneration/failure are recurring owner complaints. Clogged EGR helps cause rough idle, smoke and limp modes ([SPETUNER 2.8 Duramax guide](https://spetuner.com/blogs/news/2-8-duramax-common-problems-how-to-fix); TorqueBot & MechanicVoice roundups).
    - Turbocharger issues: turboactuator faults, boost leaks and turbo wear showing as power loss, whining or smoke.
    - Aftertreatment sensors and wiring faults: O2/NOx sensors and DPF pressure/temp sensors can trip warnings and push the engine into reduced power.
    - Fuel system & HPFP: while not as widely reported as EGR/DPF problems, high‑pressure fuel system issues can appear if maintenance is neglected.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Service every 10,000 km is the Holden‑recommended cadence for late RG models — follow the MY19 RG Owner’s Manual service tables for exact items ([Holden MY19 RG Owner's Manual](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/2408003/Holden-My19-Rg-Colorado.html)).
    - Use manufacturer‑spec diesel oil and top up with quality low‑ash oil for DPF longevity.
    - Regular short/highway drives to allow DPF regen cycles; if the vehicle is used only for short trips or heavy towing, fit an exhaust temperature monitor and adopt scheduled active regen / occasional longer drives to highway speeds.
    - EGR cleaning interval: inspect and clean around 100–150k km (more often if city/towing use).
    - Turbo health check and intake hose inspection at each service (10k).
  - Practical red flags when buying: persistent DPF lamp, evidence of frequent forced regens, smoke under acceleration, or a dealer service history without any DPF/EGR interventions on a high‑km diesel.

  *Alloytec / 3.6‑litre V6 (petrol, RC era)*  
  - Application: earlier RC‑era Colorado variants and other GM models (not the dominant Aussie diesel choice). See GM 3.6L coverage for known HF V6 issues ([GM 3.6L problems overview](https://www.carsandwrenches.com/gm-3-6l-engine-problems-top-8-problems-is-it-that-bad/)).
  - Failure points:
    - Typical HF‑series problems such as timing-chain tensioner wear (varies with exact HF generation), oil consumption on some examples, and early‑life sensor faults.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Standard service intervals of 10,000 km for oil/filter changes, earlier if towing heavy loads.
    - Watch for oil leaks around the front cover and valve‑cover gasket as the engine ages.

  *2.4‑litre four‑cylinder petrol (early RC)*  
  - Application: entry petrol for early RC models.
  - Failure points: generally straightforward, but check for intake manifold gasket leaks, sensor faults, and premature wear if the ute has been heavily used.
  - Preventative maintenance: conventional 10k oil changes; inspect timing/drive belts per the manual (varied by year).

  *2.5‑litre and 3.0‑litre Isuzu diesels (earlier RC lineage)*  
  - Application: some early RC Colorado / Rodeo derivatives and Isuzu‑sourced platforms.
  - Failure points: older Isuzu diesels have their own known issues in older engines — injector wear and turbo seals are common owner notes. Because parts overlap with Isuzu networks, specialist support exists.
  - Preventative maintenance: follow the specific engine manual if the vehicle has one of these older diesel units; generally, 10k oil services and injector checks around 120k km.

Transmission:
- Behaviour & known failure modes:
  - Automatics: Torque‑converter auto models have been flagged for shudder/lock‑up issues on some years — a torque‑converter lockup shudder that shows as vibration during cruise or low‑speed acceleration has been reported by owners and independent mechanic summaries ([MechanicVoice listing of transmission shudder/failures](https://mechanicvoice.com/holden-colorado-problems-by-year/)).  
  - Manuals: mechanically robust but watch for clutch wear on high‑towing or city stop/start use.
  - Technical detail: early RC models used 4‑speed autos in some markets and 5‑speed manuals; later RG diesels were paired with more modern auto/manual gearboxes (see the general transmission notes on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Colorado)). If you’re buying an automatic, watch for delayed downshifts, harsh engagement, slipping, or limp‑home behaviour.
- Preventative measures:
  - Transmission fluid and filter services as per the manual — automatic boxes often need inspection/service by 60–100k km depending on load usage; insist on a recent ATF change if the truck has been used for high towing or heavy duty.

Summary of diagnostic failures to watch (quick checklist):
- Exterior:
  - Panel respray/repair evidence, tailgate alignment or dent repair.
  - Recalls outstanding (seatbelt anchor campaigns on some Space Cab VINs).
- Interior/electrical:
  - Frozen/unresponsive MyLink screen, phantom touch, audio faults.
  - Unexplained battery drain, aftermarket wiring done poorly.
- Engine:
  - DPF/EGR warnings, turbo noise/smoke, limp modes on 2.8 diesel.
  - Oil leaks and excessive oil consumption on petrol HF engines.
- Transmission:
  - Torque‑converter shudder, delayed engagement (autos), clutch slip (manuals).
- Security:
  - No aftermarket immobiliser and keyless‑entry vulnerabilities; evidence of tampering around headlights or OBD port.

Broader market context:
- Nationwide listing sites (Carsales, Drive) show volumes of RG and RC examples available, with mid‑late‑2010s dual‑cabs commonly trading in the low‑to‑mid‑$20k range depending on kilometres and spec ([Carsales listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/holden/colorado/); [Drive listings](https://www.drive.com.au/cars-for-sale/search/all/all/holden/colorado/)).

Depreciation metrics:
- Baseline: Colorados depreciate more than Toyota HiLux but better than some lesser‑known rivals; Hilux median used pricing (as a benchmark) sits higher (Car‑Scout Hilux median ~ $37,993 — useful to gauge relative value) ([Car‑Scout HiLux median](https://car-scout.com.au/price/toyota/hilux)).  
- Typical drop: expect a 3‑year/36%+ initial reduction off new list prices historically for mid‑size utes; the Colorado—because Holden was retired as a brand in 2020—has an extra wrinkle: badging and parts sourcing perceptions can push private sale prices down compared to current Toyota or Ford equivalents. Use DriveIndex or depreciation trackers if you want a model‑specific percentage over time ([DriveIndex depreciation tracker](https://driveindex.com/depreciation)).

Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy:
- Fuel: Diesel dual‑cab Colorados are typically around the mid to high single‑digit to low double‑digit litres/100 km depending on load. CarsGuide lists consumption figures with examples from ~8.6 L/100 km for some dual cab configs — real world towing and urban driving will add litres ([CarsGuide consumption figures](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/colorado/fuel-consumption)).
- Servicing: use the MY19 RG Owner’s Manual schedule — nominal services at 10,000 km are typical; factor in higher costs for diesel aftertreatment diagnostics and occasional turbo/EGR jobs ([Holden MY19 RG Owner's Manual](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/2408003/Holden-My19-Rg-Colorado.html)).
- Repairs to watch: DPF/EGR/turbo interventions can be costly if ignored (a DPF replacement or turbo job will blow a small hole in the service budget). Infotainment head unit repairs are commonly needed on older examples — budget $800–$2,000 for replacement or high‑quality aftermarket install if you care about modern smartphone integration ([MyLink repair services](https://getelectronic.net/products/holden-lcd-touch-screen-mylink); [CJ Industries head‑unit swap guide](https://www.cjindustriesaustralia.com/blogs/head-unit-upgrade-guides/replacing-the-factory-mylink-head-unit-in-a-holden-colorado-how-to-decide-which-head-unit-to-choose)).
- Insurance & registration: as a ute, premiums are usually comparable to rivals; check insurer historic premiums if the vehicle has modifications or is used for business/towing.
- Towing & resale: Colorado is a capable tow vehicle (some RG specs list 3,500 kg braked towing and 6,000 kg GCM on certain specs — see the MY19 RG manual towing tables for exact figures) ([Holden MY19 RG Owner's Manual towing specs](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/2408003/Holden-My19-Rg-Colorado.html)).

Hybrid/EV vs conventional diesel math:
- There are no factory Holden Colorado hybrids/EVs in Australia to compare in the used market. Modern Chevy Colorado iterations in other markets now include high‑performance petrol turbo options and future electrified variants, but for a used Australian purchaser of a Holden‑badged Colorado your realistic choices are diesel or older petrols.
- Conclusion: paying a market premium for a rare hybrid/EV sibling (where available) might make sense over a 7–10 year horizon if you do very high urban stop/start driving and can access cheap off‑peak charging; for a ute used for towing and mix of highway trips, the diesel Colorado still makes practical financial sense in fuel/torque terms. Use a fuel‑savings calculator to model your exact kms — CarsGuide/official fuel figures can be a baseline ([CarsGuide fuel figures](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/colorado/fuel-consumption)).

Buying recommendation — short, sharp and Australian:

- Entry price/ceiling right now (NSW dealer snapshot): AUD $13,900 – $41,888, median AUD $26,990 (2013–2020 spread). Use that as your negotiating baseline.
- Best buy sweet spot: mid‑2016 to 2018 RG dual‑cab 2.8‑litre diesel automatic in LT or LSX spec with a full service history and ~80–140k km on the clock. Why? You’ll avoid some of the earliest teething RC issues, get the preferred diesel torque curve, and still be in a mileage range where major components (turbo, DPF) aren’t yet inherently overdue if properly serviced.
- Red flags to walk from:
  - Diesel examples with no documented DPF/EGR maintenance history and persistent dash warnings.
  - Automatics exhibiting torque‑converter shudder, delayed engagements or lots of slipping feel — insist on a dealer diagnosis or avoid.
  - Vehicles with dodgy electricals (dead MyLink screen, unexplained lights) unless price reflects repair costs.
- When to pay a premium:
  - If the truck has dealer stamped logbooks to 100k km and a recent service that included DPF scan/turbo inspection, a modest premium is worth it. A documented recent turbo/DPF service is gold.
- Security setup on day one:
  - Fit an aftermarket CAN‑aware immobiliser (IGLA or similar), OBD port lock and a physical steering lock if you live in a high‑theft postal area. These are cheaper than losing your ute and dealing with insurance hassles ([IGLA](https://igla.com.au/); CAN injection explainer for risk context: [Kent Indell writeup](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/)).

Final verdict:
- If you need a proper mid‑size work ute that tows and carries with reasonable economy, a well‑maintained Holden Colorado 2.8‑litre diesel is still a smart used buy — on condition you do your homework. There are niggles: DPF/EGR/turbo and infotainment headaches top the list. Pay for service history, perform a proper pre‑purchase inspection focused on the turbo and emissions system and secure the vehicle against modern theft techniques. If you’re chasing best‑in‑class resale and lowest headaches (and can stomach paying more), an equivalent Toyota HiLux will cost more up front but likely save you worry. For value and capability, the Colorado remains a good, practical, Aussie ute — just bring a torch, a service history and a healthy dose of scepticism.
