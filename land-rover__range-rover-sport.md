#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation, timeframe & codes: The current Range Rover Sport you’ll see in Australia is the third-generation L461 (introduced globally in 2022), sitting on the modern Range Rover Sport line that superseded the L494 (2013–2022) and the original L320 (2005–2013). See the generational overview on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_Rover_Sport) and the model pages at Land Rover for the current line-up [rangerover.com](https://www.rangerover.com/en-us/range-rover-sport/index.html).
- Product hierarchy: The Range Rover Sport sits under the full‑size Range Rover (the flagship) but above the Velar and Discovery families in Land Rover’s showroom ladder — it’s the sporty, more driver‑biased Range Rover rather than the full‑size lounge-on-wheels. (Manufacturer pages summarise the model positioning: [Range Rover Sport models & specs](https://www.rangerover.com/en-us/range-rover-sport/models-and-specifications.html).)
- Platform & parts availability: The Range Rover Sport uses Land Rover’s latest technical architecture for current Range Rover models (details and exact engine/transmission pairings are in Land Rover technical PDFs for 2024/2025) — that means parts and specialist expertise are widely available across the JLR dealer network and independent Land Rover specialists in Australia. See the 2024 / 2025 technical specifications for engine and drivetrain details ([2024 spec PDF](https://www.landrover.com/content/dam/lrdx/pdfs/xi/wltp/Land-Rover-Range-Rover-Sport-TD-Insert-1L4612500000XXEN02P.pdf), [2025 spec PDF](https://www.landrover.com/content/dam/lrdx/pdfs/xi/wltp/Land-Rover-Range-Rover-Sport-TD-Insert-1L4612600000XIEN01P.pdf)).
- Market-specific engines, outputs, transmissions & drive:
  - Petrol mild‑hybrid inline‑6 (3.0-litre) options: commonly labelled in marketing/spec pages as P360 / P400 / P530 / P635 depending on tune; all pair to an 8‑speed automatic and AWD in Australian market specification pages ([rangerover.com](https://www.rangerover.com/en-us/range-rover-sport/index.html), [Edmunds 2025 specs](https://www.edmunds.com/land-rover/range-rover-sport/2025/features-specs/)).
  - 5.0‑litre supercharged V8 options (higher-performance SVR/SV variants) — petrol V8s remain available in some markets.
  - Diesels (where offered in earlier model years): TDV6 / SDV6 variants were common in prior L494 lineups — diesel availability in Australia has diminished in newest line-ups but used imports and older stock exist.
  - Plug‑in Hybrids (PHEV): P400e / P460e / P510e variants with significant electric range claimed on WLTP cycles (manufacturer and technical sources list PHEV options) — PHEV models still pair with the 8‑speed auto and AWD ([Auto‑Data P460e specs](https://www.auto-data.net/en/land-rover-range-rover-sport-iii-3.0-p460e-460hp-plug-in-hybrid-iawd-automatic-50787), manufacturer tech specs).
- Marketplace rivals: BMW X5, Mercedes‑Benz GLE, Porsche Cayenne, Audi Q7, Genesis GV80 — if you’re shopping this space, those are the usual suspects to compare it with ([The Car Connection competitors list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/land-rover_range-rover-sport_choices)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Land Rover Range Rover Sport?

Exterior
- Panel alignment & trim:
  - Older L494 cars can show loose or peeling gloss‑black pillar and roof trim and A‑pillar/panoramic roof mouldings — common enough that aftermarket guides document repair and replacement techniques ([Go‑Parts Garage trim guide 2013–2022](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-land-rover-range-rover-land-rover-range-rover-sport-2013-2022-2)).
  - SV carbon‑look clearcoat wrinkling / peeling on carbon packs has a documented technical bulletin and repair guidance (see NHTSA/TSB note) — if the car has a carbon pack, inspect closely for clearcoat defects ([NHTSA TSB on SVR carbon hood clearcoat](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2023/MC-10231382-0001.pdf), SSM75913 summary [TSB search](https://www.tsbsearch.com/Land-Rover/SSM75913)).
- Paint durability & recalls:
  - Owners report roof/clearcoat peeling in threads and some recall/TSB attention has followed; always check VIN recall history. Aggregated recall listings show a range of campaigns across years ([Cars.com recalls summary](https://www.cars.com/research/land_rover-range_rover_sport/recalls/), recall summary sources).
- ADAS/sensor vulnerability:
  - Forward radar, blind‑spot radar modules and AEB systems have user‑reported failures. Some ADAS faults have been resolved with software updates, others require sensor replacement and calibration. NHTSA/recall entries and owner complaints show AEB/driver‑assist interventions have seen software recalls or TSBs in certain years ([NHTSA recall N227 AEB bulletin 2018](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2018/RCMN-18V625-1892.pdf); aggregated ADAS complaints in 2025 summary [carsafetydb](https://carsafetydb.com/land-rover/range-rover-sport/2025/driver-assist)). Expect sensor replacement costs to run into the low thousands if hardware is required — radar modules aren’t cheap and need dealer calibration.

Interior
- Durability under heavy use:
  - Cabin finishes are high quality but susceptible to wear: gloss trim scratches, scuffs on lower door cards and seats when used in rideshare or for dog duty — not unique, but repair/rehab is costly given luxury materials.
  - Doors and entry: high ride height and wide door sills make entry/exit easy for the tall and elderly but watch for door check wear on high‑kilometre examples — hinge/striker wear makes a Range Rover feel “loose” after lots of use.
- Electrical & infotainment:
  - Centre screens and infotainment have a history of blanking, ghost touches and resets across many JLR vehicles; Land Rover issued a recall for touchscreen failures affecting some early model years of the mid‑2020s family ([CarComplaints recall summary on touchscreen failures](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2025/land-rover-touchscreen-failures-recall.shtml)). Independent write‑ups cover replacement/programming costs and common failure modes ([Go‑Parts JLR infotainment failures](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/infotainment-display-land-rover-range-rover-sport-land-rover-range-rover-jaguar-e-pace-2015-2026)).
  - Connectivity hiccups: USB/Wireless charging faults and intermittent Apple CarPlay/Android Auto disconnects have troubleshooting threads and repair guides — look for updated Pivi Pro software builds (owners report OTA updates like Pivi Pro 4.3.3 improved wireless charging and stability) ([forum thread on Pivi Pro 4.3.3 improvements](https://www.rangerovers.net/threads/wireless-charing-improvement-with-pivi-pro-4-3-3.371405/), USB port fixes [Go‑Parts USB guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/usb-port-land-rover-range-rover-evoque-land-rover-range-rover-velar-land-rover-range-rover-2017-2024)).
  - 12V battery drain & downstream effects: low battery or parasitic drains can cause ADAS components to go offline and trigger multiple system warnings — troubleshooting pages for “Emergency Brake Assist not available” list low voltage as a common cause ([RangeRoverFanatic troubleshooting](https://rangeroverfanatic.com/troubleshooting/range-rover-emergency-brake-assist-not-available/)). If the 12V system has been allowed to go flat repeatedly, expect sensor recalibration and modules to misbehave.

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation: Land Rover builds impressive off‑road and luxury capability, but owner reliability tracking shows above‑average incidence of electrical and expensive mechanical failures compared with German rivals; Consumer Reports and owner databases reflect that modern Range Rovers require attentive servicing ([Consumer Reports overview](https://www.consumerreports.org/cars/land-rover/range-rover-sport/), TrueDelta problem aggregation).
- Theft & security vulnerabilities:
  - Modern Range Rover Sport models are attractive targets for both relay attacks and advanced CAN‑bus injection “headlight hacks”. Technical write‑ups and security analyses highlight the technique and its applicability across multiple marques, Range Rover Sport included ([CAN Injection attack paper & notes](https://www.can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf); general coverage on headlight hacking and mitigation recommendations [Autoblog coverage](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update); targeted article naming Range Rover Sport among popular targets [Specialist Trackers blog](https://www.specialist-trackers.uk/blog/headlight-theft-and-can-bus-injection-protecting-your-vehicle-in-2026)).
  - Mitigation: install a Thatcham‑rated tracker and a CAN‑aware immobiliser (products like the Autowatch Ghost Pro claim to add a PIN/immobiliser layer for Land Rover models) — these are recommended if you value keeping the thing on your driveway ([Autowatch Ghost Pro product information](https://www.carsoundsecurity.com/product/ghost-pro-dual-can-bus-immobiliser-by-autowatch-for-land-rover-fully-fitted/)).

Engines
- Below are italicised engine subsections for the common units you’ll encounter. Each item lists documented weak points and recommended preventative servicing intervals (practical guidance for Australian use: consider 10,000 km oil/service intervals where the vehicle has a heavy duty cycle — shorter intervals are better for longevity).

  - *Bold: **P360 / P400 / P530 / P635 (3.0L inline‑6 petrol MHEV)***
    - Typical issues: timing‑chain guide wear (on some variants), and in high‑output tunings, supercharger/intercooler cooling or oil-feed concerns can appear on neglected cars. The 3.0 MHEV range generally benefits from regular oil changes with manufacturer‑grade oil.
    - Preventative service: oil & filter at least every 10,000 km (more frequently under hard use); inspect chain tensioner and listen for cold‑start rattles; follow dealer software updates for hybrid management. Manufacturer technical specs list these engines and MHEV techs ([Land Rover technical PDFs](https://www.landrover.com/content/dam/lrdx/pdfs/xi/wltp/Land-Rover-Range-Rover-Sport-TD-Insert-1L4612600000XIEN01P.pdf)).

  - *Bold: **5.0L V8 (supercharged, SVR/SV variants)***
    - Typical issues: supercharger coupler/drive rattle, cooling system leaks, and higher running costs. Guides show common supercharger coupler faults across JLR V8s ([Go‑Parts supercharger rattle guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/turbocharger-land-rover-range-rover-sport-land-rover-range-rover-land-rover-defender-2013-2023)).
    - Preventative service: rigorous cooling system inspection every 20,000 km, oil & filter 10,000 km, and attention to supercharger noises at cold start — fix early, supercharger repairs are expensive.

  - *Bold: **TDV6 / SDV6 diesels (2.0–3.0L diesel variants)***
    - Typical issues: timing‑chain or guide wear (especially on later Ingenium diesels), DPF regeneration failures causing oil dilution and chain stress — K Motors & EngineFinders document timing chain risk on 2.0 diesels and 3.0 diesels with guide wear ([K Motors common faults](https://kmotors.co.uk/common-faults/range-rover-sport-l494/), [EngineFinders timing-chain analysis](https://enginefinders.co.uk/blog/range-rover-engine-longevity-tdv6-sdv6-p400-real-mileage-data/)).
    - Preventative service: oil & DPF diagnostic check every 10,000 km; keep diesel duty cycles active (regular motorway trips) to allow DPF passive regen; change oil promptly after faulted regen events.

  - *Bold: **P400e / P460e / P510e (PHEV variants)***
    - Typical issues: PHEVs introduce battery‑related service, high‑voltage cooling system complexities and additional software systems. PHEV electrified range claims are large (manufacturer/WLTP figures vary widely) and real‑world range depends on battery state and charging habits ([Auto‑Data P460e technical page](https://www.auto-data.net/en/land-rover-range-rover-sport-iii-3.0-p460e-460hp-plug-in-hybrid-iawd-automatic-50787), driving reviews).
    - Preventative service: follow high‑voltage system inspection at 20,000 km intervals and ensure battery charge cycles are maintained; keep PHEV-specific software updated. If you can actually charge daily, PHEV ownership reduces fuel spend; otherwise the complexity and late‑life battery replacement costs are considerations (see TCO discussion).

Transmission
- The Range Rover Sport uses modern ZF‑type 8‑speed automatics and related torque‑converter installations. Common failure modes across Range Rover history include torque‑converter shudder, valve‑body/mechatronic faults and judder/hesitation symptoms which often trace back to fluid degradation or mechatronic wear. Repair shops and technical write‑ups document these traits and typical fixes ([allautomatic ZF 6HP article](https://www.allautomatic.co.uk/post/torque-converter-issues-in-the-zf6hp26-faults-reconditioning-for-uk-gearbox), [ASR Gearbox repairs overview](https://www.asrgearboxrepairs.co.uk/range-rover-sport-common-gearbox-problems/), [YouCanic Land Rover transmission problems](https://www.youcanic.com/land-rover-automatic-transmission-problems/)).
- What to check on pre‑purchase:
  - Smooth, consistent shifts with no delay or shudder from cold.
  - No transmission fluid smell (overheating) or obvious fluid leaks.
  - Service records that include transmission fluid and filter changes; if no records, budget for a fluid service and diagnostic scan.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Land Rover Range Rover Sport?

Market snapshot (NSW dealer data)
- Live MTA NSW scraper snapshot: Active NSW listings 7; Price range AUD $166 – $284,990; Median price AUD $76,990; Years 2014–2026; Median odometer 55,580 km; Transmission: Automatic 7. Treat the low $166 entry as a data outlier — likely salvage/title sale or a listing error — and expect normal used‑market floors to be tens of thousands for late L494 examples and well above $70k for recent L461 / low‑km PHEV or V8 cars.

Depreciation & value
- Expect heavy early depreciation: long‑term resale studies show Range Rover Sport examples can lose roughly 60–65% of new value over five years depending on spec and market. CarEdge and iSeeCars data put five‑year depreciation north of 60% in historic samples ([CarEdge depreciation overview](https://caredge.com/land-rover/range-rover-sport/depreciation), [iSeeCars comparison vs BMW X5](https://www.iseecars.com/compare/land_rover-range_rover_sport-vs-bmw-x5)). In practice that’s why you can buy a very desirable spec 2017–2019 Sport for much less than new‑money would suggest — but you trade risk of expensive repairs.

Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to think about running costs
- Running costs you’ll actually feel:
  - Servicing & dealer labour: Range Rovers prefer specialist attention. Annual servicing (software updates, calibration, brake & suspension checks) will likely be more expensive than German rivals — budget higher labour and parts costs at Main Dealer or specialist.
  - Tyres, brakes and suspension: heavy luxury wheels and air suspension (if fitted) are costly when they fail — expect air‑spring and compressor repairs in high‑km cars.
  - Fuel vs PHEV math: PHEVs can be economical if you plug them in and do most short‑run urban duty. Manufacturer/WLTP and independent sources show dramatic cycle economy improvements for PHEVs when charged — examples: WLTP/technical pages for PHEV claim very low l/100km equivalents and long pure‑electric ranges on certain PHEV variants ([Auto‑Data P460e technical listing](https://www.auto-data.net/en/land-rover-range-rover-sport-iii-3.0-p460e-460hp-plug-in-hybrid-iawd-automatic-50787), media/road‑test coverage). But if you can’t charge regularly, the added mass of the hybrid system makes fuel economy worse in mixed duty cycles.
- Practical TCO calculation strategy:
  - Step 1: Run the baseline (IC engine) fuel use you expect from your typical week (percentage urban vs highway) using the petrol/diesel numbers in reviews (Parkers and Carbuyer list typical mpg ranges by engine family) ([Parkers running costs](https://www.parkers.co.uk/land-rover/range-rover-sport/review/mpg-running-costs/), [Carbuyer on running costs](https://www.carbuyer.co.uk/land-rover/range-rover-sport/mpg-running-costs-co2)).
  - Step 2: Model PHEV benefits only if >50% of your driving can be done on charged battery cycles — otherwise you pay for the extra complexity and weight with little fuel saving.
  - Step 3: Add servicing/known repair risk premiums (air suspension, turbo/supercharger work, transmission mechatronics) — if you’re not comfortable with surprise bills, add $2k–$4k/yr buffer depending on spec and mileage.
  - Use the manufacturer technical pages and independent reviews as inputs for fuel/EV‑range assumptions ([Auto‑Data, MPGCalculator, DrivingElectric reviews/specs]).

Concrete buying guidance (clear, blunt)
- If you want the Range Rover Sport for the badge and capability and can afford the upkeep, it’s a cracking car. If you’re value‑conscious and hate surprises, be blunt: buy one with a full dealer service history, get a pre‑purchase inspection from a Land Rover‑specialist with factory‑level diagnostic gear, and budget for the known expensive bits.
- Price band & sweet spot (based on NSW snapshot and broader market behaviour):
  - Bargain/Value pick: Late L494 (2016–2019) with 80k–150k km — much cheaper to buy but you must accept likely air‑suspension and potential engine/timing‑chain or transmission servicing soon. Watch for clear service history and DPF/DPF‑related repairs.
  - Best financial sweet spot: Mid‑life L494 refreshed models (2018–2021) with documented 2017 mid‑cycle updates (improved touchscreen/tech) — they combine a discount to new money but fewer early‑life bugs ([J.D. Power model history highlights](https://www.jdpower.com/cars/history/land-rover/range-rover-sport)). NSW median price of AUD $76,990 and median odometer 55,580 km suggests many listings are late‑model L494 or early L461 cars — a good used target is a low‑to‑mid‑$70k car with under 80k km and full service history.
  - If you want the latest tech and are prepared to pay: L461 (2022+) and PHEV or V8 models keep their pace and tech but command premium prices close to the new vehicle market. Buy certified pre‑owned with warranty if you go this way.
- Specific thumbs‑up / thumbs‑down:
  - Buy (with conditions): Well‑serviced PHEV if you can charge daily and prioritise lower running fuel cost and a near‑new tech stack. Get an extended battery/EV system warranty or ensure the unit has adequate remaining manufacturer coverage.
  - Buy used, avoid surprise: 3.0 petrol MHEV with full dealer history and recent service (10,000 km intervals recommended) — avoids diesel DPF drama and reduces timing‑chain extremes.
  - Walk away (unless price is spectacularly low and you’re a mechanic): High‑km older diesel L494s with vague service records, cars with recorded air‑suspension faults but no repairs, cars with multiple ADAS or infotainment warnings in the OBD history.

Pre‑purchase checklist (non‑negotiable)
- Full dealer/service history covering oil, timing‑chain/engine repairs, transmission fluid service.
- Check for ADAS fault history and that sensors/cameras calibrations are current.
- Ask for PHEV battery health and charge history if applicable.
- Lift & visual: inspect for clearcoat peeling (carbon pack), peeling pillar/roof trim and snapped gloss trims (common on L494), and check for bumper/radar damage.
- Security: fit a Thatcham‑rated tracker and CAN‑aware immobiliser (Autowatch Ghost Pro or equivalent) if you keep it on the street — Range Rover Sport is targeted by modern theft methods ([Specialist Trackers summary](https://www.specialist-trackers.uk/blog/headlight-theft-and-can-bus-injection-protecting-your-vehicle-in-2026), [Autowatch Ghost Pro](https://www.carsoundsecurity.com/product/ghost-pro-dual-can-bus-immobiliser-by-autowatch-for-land-rover-fully-fitted/)).

Verdict — final call
- If you accept the cost of ownership and pick wisely (full history, specialist pre‑purchase check, sensible trim/engine choice for your duty cycle), the Range Rover Sport gives a mix of on‑road refinement, off‑road ability and real showroom presence you won’t get from the Germans. If you’re counting pennies and want worry‑free reliability, pick a BMW X5 or Genesis GV80 and sleep easy.
- Optimal financial sweet spot for NSW buyers: hunt in the AUD $60k–$90k band for late‑L494 refreshed models (2017–2021) with 40k–100k km and documented service. If you can stretch and want the tech and (potential) fuel savings, buy a low‑km L461 PHEV but insist on warranty/vehicle health checks.
