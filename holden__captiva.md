#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation(s), production timeframe & internal codes
  - Holden Captiva (Australia): built from 2006–2018 across two main localised families — the original CG (2006–2011) and the mid‑life refreshed CG2 / Series II (roughly 2011–2017, with a late 2016 refresh before the end of local sales). Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Captiva), [australiancar.reviews CG2](https://australiancar.reviews/review-holden-cg2-captiva-2011-17/).
- Where it sits in the Holden line-up
  - Mid‑sized family crossover / soft‑roader. Available as five‑seat (Captiva 5) or seven‑seat (Captiva 7) packaging. It was Holden’s mainstream seven‑seat option competing with bigger mainstream SUVs rather than premium SUVs.
- Shared platform and sister models (parts availability benefit)
  - Built on GM’s Theta platform (shared broadly across GM compact/mid SUVs). That means many mechanical bits and body components are shared with international Chevrolet Captiva / Opel Antara derivatives — useful for sourcing spares. See: [Chevrolet Captiva / Theta platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chevrolet_Captiva).
- Engines, outputs, transmissions and drive layouts sold in Australia
  - Petrol:
    - 2.4L four‑cylinder (various LE/LD family variants) — common mid‑range option.
    - 3.0L V6 and later 3.2L V6 **(GM High Feature / Alloytec / HFV6 family)** on higher‑spec models.
  - Diesel:
    - 2.0L (Fiat/VM sourced) and 2.2L GM VCDi diesels on later/selected models.
  - Transmissions:
    - 5‑ and 6‑speed automatic torque‑converter boxes; 6‑speed manuals offered in early/lower‑spec variants. Most NSW dealer listings are automatic. See CG spec summaries: [Automobile‑Catalog](https://www.automobile-catalog.com/model/holden/captiva_holden.html), [Chevrolet Captiva technical page](https://www.auto-data.net/en/chevrolet-captiva-model-1623).
  - Drivetrains:
    - Front‑wheel drive (FWD) and optional four‑wheel drive / AWD on certain trims and engine combinations.
- Primary marketplace rivals (Australia)
  - Mainstream family seven‑seat / mid SUV rivals include Toyota Kluger, Nissan Pathfinder, Mazda CX‑9, Kia Sorento and Ford Territory (the usual suspects in Drive’s family SUV roundups). See Drive comparison context: [Drive family SUV comparison](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/family-suv-comparison/), and CarsGuide comparison pages: [CarsGuide Captiva hub](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/captiva).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden Captiva?
This is the buy‑with‑your‑eyes‑wide‑open section. Break it down.

- Exterior
  - Panel fit and finish
    - Early CG cars show inconsistent panel gaps between doors and tailgate; later CG2 improved trim alignment but quality is variable by build. Owner reports and photo threads document this as a common used‑car surprise. See owner problem aggregators: [StartMyCar — body & paint issues](https://www.startmycar.com/holden/captiva/problems/body-paint-exterior).
  - Paint and clearcoat
    - Clearcoat peeling and dulling is frequently reported on older Captivas, especially cars that have spent a lot of time in sun (Australia being Australia). Typical culprits: UV degradation and chemical etching — not unique to Captiva but common on mid‑2000s paint specs. General diagnosis: [EngineerFix clearcoat article](https://engineerfix.com/why-is-my-clear-coat-peeling-and-how-do-i-fix-it/).
  - Trim vulnerabilities
    - Gloss black plastics and chrome trim on higher trims scratch and fade with time. Expect scuffs on bumper edges and roof rails on high‑use family cars.
  - Recalls and safety actions
    - Captiva had multiple field actions and recalls across CG and CG2 lifecycles (fuel‑filler/fuel-feed hose campaigns and other targeted repairs). Always check the VIN for outstanding campaigns before purchasing: [australiancar.reviews recalls — CG2](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-cg2-captiva-2011-17/) and [australiancar.reviews CG](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-cg-captiva-2006-11/).
  - ADAS / safety electronics
    - Older Captivas are light on modern ADAS by today’s standards. Where electronic safety aids exist (parking sensors, cameras), owner reports show intermittent sensor/camera faults and false warnings — i.e., systems degrading with age or suffering wiring faults. General context on false AEB activations in automotive systems: [BrakeandFrontend ADAS faults](https://www.brakeandfrontend.com/adas-false-and-failed-corrections/). Aggregated owner complaints point to sensors and wiring rather than core sensor logic in many cases: [CarCareArena — issues summary](https://www.carcarearena.com/holden-captiva-issues/).

- Interior
  - Durability under heavy use (rideshare, family, dog hair)
    - The Captiva’s cabin is roomy but many owners report premature wear on seat bolsters, fading plastics and creaks on high‑kilometre cars. ProductReview owner sentiment is a useful sanity‑check here: [ProductReview Captiva listings & owner comments](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/holden-captiva).
  - Entry/egress ergonomics
    - Relatively conventional door sills and seat heights — comfortable for most adults and children — but the third row (on Captiva 7) is tight and better for kids. Door check angles are sensible but check rear door hinges for sag on high‑km cars.
  - Electrical oddities and battery drain
    - The Captiva has a track record of finicky electricals: intermittent radio/navigation failure, blower motor cut‑outs, reverse‑camera glitches and alternator/charging complaints. These problems can lead to parasitic drain on afflicted cars or a partially‑dead 12V battery if left standing. See StartMyCar electrical threads: [StartMyCar — electrical system issues](https://www.startmycar.com/holden/captiva/problems/electrical-system).
  - Infotainment and connectivity
    - Head unit hardware varies a lot by year and trim — older cars have simple radios/monochrome displays; mid‑to‑late CG2 may have touchscreens. Repeated owner reports show freezing/blank screens and intermittent Bluetooth/connectivity failures are common — typical remedies range from software resets to dealer reprograms. Consumer‑level troubleshooting guidance is useful: [Consumer Reports infotainment reset advice](https://www.consumerreports.org/cars/automotive-technology/how-to-reboot-your-cars-infotainment-system-a1056723043/).

- Mechanically
  - Brand engineering reputation (general)
    - The Captiva is a globally built GM‑platform vehicle. It’s not a Rolls; expect competent engineering but not the same long‑term bulletproof reliability of the best Japanese rivals. Problems are heavily weighted toward electrical and transmission failures in used‑car reporting. See CarsGuide problem aggregation: [CarsGuide Captiva problems](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/captiva/problems).
  - Theft & security vulnerabilities
    - Like many modern cars, Captivas are vulnerable to sophisticated keyless/CAN bus attacks in the wild (CAN injection/headlight hacks are documented methods thieves use to spoof key validation). These are platform/architecture risks rather than a Holden‑only problem. Recommended countermeasures: mechanical steering lock, alarm with tilt/impact sensing, and a deliberately installed RFID Faraday pouch or steering‑column immobiliser for longer‑term parking. Read more on CAN injection risks: [The Register overview of CAN bus theft](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/) and technical primer: [CAN‑CIA documentation](https://can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf).

- Engines
  - Notes: each italicised engine code/variant below covers the common Australian units, the usual failure points and recommended service cadence. Always ask the seller for a full service history and a copy of the owner’s handbook for correct intervals: [Holden Captiva Owner’s Handbook (CG)](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/3031517/Holden-Cg-Captiva.html).

  - *2.4L four‑cylinder (LE5 / LE9 / LD9 family variants)*
    - Typical faults: moderate oil consumption on high‑km cars, ignition coil and spark plug wear, thermostat and cooling‑system leaks. The 2.4 is serviceable but not invincible — regular oil changes and monitoring for coolant loss are key.
    - Preventative maintenance: service every 10,000 km for high‑use cars (or stick to the owner manual if seller provides a full history). Replace spark plugs and ignition coils around 100,000 km as a preventative measure.

  - *2.2L VCDi diesel*
    - Typical faults: DPF blockage, EGR valve soot build‑up, turbo actuator and sensor failures. Diesel Captivas can be very economical on long motorway runs but costly if the DPF or EGR require cleaning/replacement after repeated short‑trip use. Specialist write‑ups: [Craig Jones Auto on DPF problems](https://craigjonesauto.com.au/holden-captiva-dpf-problems/).
    - Preventative maintenance: diesel owners should do longer runs to allow passive regeneration; consider forced DPF regen at 100,000+ km intervals if short trips predominate. Service oil & filter every 10,000–15,000 km depending on oil spec and use.

  - *2.0L diesel (VM / Fiat sourced in some model years)*
    - Typical faults: earlier iterations were reported to suffer major internal failures on a small number of engines (rocker arm / needle roller bearing problems noted for some 2.0 diesels in Australia). See recall/fault history: [australiancar.reviews CG recalls](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-cg-captiva-2006-11/).
    - Preventative maintenance: strict oil change intervals (10,000 km recommended) and careful inspection of the service history. Avoid a car without a full documented service record.

  - *3.0L / 3.2L V6 (LF1 / LFW / LU1 — GM High‑Feature / Alloytec family)*
    - Typical faults: variable cam phasing and phaser actuator wear, ignition module/coils, and the usual age‑related gaskets/oil leaks. The HFV6 engines are generally stronger for mid‑range torque and towing but demand attention to regular oil changes and valve‑train checks — failing these, cam phaser wear can cause roughness and warning lights. See GM HFV6 overview: [GM High Feature engine on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GM_High_Feature_engine).
    - Preventative maintenance: conservative service interval of 10,000 km for heavy/urban use; ensure timing‑related components were inspected and any factory service campaigns were applied.

- Transmission
  - What’s fitted and what goes wrong
    - Most Australian Captivas use torque‑converter automatics (5‑ or 6‑speed depending on year) — these are the repeatedly‑reported trouble spot in the platform. Issues include harsh or delayed shifts, torque‑converter shudder, ATF leaks (torque‑converter seal) and eventual internal wear if leaks go untreated. See transmission problem roundups and official actions: [CarsGuide gearbox problems summary](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/captiva/problems/transmission), [Maurice Blackburn class action on Holden transmission defects](https://www.mauriceblackburn.com.au/class-actions/join-a-class-action/holden-transmission-class-action/), and practical repair notes on torque converter seal replacement: [AutoGuru repair data](https://www.autoguru.com.au/repairs/torque-converter-seal-replacement/holden/captiva).
  - Known failure modes
    - Torque converter lock‑up clutch failure — shudders, grabby or slipping feel on lock‑up, transmission codes stored.
    - Electro‑hydraulic valve body and speed sensor faults — delayed or harsh shifts.
    - Leaks at the torque converter seal that require major teardown to repair.
  - Buyer checks & remedies
    - Pre‑purchase: road test across all shift ranges, listen for clunk or shudder on torque‑converter lock‑up (usually around 40–80 km/h), inspect for ATF leaks under the engine bay and bellhousing area, and scan gearbox codes.
    - Cost mitigation: a healthy, documented ATF service history improves odds. If the car has fresh ATF and still shudders, assume major work may be coming.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Holden Captiva?
- Marketplace snapshot (NSW dealer data)
  - Live snapshot (MTA NSW scraper): Active listings 14; Price range AUD $6,450 – $14,990; Median price AUD $9,990; Year range 2008–2016; Median odometer 75,777 km; Transmission mix — Automatic: 10, Sports Automatic: 2. (Data supplied in the purchase brief.)
  - Market listings elsewhere show similar ranges: example listing summary and pricing context at Carsales and Drive: [carsales Captiva search results](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/holden/captiva/) and [Drive Captiva used inventory](https://www.drive.com.au/cars-for-sale/search/all/all/holden/captiva/).
- Typical depreciation behaviour
  - The Captiva has lost favour with buyers because of a reliability/quality reputation — that translates into steeper depreciation than some Japanese rivals. Industry tools show Captiva/chevrolet Captiva holding less of their original value into the mid‑late life phase. See depreciation references: [AutoPadre Captiva depreciation curve](https://www.autopadre.com/depreciation-curves/chevrolet-captiva-sport) and [TheMoneyCalculator depreciation forecasts](https://www.themoneycalculator.com/vehicle-finance/calculators/car-depreciation-by-make-and-model/CHEVROLET/CAPTIVA/).
  - Practical takeaway: expect faster loss of value than a similarly aged Toyota Kluger/Hyundai Santa Fe/Kia Sorento. Buyers anchor expectations accordingly — cheap purchase prices in exchange for the probability of higher repair bills later.
- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Fuel economy baseline and real‑world figures
    - Factory and tested economy varies: published figures across the Captiva range span roughly 7.9–10.7 L/100 km depending on engine and drivetrain. CarsGuide lists typical consumption starting around 8.8 L/100 km for common petrol options; diesel variants can be significantly more frugal in steady‑state driving, but only if DPF/EGR systems are healthy. See [CarsGuide fuel consumption page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/captiva/fuel-consumption) and real‑world aggregation [Fuelly Captiva reports](https://www.fuelly.com/car/holden/captiva).
  - How to think about diesel vs petrol
    - Diesel pros: better real‑world L/100 km on long motorway usage — attractive for high‑km drivers.
    - Diesel cons: DPF, turbo and EGR servicing/cleaning costs can swamp fuel savings if you mostly do short urban trips. Specialist DPF guidance: [Craig Jones Auto DPF problems & fixes](https://craigjonesauto.com.au/holden-captiva-dpf-problems/).
    - Practical rule: if you do >20,000 km/year with steady highway runs, a well‑maintained diesel can repay itself. For <15,000 km/year with lots of short trips, petrol is the safer wallet choice.
  - Service & repair cost expectations
    - High‑cost areas: transmission rebuilds/torque converter, DPF regeneration and V6 cam/timing work. Routine items (brakes, suspension bushes, tyres) are market‑standard. Warranty is unlikely on these older Holdens — budget an additional contingency of $1,500–$3,000/year for unexpected repairs on high‑km examples.
  - TCO calculation strategy (how you do it)
    - Step 1: pick your annual km and realistic fuel price. Step 2: use real‑world L/100 km from Fuelly or CarsGuide for the engine variant. Step 3: add annual scheduled servicing cost (owner manual and local dealer/indie quotes). Step 4: allocate a repair contingency based on model year and service history (higher for no service history). For guidance on hybrid vs petrol break‑even math, use the KBB approach on break‑even calculations: [KBB hybrid vs gas break‑even](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/).
- Buying recommendation — who should and shouldn’t buy a Captiva?
  - Short answer
    - Buy if: you want cheap seven‑seat utility, you’re comfortable doing mechanical checks or using an independent specialist, and you accept a higher‑than‑average maintenance risk in exchange for a low purchase price.
    - Don’t buy if: you want a near‑zero‑stress family car, you cannot tolerate potential transmission or electrical repairs, or you do not want to manage diesel DPF risks on high‑use/high‑traffic urban driving.
  - Specific recommended model years / trim (value sweet spot)
    - Best financial sweet spot: CG2 cars from about 2012–2015 (post‑refresh) — they generally combined improved engines/transmissions and a better feature set while still being cheap in the used market. Your NSW median listing of AUD $9,990 and median 75,777 km aligns with a 2013–2015 CG2 example — a practical buy if it has a full service history.
    - Trim advice:
      - Prefer a well‑maintained 2.4L petrol for mixed urban/highway duty if you want low hassle.
      - Consider a 2.2 diesel only if the car has long‑distance servicing evidence and the DPF/EGR history is clean.
      - Avoid V6s unless you require towing performance and the example has a documented history of regular oil servicing (V6s are more expensive to fix).
  - Pre‑purchase checklist (pragmatic, non‑negotiable)
    - Full service history to manufacturer intervals (or better).
    - VIN check for outstanding recalls/campaigns via GM / recall databases and australia recall lists: [australiancar.reviews recalls summary](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-cg2-captiva-2011-17/).
    - Test drive across the city and highway to exercise torque‑converter lock‑up (listen for shuddering/slipping).
    - Scans for stored ECU and TCM codes (electrical and transmission trouble codes).
    - Inspect DPF health on diesels — ask for evidence of forced regeneration or a recent clean if city‑use is dominant.
    - Visual check for wetness/oil leaks under the torque‑converter bellhousing area.
    - If buying from a NSW dealer: use your consumer rights (MTA guidelines) and ask for dealer inspection documentation.
- Final practical verdict
  - The Captiva is a pragmatic used‑car buy when priced sensibly. At the median NSW dealer price you provided (AUD $9,990, ~75k km) a CG2 Captiva with a clean service history and a benign transmission report is a decent value family wagon if you accept: (a) modest cabin refinement, (b) higher electrical/transmission risk than class leaders, and (c) the need for regular preventative maintenance. If you’re not a DIY‑capable owner or you want low‑stress ownership, step up to a more reliable residual specialist (Toyota/Kia/Honda) even if you pay more up front.
