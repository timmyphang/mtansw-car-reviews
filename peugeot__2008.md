#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

The second-generation **Peugeot 2008** (AU internal designation: P80) arrived locally in late 2020 as a complete architectural overhaul of its predecessor, replacing the aging B0-platform hatch-based crossover with the ground-up PSA **CMP (Compact Modular Platform)**. This architecture deliberately shares structural underpinnings, suspension geometry, and modular wiring harnesses across the Stellantis group, meaning you’ll find identical subframes, door modules, and sensor clusters sitting inside the DS3 Crossback, Vauxhall Corsa, and Opel Mokka. That cross-brand parts pooling is your biggest advantage when sourcing panels, electronic control units, and brake calipers outside manufacturer circuits. 

Within the French marque’s hierarchy, the 2008 slots neatly below the larger **3008** and **5008** SUVs, positioning itself as the entry-level premium compact crossover. It trades vertical volume for a lower centre of gravity and sharper dynamic intent, deliberately angling at urban professionals, young families, and rideshare operators who want European build quality without sedan boot depths.

Australian specification focuses heavily on front-wheel drive configurations to maximise cabin footprint and simplify drivetrain maintenance. The primary powertrain offered through authorised Peugeot channels is the **1.2-litre PureTech turbocharged three-cylinder**, producing 81kW / 205Nm mated exclusively to a six-speed torque converter automatic. Higher-volume trims run the **Allure** and **GT** specifications, bundling progressive damping, multi-mode Grip Control, and full ADAS suites. As demand for electrified commuter vehicles accelerates, newer grey imports and parallel channels have introduced the **Hybrid 145 (e-DSC6)** mild-hybrid and the fully electric **e-2008**, though domestic dealer stock remains predominantly petrol-driven. 

Market rivals are fierce and numerically saturated. The 2008 directly contests the **Hyundai Kona**, **Mazda CX-3**, **Volkswagen T-Cross**, **MG HS**, and the **Toyota Corolla Cross**. Unlike its Japanese counterparts that prioritise reliability blandness, the 2008 bets everything on tactile interior ergonomics, distinctive styling cues, and a chuntery-but-characterful engine character. #### 2. What goes wrong with a used Peugeot 2008?

#### Exterior
Panel alignment is generally consistent, but the 2008 suffers from common French panel-gap tolerances that widen over time due to thermal expansion cycles and minor impact fatigue. Gloss black roof rails and lower cladding strips scratch catastrophically easily; one gravel storm and you’ve got visible swirl chains. Chrome accents on base models oxidise and trap road grime, demanding regular polishing or heat-gun straightening after minor contact. Paint durability sits around industry average, though the bonnet leading edge consistently chips without protection film. 

ADAS hardware integration is largely competent but notoriously picky about calibration. The forward-facing camera mounted behind the windscreen top-centre, the grille-mounted radar, and the wing-mounted blind-spot emitters rely on precise factory alignment. Any bumper replacement, windscreen swap, or even severe pothole impacts can throw lane-departure warnings off their axis. Later firmware updates improved low-light pedestrian detection thresholds, but owners report aggressive false-positive braking on heavily shaded roads or when approaching dark-coloured trucks. Adaptive cruise control also struggles in heavy rain, reverting to basic speed limiting rather than maintaining gap distance until conditions clear.

#### Interior
The i-Cockpit layout is polarising by design. The ultra-low, flat-bottomed steering wheel intentionally sits beneath the instrument binnacle, but taller drivers (>180cm) will inevitably get the upper rim cutting into their sightline of the digital speedo. Shorter drivers usually enjoy the unobstructed view, creating a classic ergonomic divide. Window sill heights are pronounced; entry and exit require a deliberate twist rather than a casual step-in, which becomes fatiguing after 200,000+ km for elderly passengers.

Electrical behaviour centres on the 12V architecture. Infotainment hardware varies significantly by trim: base Active models carry a smaller resistive screen requiring wired Apple CarPlay/Android Auto, while Allure and GT variants install a 10-inch capacitive display with wireless projection. Connectivity reliability is solid once paired, but prolonged short-trip commuting (<15 km per journey) frequently drains the 12V auxiliary battery. The head unit fails to cycle deep-sleep modes properly, leaving CAN-bus modules awake and bleeding amperage overnight. Parking assist cameras function but render a noticeably soft, pixelated image at night; glare washing out reversing guidance on dark carparks.

Storage practicality exposes classic French prioritisation of aesthetics over convenience. The centre console features a rolling soft-touch lid that collapses under lateral elbow pressure, a documented failure point after moderate use. Door bins lack dedicated bottle retention loops, meaning 500ml containers slide and rattle during cornering. Rear climate vents sit flush in the front dashboard, delivering adequate airflow but zero adjustable direction. Parcel shelf mounting brackets fatigue quickly, allowing the tray to slide and vibrate against the liftgate rubber stops.

#### Mechanically
Peugeot’s broader engineering philosophy leans towards compact packaging and weight optimisation, which delivers sharp turn-in and compliant ride quality, but occasionally sacrifices long-term component accessibility. Security vulnerabilities skew toward digital exploits rather than traditional hotwiring. Modern keyless-entry push-button systems are vulnerable to RF relay attacks and CAN-bus message injection, where thieves amplify the fob signal within 30 metres or inject authentication spoof commands to unlock doors and start the engine. Countermeasure strategy is straightforward: store keys in a passive RFID-shielding pouch, fit a secondary immobiliser kill-switch behind the kick panel, and consider a disc brake lock for high-theft suburbs. #### Engines
Each powertrain variant carries distinct engineering signatures, wear patterns, and maintenance philosophies. Diagnostic scrutiny must begin here, as engine health dictates the entire vehicle’s lifespan.

##### *1.2L PureTech Turbo (TU3A/TU3J)*
The workhorse of the Australian fleet. Produces 81kW / 205Nm in local trim, featuring a direct-injection turbocharger with variable valve timing. The critical vulnerability is the **wet timing belt**. Unlike conventional dry belts, this polymer-reinforced toothed belt runs submerged in engine oil. Over time, the friction-facing coating softens and sheds microfibres, accumulating in the oil pickup strainer and starving the high-pressure fuel pump, cam bearings, and turbo shafts of lubrication. Factory service protocols initially delayed intervention, causing widespread sludge blockages. Current PSA guidance mandates inspection/replacement at 5 years or 100,000 km, alongside an updated water pump design that reroutes coolant flow away from belt exposure. Service interval sits strictly at **10,000 km or 12 months**, whichever arrives first. Oil specification requires fully synthetic 0W-30 meeting ACEA C2 standards. Neglecting fluid changes accelerates timing belt degradation exponentially.

##### *1.5L BlueHDi Diesel*
Primarily exported to Europe and select grey-import markets. Features common-rail direct injection, twin-scroll turbocharging, and a diesel particulate filter (DPF) managed by exhaust temperature manipulation. Urban commuting below 50 km/day prevents passive DPF regeneration cycles, forcing manual active burns that consume extra fuel and increase soot loading. AdBlue dosing pumps freeze in alpine winters and crack housing seals in tropical humidity. Service interval remains **10,000 km**. Recommended only for high-kilometre highway commuters who understand emissions system thermodynamics.

##### *1.5L Hybrid 145 (e-DSC6)*
Mild-hybrid configuration pairing the 1.2L turbo with a 48V integrated starter-generator (ISG) and lithium-ion buffer battery. Electrical support assists low-speed acceleration and enables brief coasting shutdowns. Software-controlled power management dictates drivability; outdated ECU calibration causes laggy throttle mapping and erratic torque blending during gear shifts. Cooling vent filters located upstream of the power electronics radiator collect highway dust rapidly, restricting airflow and triggering derating warnings. Maintenance follows the **10,000 km** schedule, requiring special attention to 48V battery terminal torque checks and inverter coolant level verification.

##### *e-2008 Electric Powertrain*
Single-speed reduction gearbox fed by a 50 kWh NCM lithium pack rated for roughly 200–220 km real-world mixed driving. Charging accepts up to 100 kW DC input, but peak acceptance tapers aggressively past 80% state of charge. Thermal management relies on a dedicated liquid cooling loop for the traction inverter and motor stator; degraded coolant or trapped air pockets cause rapid depowering under sustained uphill loads. Battery cell balancing drifts faster if subjected to frequent SuperCharger sessions; home AC charging (7–11 kW) preserves cell longevity. Warranty coverage applies, but third-party repair pathways remain limited, making out-of-warranty pack replacement financially prohibitive. Service interval extends to **15,000 km or 24 months**, reflecting reduced mechanical friction points. https://www.drivingelectric.com/peugeot/2008/range

#### Transmission
The six-speed automatic employed in the 1.2 PureTech models utilises a conventional hydraulic torque converter with lock-up clutch engagement occurring typically above 70 km/h. Shift progression is smooth, avoiding the jerkiness common in competitor dual-clutch assemblies. The box holds third gear comfortably during overtaking maneuvers and seamlessly modulates torque delivery to prevent turbo lag from translating into driveline shock. Fluid life is marketed as “sealed for life,” but authoritative mechanic consensus strongly advises draining and refilling synthetically rated ATF every **60,000–80,000 km**, filtering contaminants before they abrade planetary gear sets and valve bodies. Pressure regulator valves show wear patterns around 120,000 km, manifesting as mild shudder during 3rd-to-4th transitions or delayed engagement from Park to Drive after cold starts. Sludge-free fluid changes permanently resolve 90% of premature wear symptoms. https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/2021-peugeot-2008-review/

Older first-generation iterations equipped with the 1.6L naturally aspirated engine featured a four-speed automatic that struggled significantly. Valve body carbon tracking, sluggish shift solenoids, and inadequate torque multiplication created acceleration times exceeding 11 seconds to 100 km/h, along with persistent overheating in mountainous or towing scenarios. These mileage-heavy examples should be avoided outright.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Peugeot 2008?

Current marketplace realities dictate that patience pays dividends. Live NSW dealer snapshot data confirms active listings sit between **AUD $6,984 – $47,888**, establishing a median transacted price of **$23,990**. Year coverage spans **2018 – 2024**, with median odometer readings clustered around **65,145 km**. The transmission mix is entirely automatic, reflecting how buyers universally rejected early manual variants due to stiff clutch actuation and poor low-speed creep refinement. 

Depreciation metrics reveal a steep initial curve typical of European imports entering a segment dominated by Japanese stalwarts. Expect a baseline **45–50% value drop** over the first three years compared to core segment rivals like the Hyundai Kona or Mazda CX-3. However, depreciation flattens considerably thereafter as perceived reliability catches up and specialist dealer networks establish robust refurbishment pipelines. Paying premium prices for early high-mileage imports usually offers diminishing returns; waiting for the three-to-four-year mark captures maximum tech features at minimum financial penalty.

Total cost of ownership calculations strongly favour the conventional **1.2L PureTech turbo** over electrified alternatives for average Australian usage patterns. Factoring in fuel consumption, the 1.2L automatic consumes approximately **7.2–7.8 L/100km** in blended urban/highway cycling, translating to roughly **$55–$60 per 100 km** at prevailing unleaded prices. Electric equivalents require upfront premiums exceeding $12,000 AUD, with public charging infrastructure fees eroding any theoretical savings unless home solar PV generation covers daily replenishment. Hybrid 145 models offer marginal CO2 reductions but introduce complex 48V electrical fault diagnosis pathways that independent workshops still struggle to access. Mathematically, unless you clock >35,000 km annually predominantly on highways, the ICE variant wins the TCO spreadsheet decisively.

**Buying recommendation:** Target model years **2021–2023** equipped with the confirmed revised **1.2L PureTech wet belt and water pump overhaul**, preferably in **Allure** or **GT** trim for optimal sensor calibration and infotainment hardware. Verify service logs meticulously—missing the 10,000 km oil change window triggers warranty voidance and accelerates timing belt disintegration. Prioritize vehicles with verified ADAS recalibration certificates post-windscreen/bumper work. Avoid grey imports lacking transparent Australian compliance badges or dealer-backed transferable warranties. The financial sweet spot currently resides around **$22,000–$26,000 AUD** for under 80,000 km examples, where depreciation has already been absorbed by earlier owners, leaving you with a mechanically sound, technologically competent crossover that rewards attentive buyers while penalising negligent ones.
