#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation & codes
  - The Range Rover you’re looking at is a full‑size luxury SUV spanning two recent chapters: the fourth‑generation **L405** (2012–2021) and the current, fifth‑generation **L460** (revealed 2021, on sale from model year 2022+). The L460 is the contemporary machine in the showroom and the one with the latest tech and electrified powertrains. See the L460 overview [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_Rover_%28L460%29).
- Where it sits in the brand lineup
  - Flagship full‑size luxury SUV for Land Rover — above the Range Rover Sport and below only bespoke SV Coachbuilt specials. Trims typically run SE → HSE → Autobiography → SV (top end) depending on year and market spec. Official trim breakdowns are on the Range Rover model pages at [rangerover.com](https://www.rangerover.com/en-xi/range-rover/models-and-specifications.html).
- Shared platform and parts availability
  - The L460 rides on JLR’s MLA‑Flex architecture (Modular Longitudinal Architecture) which also underpins higher‑end Jaguar/Land Rover products and allows a mix of inline‑six, V8 and PHEV setups — good news for parts sharing and aftermarket support. That platform note is on the L460 wiki and technical specs linked above.
- Market‑specific powertrains, transmissions and drive layouts (Australia)
  - Combustion and hybrid mix in recent model years:
    - Petrol straight‑six mild‑hybrids (e.g., badged **P400**, **P530**).
    - V8 petrols (higher output variants, P615/SV).
    - Plug‑in hybrid variants (badged **P460e**, **P550e** / PHEV family) with battery packs and an electric range (WLTP/claimed figures differ by year).
  - All use longitudinal engines with AWD and an 8‑speed automatic (ZF family) in modern cars — JLR documentation and spec inserts for 2024/2025 list those options ([Land Rover technical spec PDFs](https://www.landrover.com/content/dam/lrdx/pdfs/xi/wltp/Land-Rover-Range-Rover-TD-Insert-1L4602500000XXEN01P.pdf), [rangerover.com specs](https://www.rangerover.com/en-xi/range-rover/models-and-specifications.html)).
- Key rivals in the Australian market
  - Porsche Cayenne / Cayenne Turbo, BMW X7, Mercedes‑Benz GLS, Audi SQ7/RSQ8, Cadillac Escalade and Genesis GV80. Comparison listings and competitor roundups are covered by The Car Connection and CarGurus ([The Car Connection competitor page](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/land-rover_range-rover_choices)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Land Rover Range Rover?

Exterior
- Panel gaps & trim fitment
  - Recent owner reports show inconsistent panel alignment and rattles on some MY24+ cars — owners reporting door/trim gap issues on forum threads and build‑quality threads (e.g., RangeRovers.net). These are cosmetic but annoying and sometimes costly to fix if a door needs realignment ([forum reports](https://www.rangerovers.net/threads/my24-build-quality-issues.361510/)).
- Gloss trim and clearcoat
  - Gloss black pillar trims and roof finisher pieces on the L405 era (and some later models) can peel, fade or rattle loose; aftermarket or dealer replacements are commonly required after a few years in harsh sun. Go‑Parts documents these recurring exterior trim failures ([Go‑Parts trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-land-rover-range-rover-land-rover-range-rover-sport-2013-2022)).
- Panoramic roof / sunroof finisher recall
  - A safety recall (2026 North America) flagged that a panoramic sunroof side finisher may be insufficiently secured and can detach — an example of how a trim/fastener fault escalates to a safety recall. Check by VIN at Land Rover’s official recall lookup ([Land Rover recall search](https://www.landroverusa.com/ownership/vin-recall.html)) and the recall summary ([aboutautomobile recall report](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Safety-Recall/2026/Land-Rover/Range-Rover/26V097000)).
- ADAS sensor vulnerability
  - Radar sensors and front cameras live behind bumpers and grills. When these get misaligned, obstructed or electrically faulty the car disables ACC, AEB and lane systems — owners see “Emergency Braking not available” or similar messages. This behaviour is well‑documented in ADAS troubleshooting guides and complaint aggregators ([Go‑Parts ADAS guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/collision-avoidance-sensor-land-rover-discovery-land-rover-range-rover-sport-land-rover-range-rover-2017-2023), [CarSafetyDB driver assist complaints](https://carsafetydb.com/land-rover/range-rover/2024/driver-assist)).

Interior
- Infotainment black screens & software quirks
  - A significant fleet of JLR vehicles hit a centre‑screen blanking fault that prompted a recall and software update (recall N972) after thousands reported blank/black touchscreens at startup or random reboots. It’s not rare; ask for proof of the software remedy in dealer service history or VIN recalls before buying ([CarComplaints coverage of the recall](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2025/land-rover-touchscreen-failures-recall.shtml)).
  - Pivi Pro/Touch‑screens: freezing, camera loss and reboots are well documented in owner guides and aftermarket writeups — fixes range from software reflash to replacing entire head units at cost ([Go‑Parts infotainment guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/audio-system-land-rover-range-rover-sport-land-rover-range-rover-land-rover-lr4-2010-2024)).
- USB/phone charging and CarPlay issues
  - USB ports and wireless chargers can fail or under‑deliver current, leading to CarPlay dropouts. A JLR technical bulletin is referenced in technician guides — these are cheap fixes if harness or port swaps are needed, but factory modules can require dealer programming ([Go‑Parts USB/charger guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/usb-port-land-rover-range-rover-evoque-land-rover-range-rover-velar-land-rover-range-rover-2017-2024)).
- Cabin durability under heavy use
  - Leather and soft‑trim hold up well in normal use but in rideshare/taxi or heavy family duty expect scuffs on lower door sills, creasing on seat bolsters and occasional squeaks from door checks. The high‑tech cabin is less forgiving than old‑school metal.

Mechanically
- General engineering reputation
  - Range Rovers are brilliant when they’re right — and complex and expensive when they’re not. Expect higher repair bills than a mainstream SUV. Aggregated complaint sites and reliability summaries show electronics, cooling and suspension faults are common cost drivers ([CarComplaints Range Rover page](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Land_Rover/Range_Rover/), [OwnerKeep reliability report](https://ownerkeep.com/vehicles/2025-land-rover-range-rover)).
- Theft and security vulnerabilities
  - Modern Range Rovers have been targets for CAN‑bus headlight/plug attacks and keyless hacks where thieves inject fake messages onto the vehicle network to simulate a present key. Research pieces and security reporting show the risk is real for high‑value SUVs — JLR has been rolling out software hardening and owner advice ([The Register on CAN injection](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/), [SecurityWeek coverage](https://www.securityweek.com/thieves-use-can-injection-hack-to-steal-cars/), [JLR press on security updates](https://media.landrover.com/en-gb/news/2024/06/jlr-accelerates-roll-out-security-updates-uk-600)).
- Recommended anti‑theft mitigations
  - Layered approach: instal a Thatcham S5 compliant tracker, mechanical steering‑wheel lock or deadlock, immobiliser & VIN‑tagging for resale deterrence; keep key fobs in a Faraday pouch. Specialist vendors and JLR both recommend these interim steps ([Specialist‑Trackers headlight hack guide](https://www.specialist-trackers.uk/blog/headlight-theft-and-can-bus-injection-protecting-your-vehicle-in-2026)).

Engines
- * **2.0‑litre Ingenium (four‑cylinder petrol/diesel)**  
  - Typical fail points: timing‑chain guide/tensioner wear and stretched chains, turbocharger failures on early diesels, oil dilution and DPF‑related regeneration issues on diesel variants. These faults have been tracked in aftermarket diagnostics and owner reports ([Apex Engines Ingenium notes](https://www.apexengines.co.uk/blog/land-rover-ingenium-engine-problems-you-should-never-ignore/), [Go‑Parts timing chain guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/engine-camshaft-land-rover-range-rover-land-rover-range-rover-velar-land-rover-defender-2012-2024)).
  - Preventative maintenance: strict oil‑change discipline. Use synthetic oil meeting JLR specs, and for suburban/stop‑start work shorten intervals to 10,000 km; for open‑road owners 12–15,000 km is common but confirm with the service manual.
  - What to watch for at inspection: rattling on cold start (chain/guide beginning to fail), persistent DPF regen cycles or a “Restricted Performance” limp mode.
- * **P400 / P530 (3.0‑litre inline‑six mild‑hybrid)**  
  - Known issues: torque‑converter shudder (covered under transmission notes), occasional cam actuator and sensor faults that throw misfire or engine system warnings. These straight‑six MHEVs are otherwise robust but expensive to repair when sensors/actuators fail.
  - Preventative maintenance: engine oil and filter every 10,000–15,000 km depending on duty; be vigilant for oil leaks around cam covers and DOHC actuator codes.
- * **P550e / P460e / PHEV family (plug‑in hybrid)**  
  - Specific failure points: battery module cooling and BMS curiosities, high‑voltage cabling wear, and the usual PHEV “if you don’t charge it, you’ll burn fuel” reality — real‑world econ can be far worse than WLTP if owners don’t charge regularly. Long‑term tests show real consumption often higher than lab claims ([Autocar long‑term PHEV test](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-review/land-rover/range-rover/long-term-reviews/range-rover-plug-hybrid-2023-long-term-test), [Carscoops PHEV real‑world study](https://www.carscoops.com/2026/02/plug-in-hybrid-real-world-fuel-use/)).
  - Preventative maintenance: keep cooling systems and HV cabling inspected; follow hybrid battery service routines in the owner’s handbook. Recharging habit is the single biggest factor for getting promised economy.
- * **V8 petrols / high‑output S/V models (e.g., P615 / SV)**  
  - Common points: high‑service costs, occasional oil consumption, and very expensive repairs for cylinder head / timing or turbocharger work if aftermarket neglect occurs. These motors are mechanically stout but the running and repair bills bite.
  - Preventative maintenance: premium fuel only, oil changes every 10,000–15,000 km, valve and cam system checks as per dealer schedule.

Transmission
- Torque‑converter ZF automatics (8‑speed)
  - Symptoms and failure modes:
    - Judder and hesitation under light acceleration or when pulling a load — often a torque‑converter clutch or degraded fluid/valve body symptom. Community and specialist writeups point to ZF 6HP/8HP style failures across Range Rover lineages ([4x4‑specialists ZF guide](https://4x4-specialists.com/range-rover-gearbox-judder-hesitation-zf-transmission-issues-explained/), [Go‑Parts torque‑converter diagnostics](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/torque-converter-land-rover-range-rover-sport-land-rover-defender-land-rover-range-rover-2019-2024)).
    - Slipping, overheating and transmission fault lights may follow if fluid is neglected or internal clutches wear.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Fluid changes: some garages recommend drain+fill every 60–80,000 km; a full flush and new filter every 100–150,000 km for longevity — but check the stamped service book and prefer evidence of recent fluid service. Replacing a failed torque converter or valve body is expensive; a used Range Rover without documented transmission fluid history is higher risk.
  - Behaviour notes:
    - If the car judders under acceleration, hunts between gears, or shows delayed engagement — walk away or budget a transmission inspection and likely rebuild cost.

Entry price, ceiling and market bands
- In NSW today (tiny sample from the snapshot), you’ll see Range Rovers from under $40k — often older L322/L405 cars with very high mileage or heavy option lists needing work — up to near‑new L460 SV/Autobiography models hitting $230k. A median of $129,999 in that snapshot suggests the market still values late‑model, low‑kms examples heavily. For broad used‑market context, other public aggregators put the average used Range Rover price much lower — CarGurus and CarEdge show the model’s used pricing and depreciation pressure ([CarGurus price trends](https://www.cargurus.com/research/price-trends/Land-Rover-Range-Rover-d156), [CarEdge depreciation](https://caredge.com/land-rover/range-rover/depreciation)).
- What to expect to pay:
  - Cheap buy: <$50k — likely older L322/L405 with higher kms or mechanical/appearance compromises.
  - Mid market: $70–140k — good-condition L405 late facelifts or early L460 SWB with moderate kms.
  - Top end: $150–230k+ — almost-new L460 Autobiography/SV, LWB PHEVs or low‑km flagship models.

Depreciation metrics
- Known heavy depreciation:
  - Studies and aggregator analyses show Range Rovers typically lose a lot of their new‑car value. CarEdge estimates up to ~74% depreciation at five years in some scenarios, and other roundups put 5‑year drops in the 60–62% band for the Range Rover specifically — much steeper than many German competitors that hold value a bit better ([CarEdge depreciation](https://caredge.com/land-rover/range-rover/depreciation), [GuideAutoWeb roundup](https://www.guideautoweb.com/en/articles/81531/new-study-reveals-fastest-and-slowest-depreciating-5-year-old-vehicles-in-2026/)). Bottom line: you can buy prestige but you’ll pay for ownership in maintenance and insurance, not just purchase price.
- Expectation for buyers:
  - Rapid depreciation is both a curse and an opportunity: you can pick up a largely good car at a bargain but must budget high running and repair costs.

TCO strategy — how to think about total cost of ownership
- Components of your TCO: purchase price, fuel, servicing, scheduled brakes/tyres (big wheels chew tyres), insurance (higher for prestige SUVs), repairs (electronics, transmission, air suspension), and depreciation/resale.
- Practical steps:
  - Demand a full service history stamped by an authorised dealer or reputable independent with JLR experience. Each missed or DIY service increases TCO risk.
  - Insist on a transmission fluid change history and proof of any software/recall updates (infotainment recall N972, security/BCM updates). Dealer records matter.
  - For PHEVs: run the numbers based on your commute. If you charge daily and do short runs, a PHEV can materially save fuel. If you rarely charge and do lots of long runs, you’ll carry added complexity and likely burn more fuel than a clean petrol six because the petrol engine will do most of the work and you’ll carry battery weight ([Autocar PHEV long‑term test](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-review/land-rover/range-rover/long-term-reviews/range-rover-plug-hybrid-2023-long-term-test), [Carscoops PHEV real-world analysis](https://www.carscoops.com/2026/02/plug-in-hybrid-real-world-fuel-use/)).
- Sample TCO thinking (Australian example)
  - Buy a late‑model L405 for AUD $80k with 80,000 km: anticipate service & repairs averaging $3–5k/yr over the next 3 years (air suspension, electronics, consumables), insurance higher than mainstream, and potential big tickets (transmission or engine repairs $8–20k). That’s conservative — expect surprises.
  - Buy a nearly new L460 Autobiography for AUD $180k: depreciation hit is lower dollar‑for‑dollar short term, but running costs and expensive options are costly to maintain; warranty coverage is the big saver here if remaining.
  - Use Edmunds’ TCO pages as a methodology example to structure your calculation — list out five years of depreciation + fuel + maintenance + insurance ([Edmunds True Cost to Own example](https://www.edmunds.com/land-rover/range-rover/2025/cost-to-own/)).

Is a PHEV worth paying a premium for?
- The math hinges on:
  - How often you can plug in (daily charging vs never).
  - Typical driving distances — PHEVs only out‑save petrols if you frequently do trips within the EV range and keep the battery charged.
  - Real‑world studies show many PHEVs perform far worse in the wild than lab figures suggest, sometimes using multiple times the claimed fuel if not charged routinely ([Carscoops PHEV real‑world study](https://www.carscoops.com/2026/02/plug-in-hybrid-real-world-fuel-use/)). Autocar’s long‑term Range Rover PHEV review underlined the gulf between WLTP numbers and reality ([Autocar long‑term PHEV test](https://www.autocar.co.uk/car-review/land-rover/range-rover/long-term-reviews/range-rover-plug-hybrid-2023-long-term-test)).
- Short verdict:
  - If you can charge at home and your typical daily driving is within the PHEV’s electric range, the PHEV trims can save you money on fuel and deliver a quieter, smoother urban experience. If you can’t charge regularly, buy a petrol straight‑six and spend the PHEV premium elsewhere (service history, tyres, or a pre‑purchase inspection).

Buying recommendation — who should buy what and when
- If you want Range Rover badge + usable reliability at a fair cost:
  - Best value sweet spot: late‑L405 (post‑2016 facelift) with comprehensive service history and ideally a recent gearbox and electronics service. These are cheaper than L460s but still have the imposing Range Rover presence and fewer bleeding‑edge software headaches.
    - Evidence: L405 years are plentiful in the used market; they depreciated hard so you can get low‑km examples at realistic prices.
  - If you want the latest tech, the quieter inline‑six, and are prepared to pay for coverage:
    - Buy an L460 (2022 onwards) only if you can either take warranty coverage (dealer certified pre‑owned) or have the cash to cover expensive out‑of‑warranty repairs. Demand proof of all software/recall campaigns and security updates.
- Specific trim/year to target:
  - For financial sense: late‑model L405 (2017–2021) with under ~100,000 km, fully stamped service book, no saved fault codes on a dealer scan. These trade good kit against lower purchase price.
  - For tech and lowest short‑term hassle: 2022–2023 L460 with remaining factory warranty or an extended dealer warranty and documented software/security patches.
- Walk‑away points at inspection
  - No service history for transmissions or engine oil changes.
  - Unresolved ADAS faults, recurring infotainment blackouts (unless dealer has performed recall/software remedy).
  - Evidence of air‑suspension failure (car sitting low or fault lamps without recent repair), unexplained coolant or oil leaks, or a juddering gearbox that hasn’t been addressed.
- Final blunt Aussie advice
  - Buy one if you love it and accept the running costs — Range Rover ownership is aspirational, not inexpensive. If you treat it like a disposable prestige toy you’ll end up spending thousands fixing what you didn’t budget for. Buy well‑documented cars with recent fluid services, software updates, and, ideally, a dealer or reputable independent warranty. Otherwise, take the badge out to a pub only — admire it from afar.

Final note: buy a Range Rover for the feel, the capability and the presence — but treat it like a high‑maintenance relationship. If you want prestige without the surprises, either buy a near‑new L460 with warranty or pick a late‑L405 with a full dealer history and a good independent pre‑purchase inspection. If you’re chasing cheap badge value under $50k, go in eyes wide open — you’ll likely need to be handy with a Wrench or have deep pockets.
