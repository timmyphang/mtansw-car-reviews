#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation & codes: the current, post‑2022 Ford Everest is the T6‑based second/next‑gen model family (the platform common to the most recent Ranger-derived Everest). Production for this wave runs through 2023–2026 model years and beyond as Ford refreshes the line; official spec sheets for the Next‑Gen/2024+ Everest confirm the modernised mechanical and trim split ([Ford Australia showroom](https://www.ford.com.au/showroom/suv/everest/), [MY24 spec sheet](https://online.flippingbook.com/view/789355651/)).
- Product hierarchy: the Everest sits above mid‑sized soft‑road SUVs (e.g., Territory-sized vehicles) and below full‑size, luxury body‑on‑frame wagons (like the LandCruiser 300). Within Ford’s range it is the seven‑seat, rugged family SUV complement to the Ranger ute — more comfort‑oriented and better equipped than the Ranger but sharing much of its underpinnings ([Wikipedia on Ranger/Everest parts sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Everest)).
- Shared platform architecture & parts commonality: Everest shares roughly 60% of its heavy mechanical architecture and many drivetrain components with the Ford Ranger (T6 platform lineage) — a big plus for parts availability and independent workshop knowledge across Australia ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Everest)).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drivetrains (Australia):
  - **2.0L Bi‑Turbo diesel** (Ford EcoBlue family) — offered across mainstream trims; 10‑speed automatic; part‑time or full‑time 4×4 options depending on grade ([Ford Australia specs](https://www.ford.com.au/showroom/suv/everest/specs/), [CarExpert MY24](https://www.carexpert.com.au/ford/everest/2024/features-and-specs)).
  - **2.3L I‑4 EcoBoost petrol** — high‑power petrol option in some markets, paired with the 10‑speed SelectShift automatic and 4×4 specification in higher grades ([Next‑Gen spec PDF](https://fordbahrain.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/next_gen_everest_2024_specification_en.pdf)).
  - **3.0L V6 turbo‑diesel** — the top‑end V6 available in Platinum / flagship grades in Australia; 10‑speed SelectShift automatic; full‑time 4WD ([Ford Australia showroom](https://www.ford.com.au/showroom/suv/everest/), [MY24 spec sheet](https://online.flippingbook.com/view/789355651/)).
  - Older/legacy engines you’ll still see in used stock: **3.2L Duratorq diesel**, **2.2L Duratorq**, and early‑generation 2.5-litre diesels appear in older (2015–2021) examples in the market ([Unsealed4x4 problems guide](https://unsealed4x4.com.au/ford-everest-common-problems/)).
- Explicit marketplace rivals: Toyota Prado, Mitsubishi Pajero Sport, Isuzu MU‑X, Nissan Patrol/Pathfinder in some segments, Hyundai Palisade/Kia Telluride for buyers leaning premium family space; the Prado is the closest mainstream competitor on reputation and sales volume in Oz ([Drive Prado v Everest comparison](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/2025-toyota-landcruiser-prado-vs-ford-everest-platinum-comparison/), [CarsGuide comparisons](https://www.carsguide.com.au/ford/everest/vs)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Ford Everest?

Exterior
- Panel fit and trim:
  - Generally solid panel alignment thanks to its Ranger heritage; however, owner reports highlight peeling/loose exterior trims and mouldings on older models (A‑pillar appliques and exterior mouldings have been subject to recalls/TSBs in Ford’s wider fleet — check VIN) ([NHTSA A‑pillar recall PDF](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2024/RIONL-24V031-4728.pdf), [CarsGuide recall listing](https://www.carsguide.com.au/ford/everest/problems)).
  - Gloss black and plastic trim on some trims scratch and weather faster than painted surfaces — tidy cosmetic checks will save you future repro costs.
- Paint & corrosion:
  - Early and certain model years have occasional corrosion and paint bubbling issues noted across Ford family vehicles; a TSB exists for aluminium‑panel corrosion on older Ford vehicles — inspect wheelarches, under sills and tailgate lower edge for bubbling or paint peel ([TSB 19‑2026 summary](https://dot.report/bulletins/10156863)).
- ADAS & sensors:
  - Owners reported over‑sensitive ADAS warnings (phantom Pre‑Collision Assist/Adaptive Cruise cutoff and “Sensor Blocked” messages). Ford issued a software update to reduce nuisance alerts for Ranger and Everest families; many owners reported improvements after dealer updates, but some vehicles still require hardware cleaning/replacement of radars/cameras if messages persist ([Ford Authority reporting of update](https://fordauthority.com/2024/09/ford-ranger-and-everest-will-get-update-to-address-overactive-warnings/), [TopicMotor sensor blocked write‑up](https://topicmotor.com/sensor-blocked-warning-in-ford-how-to-fix/)).
  - Practical check: walk the vehicle with a service adviser and confirm ADAS calibration history. On a used buy, insist the ADAS self‑checks are clear and ask for a printout of faults after a drive‑by diagnostic.
- Fast checklist for the exterior:
  - Trim adhesion/peel around A‑pillars and roof rails
  - Evidence of resprays (poor overspray around rubbers)
  - Camera/radar housings, and sensor lenses (cracked, clouded, misaligned)

Interior
- Wear & durability:
  - The Everest interior is built tough — plenty of hard plastics chosen for durability rather than luxury. That’s good if you plan tradie duty or school runs; watch seat bolsters on higher‑kilometre units and third‑row fabric wear (CarExpert’s 2025 review calls the layout “durable” and “workmanlike”) ([CarExpert Sport V6 review](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-reviews/2025-ford-everest-sport-v6-review)).
  - Spatial strength: entry/exit is easier than some ladder‑frame SUVs — door sills and step‑in height are acceptable for most adults, rear‑row ingress is reasonable but check headroom with roof‑mounted options (sunroofs reduce clearance).
- Electronics & infotainment:
  - Infotainment freezing / blank screens have been reported across late Ford models; Ford provides troubleshooting steps for SYNC blank/frozen screens but many owners needed dealer software reflashes or module replacement. A wide Ford recall in 2025 covered screen freezes/missing rear camera images across multiple models — verify screen behaviour, Bluetooth, Apple CarPlay/Android Auto connectivity and the rear camera on test drive ([Ford SYNC troubleshooting](https://www.ford.com/support/how-tos/sync/troubleshooting/why-is-sync-3-showing-a-blank-screen/), [TFLcar recall note](https://tflcar.com/2025/05/ford-major-recall-screen-freezes-missing-images-news/)).
  - Wireless charging pads are picky: Ford troubleshooting tips show most failures come from phone case incompatibilities or misplacement — still, test the pad with a phone in‑car and with multiple USB ports ([Ford wireless charging support](https://www.ford.com/support/how-tos/sync/troubleshooting/why-is-the-wireless-charging-pad-in-my-ford-not-charging-my-phone/)).
  - 12V battery drains / accessory drains: anecdotal reports across user forums and ProductReview note occasional battery drain/electrical gremlins. Ask the seller/dealer to show recent battery health and whether any modules have current draw recorded. If the vehicle has long dealer lease or rental history, expect heavier accessory wiring and greater chance of DIY installs that can create drains ([ProductReview owner reports](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/ford-everest), [Unsealed4x4 common problems](https://unsealed4x4.com.au/ford-everest-common-problems/)).
- Fast checklist for the interior:
  - Boot up/down infotainment, wireless charging pad test, camera operation
  - 12V battery test and check for aftermarket audio wiring
  - Confirm ADAS menu options work and instrument cluster shows no logged faults

Mechanically
- Brand reputation & engineering: Ford’s Ranger/Everest mechanical architecture is competent and widely supported in Australia — Ranger parts are plentiful and independent workshops know these drivetrains well. That said, any high‑volume pickup platform carries wear items at work rates typical of heavy family/tow use ([Wikipedia parts commonality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Everest), [Drive/Carexpert model summaries](https://www.carexpert.com.au/ford/everest/2024/features-and-specs)).
- High‑priority security vulnerabilities:
  - Modern Everest keyless systems are not immune to the CAN‑bus “headlight” or CAN injection style attacks that have hit many brands. Research shows attackers can inject messages into the CAN network to mimic a keyfob or unlock messages — physical hardening helps (secure parking, Faraday pouch, steering wheel lock). See deep dives on CAN injection for the threat model and mitigations ([TheRegister CAN bus attack primer](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/), Dr Ken Tindell analysis [CAN injection](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/)).
  - Practical anti‑theft countermeasures:
    - Faraday pouch for keys overnight or in garage
    - Mechanical steering/wheel lock for overnight parking
    - VIN‑tied immobiliser upgrade or dealer security pack if available
    - Park in well‑lit, video‑monitored areas; avoid leaving trailers with vehicles unattended (recall/ITRM CAN issues highlight trailer/Harness attack surfaces — monitor software updates) ([ITRM recall reporting](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2026/RCLRPT-26V104-9142.pdf)).
- Fast checklist mechanically:
  - Check for oil leaks, coolant stains, and for 4WD models test low‑range engagement and diff locks
  - Ask for a transmission and engine health report if the dealer offers one

Engines
- For every engine/variant emerging in the local market I’ll give the practical failure points, maintenance pointers and service intervals to watch.

  *2.0L Bi‑Turbo diesel (EcoBlue, twin‑turbo)*  
  - Typical faults: turbocharger failures, intercooler hose splits, boost‑leak symptoms and EGR fouling. Owner threads and service write‑ups show boost system hosework and intercooler plumbing as moderate‑frequency service items; turbo failures are often oil‑starvation or debris related ([myenginespecs Ford 2.0 diesel problems](https://myenginespecs.com/ford/ford-2-0-diesel-engine-common-problems-and-issues/), [4x4community bi‑turbo thread](https://www.4x4community.co.za/forum/showthread.php/333180-Ford-Everest-2-0-Bi-Turbo-Issues)).
  - AdBlue / emissions systems: EGR and AdBlue dosing faults are commonly reported in Euro‑diesel engines — expect occasional regen/AdBlue dosing issues in colder climates or low‑mileage city use ([Unsealed4x4 problems list](https://unsealed4x4.com.au/ford-everest-common-problems/)).
  - Preventative maintenance: change oil and filter every 10,000 km if used in heavy towing or town stop‑start — many fleet operators prefer 10,000 km intervals. Inspect intercooler hoses and boost pipes visually at each service. EGR cleaning every 60–100k km depending on mileage and duty (towing/frequent short trips accelerate soot).
  - Real‑world economy: expect 7–10 L/100km on mixed driving; tow and urban work pushes that toward 9–12 L/100km. Check official Ford Australia figures for advertised combined figures ([Ford Australia specs](https://www.ford.com.au/showroom/suv/everest/specs/)).

  *2.3L EcoBoost (I‑4 petrol, high‑power petrol)*  
  - Typical faults: turbo and charge‑cooling plumbing wear, higher fuel consumption compared to diesels, and higher service costs for premium petrol. The 2.3 EcoBoost is mechanically straightforward but can show higher operating costs if used heavy loads or towing.
  - Preventative maintenance: 10,000–15,000 km oil change cycles depending on oil spec and duty; spark/plugs per manufacturer schedule and regular intake cleaning if you do a lot of low‑speed driving.

  *3.0L V6 turbo‑diesel*  
  - Typical faults: generally smoother and stronger than smaller diesels, but complex emissions ancillaries (DPF, EGR, AdBlue) exist. Watch for DPF regeneration history and whether the vehicle has done consistent highway kms; overcrowded city use may lead to DPF/EGR headaches.
  - Preventative maintenance: heavy‑duty service intervals (10,000 km recommended for towing/heavy work), AdBlue maintenance and filter changes per Ford schedule. The V6 is the towing champ but brings higher purchase price and fuel costs.

  *3.2L Duratorq diesel (legacy)*  
  - Typical faults: some 3.2s suffered coolant‑intrusion issues and head/block failures in extreme cases; earlier owner guides flag ‘wet timing belt’ style damage and costly repairs if belt/tensioners fail. Unsealed4x4 and regional buyers’ guides recommend careful inspection on 3.2 units and block‑pressure tests if coolant loss suspected ([UsedFordParts blog on 3.2 issues](https://usedfordparts.co.za/blog/ford-everest-problems/), [Unsealed4x4 problems](https://unsealed4x4.com.au/ford-everest-common-problems/)).
  - Preventative maintenance: strict timing belt replacement intervals — don’t accept an Everest without documented timing service if OEM calls it out.

  *2.2L Duratorq diesel (older units)*  
  - Typical faults: injection system wear, EGR soot, and turbocharger longevity contingent on oil change discipline. Address injector seals and turbo hose condition at inspection.

Transmission
- The modern Everest uses Ford’s 10‑speed SelectShift automatic across most new models (some older/cheaper trims used 6‑speed automatics). The 10‑speed family (including 10R140 derivatives used in Ranger/Everest) has documented torque‑converter related service issues in some cases — failing lock‑up clutches, valve body wear and oil pump problems causing harsh shifts or internal damage in extreme failures ([TransmissionAudit Ford 10R140 guide](https://transmissionaudit.com/units/ford-transmissions/10r140/), [NextGenDrivetrain issues](https://nextgendiesel.com/blogs/transmissions-101/ford-10r140-transmission-problems-solutions)).
- Symptoms to watch for:
  - Harsh or delayed up/down shifts
  - Transmission fault codes or “Trans Service Soon” warnings
  - Torque‑converter shudder or excessive noise on lock‑up
- Preventative approach:
  - Get transmission fluid condition and service history. A dealer fluid and filter service every 60–100k km can help but won’t fix long‑running internal wear.
  - If the test drive shows slipping, hunting or harsh lock‑up, walk away or get a specialist transmission inspection.
- Older 6‑speed boxes: less complex but watch for worn solenoids and torque‑converter clutch issues, particularly on high‑towing examples.

Wider Australian market context
- Aggregators show broad used supply: Carsales and Drive list large pools of Everest stock across model years; Car‑Scout’s median used price survey reports a median of about AUD $48,810 across many listings — expect variance by year, trim and kms ([CarScout median listing data](https://car-scout.com.au/price/ford/everest), [Carsales listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/ford/everest/), [Drive marketplace](https://www.drive.com.au/cars-for-sale/search/all/all/ford/everest/)).
- Newer 2024+ price signalling: CarExpert notes the MY24.5 range opening around AU$54,240 (Ambiente 4×2 bi‑turbo) to AU$81,115 for a Platinum 4WD V6 — this positions late new model Everests at a premium relative to older stock ([CarExpert 2024 price and specs](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/2024-ford-everest-price-and-specs)).

Depreciation & resale
- Baseline: Expect Everest depreciation to be moderate — not as bulletproof as Toyota Prado but holding value better than a handful of soft‑road SUVs because of Ranger parts commonality and proven towing chops. AutoExpert sales data shows Toyota Prado selling in larger volumes (Prado: ~20k units vs Everest ~15k in a year snapshot), and that Prado demand underpins stronger resale values in Australia ([AutoExpert resale analysis](https://autoexpert.com.au/posts/large-4wd-suv-resale-value)).
- As a rule‑of‑thumb: a well‑equipped Everest will shed about 30–40% over 3 years if new‑buy pricing and market conditions follow the patterns of recent mid‑sized ladder‑frame SUVs. Exact depreciation varies widely by trim (V6 and Platinum hold better nominal value but cost more up front).

Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy
- How to calculate: sum purchase price + expected fuel spend + servicing + insurance + road costs + expected repairs over a 5‑year ownership window; divide by annual km to find $/km.
- Fuel & running:
  - Diesel models (2.0 Bi‑Turbo or 3.0 V6) typically deliver better $/km for highway and towing duty — official figures exist on Ford Australia; in the real world expect 8–11 L/100km for the 2.0 Bi‑Turbo depending on load/tow and 9–13 L/100km for V6 under heavy loads.
  - Petrol 2.3 EcoBoost variants will typically burn more in mixed use.
- Service intervals and costs:
  - Expect dealer/owner service intervals in the 10,000–15,000 km band depending on fleet schedule and driving style; fleet/towing duty favours 10,000 km. Check if the used vehicle has full dealer history — that materially impacts future resale.
- Is paying a premium for V6 or alternative powertrains worth it?
  - If you tow heavy loads regularly, the 3.0 V6 diesel may save time, offer better longevity under load and provide superior towing performance — it can be worth the premium when you rely on towing capability.
  - For mixed urban/commute and moderate towing, the 2.0 Bi‑Turbo diesel is often the pragmatic mid‑point: better economy, lower running costs and cheaper to buy than V6 flagships.
  - Hybrid/E‑variants: current Everest line hasn’t broadly pushed plug‑in hybrid variants in Australia; unless you have clear data showing dramatic fuel savings and access to discounted servicing, the upfront premium for hybrid tech in similar segments rarely pays back in pure fuel savings unless you do high urban cycles suitable for EV/hybrid energy recovery.
- Insurance & servicing: Premium trims cost more to insure. Factor an extra ~$800–1,500/yr in insurance for Platinum/V6 models vs Trend/Ambiente equivalents depending on driver history and location.

Buying recommendation — who should buy one, and the sweet spot
- Buy one if:
  - You want a rugged, seven‑seat SUV with genuine towing chops and a dealer/parts network backed by Ranger commonality.
  - You need a workhorse that doubles as a family car — Everest takes a lot without complaining as long as service history is solid.
- Walk away or be cautious if:
  - The example has an unknown ADAS history, frozen infotainment, unknown timing belt history (on 3.2 Si motors) or transmission‑telltale symptoms on a test drive — these faults can be expensive and fiddly.
- The sweet spot:
  - For most used buyers in Australia the best balance is a late‑model (post‑2020) Everest with the **2.0L Bi‑Turbo diesel**, mid‑range trim (Trend/XLT equivalent) with documented service history and a low‑to‑moderate odometer. That configuration gives towing practicality and decent economy without the V6 price premium. NSW dealer median pricing (~AUD $63,990 from the live snapshot) suggests a lot of mid to high‑trim stock sits in this zone — negotiate for a dealer warranty and check for outstanding recall/ADAS software updates before you sign ([MTA NSW snapshot — provided], [CarExpert pricing notes](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/2024-ford-everest-price-and-specs)).
  - If you tow very large caravans or need the top tow quota regularly, step up to the **3.0 V6** — but only if the price delta is sensible given your budget for fuel and maintenance.
- Practical buying steps:
  - Check VIN for open recalls at Ford’s recall lookup and ask the dealer for proof of fixes ([Ford Recalls page](https://www.ford.com/support/recalls-details/)).
  - Get a specialist Ranger/Everest pre‑purchase inspection (focus on boost plumbing, DPF/AdBlue history, transmission diagnostics).
  - Confirm ADAS software level and request a dealer log of last firmware updates — persistent ADAS warnings should be fixed before purchase ([Ford Authority ADAS update story](https://fordauthority.com/2024/09/ford-ranger-and-everest-will-get-update-to-address-overactive-warnings/)).
  - For security, follow basic hardening — Faraday pouch for keys at night and a mechanical lock for long‑term parking are cheap, sensible extras given CAN‑bus risks (see CAN injection analysis) ([TheRegister CAN bus primer](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/)).

Final verdict (short & blunt)
- The Everest is a grown‑up, well‑supported family 4×4 that will serve families, tradies and towing enthusiasts very well — provided you pick a unit with clean service history and evidence of ADAS and infotainment software maintenance. It’s not flawless (electrics, turbodiesel ancillaries and the 10‑speed transmissions deserve a good look), but it’s pragmatic and parts are easy to find thanks to Ranger commonality. For most Aussie buyers, a mid‑range, late‑model 2.0 Bi‑Turbo Everest is the financial and practical sweet spot. Be prepared to walk away from any example that shows transmission hunting, turbo/blow‑by symptoms or persistent ADAS faults.
