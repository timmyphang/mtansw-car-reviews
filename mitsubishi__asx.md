#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation and codes: The Mitsubishi ASX (first launched 2010) is the compact/sub‑compact crossover from Mitsubishi built initially on the company’s GS platform (shared architecture with the Lancer and Outlander). Over its life the ASX has been sold globally as the RVR and Outlander Sport; from 2023/2024 some markets received a rebadged Renault Captur derivative and the range continued evolving through facelifts into 2024–2026 model updates. (See [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_ASX) and the model timeline on [Autoevolution](https://www.autoevolution.com/mitsubishi/asx-rvr-outlander-sport/).)

- Product hierarchy: In Mitsubishi’s local lineup the ASX sits below the larger Outlander. It’s the entry/compact SUV offering — smaller than Outlander, larger and more practical than the Mirage/Attrage hatchback offerings.

- Shared global platform / parts availability:
  - GS platform (Lancer / Outlander family) — that means many common mechanical bits and reasonably broad parts availability through Mitsubishi dealer networks and independent importers. See the platform note on [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_ASX) and the broader spec tables at [Autodata1](https://www.autodata1.com/en/car/mitsubishi/asx).

- Market-specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drivetrains (overview):
  - Petrol: 1.6L (early models), 2.0L MIVEC (common), 2.4L in some markets, later small forced‑induction 1.3L/1.5L/1.0L turbo variants in recent model updates and mild‑hybrid / 1.6 hybrid options in newer global specs. (Aggregated specs: [UltimateSpecs](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Mitsubishi/M1413/ASX) / [Cars‑Data](https://www.cars-data.com/en/mitsubishi/asx).)
  - Diesel: 1.6 (PSA DV6C), 1.8 (4N13), 2.2 (4N14) available in certain markets — note diesel availability varied by region.
  - Transmissions: 5/6‑speed manuals, conventional 6‑speed automatics, and Mitsubishi CVT (INVECS‑III or CVT variants). Newer models include dual‑clutch or DCTs in some rebadged/renewed platforms.
  - Drive configuration: Primarily FWD in Australia; AWD/4WD available on some trims and markets.

- Explicit local marketplace rivals:
  - Hyundai Kona, Kia Seltos/KX‑Series, Mazda CX‑30 / CX‑3 older, Nissan Qashqai/X‑Trail small segment, Honda HR‑V, Toyota C‑HR. Compare side‑by‑side in [CarsGuide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/asx/vs) and general rivals list at [CarSpecsLab](https://www.carspecslab.com/mitsubishi/asx/rivals).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mitsubishi ASX?

Exterior
- Panel fit and paint:
  - Generally acceptable for the class but owner reports show recurring paint/finish and trim wear on older models. Expect scuffs on gloss/black trim and fading on wheelarches on high‑use cars. (See aggregated owner complaints at [CarComplaints](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Mitsubishi/ASX/body_paint/index.shtml) and writeups at [Fixter](https://www.fixter.co.uk/blog/common-problems-with-mitsubishi-asx).)
- Trim vulnerabilities:
  - Gloss black and lower bumper trims scratch easily; cheap plastic lower claddings age badly on high‑mileage examples.
- Body recalls and safety system fitment:
  - The ASX has had numerous recall campaigns historically — aggregated recall lists are available at [RecallsList](https://www.recallslist.com/mitsubishi/asx/) and Mitsubishi’s official recall lookup tool should be checked per VIN: [Mitsubishi Recalls](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls).
- ADAS / sensor issues:
  - Front radar sensors and blind‑spot radar are known to fault (dirty radar behind bumper can trigger multiple warnings). Symptoms: AEB or intelligent cruise disabled, instrument cluster warnings. Owners report fixes as simple as cleaning or as complex as sensor replacement plus calibration. See community reports and guidance at [EverydayMitsubishi](https://everydaymitsubishi.com/article/mitsubishi-asx-emergency-braking-system-how-to-test-and-maintain-it/) and a forum post with radar errors at [MyCarly](https://www.mycarly.com/community/mitsubishi-asx-radar-sensor-error-other-systems-going-haywire%21-0c8ccb73-3b48-4334-a841-ffef4924df34/).
  - Replacement of ADAS sensors requires post‑fit calibration; replacement parts and re‑cal procedures are documented in guides like the Go‑Parts blind‑spot video instructions ([Go‑parts video guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/video-camera-mitsubishi-outlander-sport-mitsubishi-rvr-mitsubishi-asx-2019-2025)).

Interior
- Durability under hard use:
  - For lives involving rideshare or school runs, expect accelerated wear to seat bolsters, cheap door‑trim soft touch surfaces and scuffs on sill plates. ProductReview and owner forums frequently cite interior wear concerns: [ProductReview AU](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/mitsubishi-asx).
- Entry/egress and ergonomics:
  - Door sills are moderate height — easier than some tall SUVs but taller than a hatchback; door check angles and aperture generally fine for older passengers, but check the front seat runners for play on high‑km cars.
- Electronics and infotainment:
  - Multiple owner reports of frozen or unresponsive infotainment screens; soft reset (hold volume ~10–15s) often works — deep resets sometimes require battery disconnect. See a how‑to at [Scegliauto video tutorial](https://www.scegliauto.com/en/video/mitsubishi/tutorial/147638/).
  - USB charging ports can suffer corrosion or connector failure on older cars — diagnostics and replacement guidance at [Go‑Parts USB port fix](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/usb-port-mitsubishi-outlander-sport-mitsubishi-rvr-mitsubishi-asx-2010-2025).
  - Screen sizes and hardware differ across trims and model years — earlier cars had smaller radio units, later cars get larger touchscreen units; confirm exact unit and whether Apple CarPlay / Android Auto works reliably.
- 12V battery drain:
  - There are owner complaints of parasitic drains after aftermarket installs or due to aging body control modules. If a vehicle has long-term storage or poor usage, budget for a new battery and test for parasitic draw. Owner‑reported battery and electrical recalls are listed via [RecallsList](https://www.recallslist.com/mitsubishi/asx/).

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation:
  - Mitsubishi’s mechanicals are generally conservative and serviceable; the ASX benefits from parts commonality with Lancer/Outlander families, which helps independent servicing. Platform parts are well documented on sites such as [Autodata](https://www.autodata1.com/en/car/mitsubishi/asx).
- High‑priority security vulnerabilities:
  - Like many modern cars, the ASX is susceptible to CAN bus injection attacks if thieves can access exposed wiring (e.g., behind headlights). The technique is broadly documented and is not model‑specific: see writeups like The Register’s CAN bus theft description ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/)) and technical documentation from CAN‑CIA ([CAN‑CIA writeup](https://can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf)).
- Aftermarket anti‑theft measures:
  - Mechanical steering locks (Securon‑style), immobiliser upgrades, or CAN‑monitoring products (e.g., CANM8’s ASX unit) can mitigate risks. See the aftermarket CANM8 page for an ASX solution: [CANM8 ASX](https://www.canm8.com/canm8-vrl-asx.html).
  - Bottom line: if you live in a metro area with a theft wave, assume keyless/CAN exploits are plausible and harden accordingly.

Engines
(Italicised subsections for every engine variant discussed)

- *1.6‑litre petrol (early NA 1.6)*  
  - Failure points: timing‑chain stretch/wear reported (signs from roughly 80,000–120,000 km; critical risk around 120k–150k km). Symptoms: rattle on cold start, rough idle, cam phasing errors. (Reported and analysed at [AUTODOC Blog](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-asx).)  
  - Prevention: check service history for timing‑chain tensioner replacements, listen for early chain noise on cold start, and inspect for cam timing alignment codes at a pre‑purchase inspection.  
  - Service interval: follow the OEM manual; for Australian abuse cycles we recommend oil & filter every 10,000 km if mostly urban, otherwise 12–15,000 km if mostly highway and using the correct grade — consult the owner manual ([ManualsLib repository](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/1141878/Mitsubishi-Asx.html)).

- *2.0‑litre petrol — 4B11 (Mitsubishi 4B11 family)*  
  - Failure points: harmonic balancer (crank pulley) degradation causing vibration and noise; oil leaks and occasional oil consumption. The 4B11 family has well‑documented service items; see the Go‑Parts analysis on 2.0L failures ([Go‑Parts 4B11 guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/parts-cars-trucks-mitsubishi-outlander-sport-mitsubishi-rvr-mitsubishi-lancer-2007-2022)).  
  - Prevention: inspect harmonic balancer for free play / wobble; check service history for oil changes at regular intervals.  
  - Service interval: same guidance – follow the OEM schedule in the manual but prioritise oil and filter changes at least every 10,000 km in urban stop/start usage.

- *1.8 / 2.2 Diesels — 4N13 / 4N14 and PSA DV6C (where fitted)*  
  - Failure points: Diesel Particulate Filter (DPF) clogging is commonly reported; injectors and turbo wear vary with duty cycle. The 1.8 DI‑D has DPF complaints in service literature; the PSA DV6C diesel (in some early exports) has its own known weak spots. (See AUTODOC’s diesel notes and owner commentary at [Carbuyer](https://www.carbuyer.co.uk/mitsubishi/asx/owner-reviews) and [AUTODOC](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-asx).)  
  - Prevention: diesel cars need regular longer runs to allow DPF regeneration; avoid short urban hop duty cycles unless you’re committed to forced regens and prescribed maintenance.  
  - Service interval: heavy‑duty diesel servicing and oil change intervals should follow manufacturer diesel schedules; recommend 10,000 km oil checks and 15,000 km major service intervals depending on oil spec.

- *1.3 / 1.0 / 1.5 turbo and 1.6 hybrid (recent models)*  
  - Failure points: these newer downsized turbo engines can be sensitive to poor maintenance (oil quality and intervals) and soot buildup if run in low‑speed urban cycles. The 2025/2026 ASX variants include turbo/mild hybrid and standalone 1.6 hybrids with WLTP fuel claims; the 1.6 hybrid is quoted at ~4.7 L/100 km WLTP in spec sheets ([UltimateSpecs 1.6 Hybrid](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Mitsubishi/151536/Mitsubishi-ASX-2025-16-Hybrid.html)).  
  - Prevention: stick to OEM oil grades, and ensure intercooler/turbo hoses and PCV systems are inspected on high‑km cars.

Transmission
- CVT behaviour and known failures:
  - The ASX commonly uses the INVECS‑III CVT or other CVT variants across the range. Typical owner symptoms: shuddering, hesitation, poor acceleration response, and slipping — frequently appearing between ~80,000 and 150,000 km depending on service. (See [AUTODOC CVT notes](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-asx), [CVTMechanic overview](https://cvtmechanic.com/mitsubishi/mitsubishi-asx-cvt-transmission-problems-and-costs/), and troubleshooting suggestions at [YouCanic](https://www.youcanic.com/mitsubishi-transmission-problems/).)  
  - Notable recall / TSB: a US Technical Information Notice (TIN) SR‑22‑004 addresses CVT ECU programming faults affecting some 2019–2022 Outlander Sport (US-market ASX sibling) CVT units where the ECU could wrongly reduce gear ratio at speed and potentially stall the engine — a software fix/recall was issued. See the NHTSA TIN: [RCMN‑22V563‑9063.pdf](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/rcl/2022/RCMN-22V563-9063.pdf).
- Mechanical diagnosis:
  - CVT faults can be due to degraded fluid, failed solenoids/valve body, belt/pulley wear, or torque converter issues. Torque converter shudder is a common symptom shared with conventional automatics; general torque converter symptoms are summarised at [CarTreatments torque converter guide](https://cartreatments.com/bad-torque-converter-symptoms-and-replacement-cost/).
- Service and prevention:
  - CVT fluid condition is key. Where the OEM calls for “lifetime” fluid, those claims rarely match real‑world wear — insist on CVT fluid drain and filter (where applicable) at recommended intervals or sooner (~60,000–100,000 km) if you plan to keep the car long term.

Diagnostics failures checklist (what to look for pre‑purchase)
- Exterior:
  - Uneven panel gaps or fresh paint masks (possible accident repair).  
  - ADAS warnings on dash, and AEB/ICC disabled warnings — investigate prior radar sensor replacement and calibration.
- Interior:
  - Frozen infotainment/USB ports not working, non‑functional wireless chargers.  
  - Excessive seat/armrest wear for stated km.
- Mechanical:
  - Timing‑chain rattle on 1.6 engines at cold start.  
  - Vibration/noise from harmonic balancer or engine front accessory area on 2.0/2.4.  
  - CVT hesitation/slip, especially under load or at highway merge speeds.
- Engines:
  - DPF soot light on diesel variants.  
  - Oil burn/low oil signs or patchy service history.
- Security:
  - Evidence of aftermarket alarm or wiring hacks; ask if a CAN‑security device is fitted.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mitsubishi ASX?

- Current used market pricing (NSW snapshot):
  - Entry: listed price floor appears to show some anomalous $0 entries (probably mislisted) — ignore $0 listings. Realistic market range across NSW listings: up to $36,750; median dealer price sits at AUD $22,990 with typical example odometer ~63,127 km and years spanning 2012–2025. Use that $22,990 median as the current market centre. (NSW dealer snapshot provided.)

- Depreciation metrics:
  - Broad market studies indicate the Outlander Sport / ASX family depreciates materially faster than Japanese rivals: iSeeCars cited ~28.25% loss over 3 years for Outlander Sport examples; CarEdge/CarGurus show heavy depreciation over five years (~50%+ in many cases). Expect the ASX to lose roughly 25–35% of value in the first three years from new (varies by trim and market), and roughly 50% over five years in standard ownership scenarios — those figures align with US/UK online tracking relative to segment peers ([iSeeCars depreciation summary](https://www.iseecars.com/car/mitsubishi/outlander-sport/resale-value), [CarEdge](https://caredge.com/mitsubishi/outlander-sport/depreciation)).

- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Inputs you should use:
    - Purchase price (use NSW median $22,990 as baseline).  
    - Fuel: petrol WLTP official figures are optimistic; expect ~15–20% higher in real world. For the newer 1.3 turbo mild‑hybrid quoted around 6.0 L/100 km in WLTP cycle, budget ~7.2–7.5 L/100 km in real Australia urban use. Hybrid WLTP (1.6 hybrid) is listed ~4.7 L/100 km — real world likely 5.5–6.0 L/100 km depending on trip mix ([UltimateSpecs 1.6 Hybrid](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Mitsubishi/151536/Mitsubishi-ASX-2025-16-Hybrid.html), real‑world uplift noted at [Real Fuel Consumption](https://www.real-fuel-consumption.com/Mitsubishi-ASX)).  
    - Service & repairs: older CVT cars have higher risk of expensive transmission work; budget an additional $1,000–$2,500 over five years for potential CVT servicing or repairs depending on car condition and mileage. Diesel cars can incur DPF servicing costs and injector work.  
    - Insurance and registration: use your insurer’s quotes; models with higher theft rates may attract higher premiums if no aftermarket hardening is fitted.
  - Mathematical TCO tradeoff — hybrid vs petrol:
    - Example back‑of‑the‑envelope 5‑year ownership for a median ASX (AUD values illustrative):
      - Purchase: $23,000
      - Fuel: 15,000 km/year at 7.5 L/100 km petrol and AUD $2.00/L fuel = 15,000 * 0.075 * $2 * 5 = $11,250 fuel over 5 years.
      - Hybrid fuel (5.5 L/100 km real): 15,000 * 0.055 * $2 * 5 = $8,250 — savings ≈ $3,000 over 5 years.
      - Premium for hybrid purchase (if market prices hybrid at say $3–5k extra): payback time >5 years unless finance rates/maintenance differences favour hybrid. Use a TCO calculator to plug real NSW prices ([CostMyCar](https://costmycar.com/) or [Best‑Calculators EV vs Hybrid Calculator](https://best-calculators.com/automotive/ev-vs-hybrid-vs-gas-calculator/)).  
    - Verdict: hybrids can return fuel savings, but only if you actually drive enough (15k+ km/year) and the hybrid premium over an equivalent petrol ASX is modest. For low annual kilometres or if hybrid prices carry an unsustainably high dealer premium, a well‑serviced 2.0 petrol might be cheaper over 5 years.

- Should you buy one?
  - Yes — with conditions. The ASX is a competent compact SUV: practical, cheap to buy (median $22,990 in NSW), and parts/service routes are broad thanks to platform commonality. But don’t buy blind.
  - Walk away or be cautious if:
    - CVT symptoms are present (shudder, hesitation, stall) and there’s no service or recall evidence — CVT repairs are expensive. Verify any CVT-related campaigns via Mitsubishi recall lookups and ask the dealer for proof of completed TSB work ([Mitsubishi recalls](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls)).
    - The 1.6 petrol shows timing chain rattle, or there’s no service history for tensioner checks/replacement.  
    - ADAS errors are active on the dash with no evidence of proper sensor calibration/repair — replacement radar/cameras need calibration and can be costly.
  - Best‑buy sweet spot:
    - Financially and technically the most sensible purchases in the NSW market are:
      - Mid‑life facelifted examples (approx 2016–2019) with a well‑documented service history and the 2.0 MIVEC petrol paired to a conventional automatic — better longevity and fewer CVT headaches. Check odometer ~60–120k km and price around or below the NSW median of $22,990 depending on condition.
      - If you can find a recent low‑km hybrid or the 1.3 turbo mild‑hybrid at a small premium and you do >15k km/year, that’s worth consideration — but run the TCO numbers (fuel saving vs purchase premium). For specs/fuel claims see [Carsales ASX research page](https://www.carsales.com.au/research/mitsubishi/asx/) and RACV overview on newer pricing/specs ([RACV 2026 ASX coverage](https://www.racv.com.au/royalauto/transport/cars/2026-mitsubishi-asx-price-specs-release-date.html)).
  - Negotiation and inspection checklist:
    - Ask for full service history and receipts, specifically for timing chain/tensioner work (1.6 engines), CVT fluid changes or ECU software updates, ADAS sensor work, and any transmission recalls/TSBs.
    - Pre‑purchase inspection must include:
      - Cold start listen for timing chain rattle (1.6).  
      - Road test to check CVT behaviour under load and at highway merge speeds.  
      - Scan for stored ADAS codes (C2582 or forward radar sensor faults are known codes — reading technical guide at [Go‑parts OBD C2582 explanation](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-codes) can help).  
      - Physical inspection for aftermarket wiring or suspicious tampering (possible CAN access points).
    - If buying from a dealer, insist on certification that outstanding recalls/TSBs are completed — dealer should complete recall repairs free of charge ([Mitsubishi Recall Lookup](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls)).  

Conclusion — short, blunt verdict
- The Mitsubishi ASX is a credible small SUV: good parts support, simple mechanicals on the older 2.0 petrols, and an attractive price point in the NSW used market (median $22,990). If you want a cheap, practical daily and can avoid CVT headaches (or verify the CVT has been properly serviced/updated), go for it.  
- Target: a facelifted 2016–2019 2.0 petrol/auto with full service history is the tactical buy — not too old, not priced like a lemon, and easier to inspect for the common faults. If you do heavy annual kms and can find a modestly‑priced hybrid or newer turbo‑hybrid, crunch the TCO numbers — the fuel savings are real but not always enough to offset higher purchase price unless you drive a lot.
