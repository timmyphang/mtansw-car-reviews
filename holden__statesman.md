#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation & codes: the Holden Statesman nameplate spans decades (original HQ-era launch 1971 through the WB era in the 1970s–80s, then revived in 1990 as the Commodore‑derived VQ Statesman/Caprice and continued in long‑wheelbase Commodore‑based derivatives through to the final Caprice/Statesman WN/WM lines). For a quick anchor: the 1990–1994 VQ was the first Commodore‑based Statesman; later long‑wheelbase siblings include the WH/WK/WL/WM/WN series. See the high‑level history on Wikipedia’s Statesman page and the VQ/Caprice detail pages: [Statesman (automobile) - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statesman_(automobile)) and [Holden Caprice (VQ) - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Caprice_(VQ)).
- Product hierarchy: the Statesman usually sat below the upmarket Caprice (or Caprice‑badged models) in Holden’s long‑wheelbase luxury ladder — Statesman for the trad‑lux buyer, Caprice for the full‑fat executive experience. See the Caprice/Statesman positioning on the Caprice page: [Holden Caprice - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Caprice).
- Platform architecture and parts sharing: from 1990 the Statesman moved onto the Commodore rear‑wheel‑drive platform (long‑wheelbase derivatives of VN/VQ/WH/VZ/WM family). That means a wide parts pool — many body panels are Statesman‑specific, but engines, drivetrain components, suspension bits and interior electrics are heavily shared with Commodore models. Evidence and specs are listed on the VQ Statesman specs pages and Commodore derivative overviews: [VQ Statesman Specifications - Australian Holden](https://australianholden.com/index.php/home/commodores/vn-commodore/vq-statesman-specifications/) and [Unique Cars and Parts VQ Statesman history](https://www.uniquecarsandparts.net.au/car_info_holden_vq_statesman).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drivetrains (summary across the long‑wheelbase run):
  - Engines: straight‑six and V6s (3.3L inline‑6; 3.6L **Alloytec**/HFV6 variants), 3.8L V6 (L‑series), and a range of V8s (5.0L pushrod small‑block variants, **LS1** 5.7L, later 6.0L/5.7L derivatives — see the engine lists at TotalEngines). Source: [Statesman engine list - TotalEngines](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Holden/Statesman).
  - Transmissions: historically a mix of GM automatics — early VQ cars used the TH700/4‑speed torque‑converter box; later WH/WM used 4‑, 5‑ and 6‑speed automatics (including the 6L80 family on V8 models). See VQ specs and later gearbox notes: [VQ specs - vaber.au](https://vaber.au/commodore/vq/vq-specs.html) and documented later gearbox faults on WM: [Recalls & faults: WM Statesman/Caprice](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-wm-statesman-caprice-2006-13/).
  - Drive layout: traditional rear‑wheel drive (RWD) for long‑wheelbase Statesman/Caprice models derived from Commodore.
- Marketplace rivals (Australia): Ford Fairlane/LTD (domestic full‑size sedan rival historically), later imported executive sedans (Lexus LS/E‑class/5‑series when buyers wanted German/Japanese alternatives). Historical rival context: [Ford Fairlane (Australia) - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ford_Fairlane_(Australia)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden Statesman?
This is the “what to look for with your torch and a stout handshake” section. Broken down so you can scan and target‑inspect.

Exterior
- Panel alignment & body panels: long‑wheelbase Statesman bodies use many Commodore parts up front and unique, stretched rear doors and quarters. That long rear door means repair bills for door skins, glass and mouldings can be higher — many breakers sell Statesman‑specific mouldings because they wear or get bashed: [Holdspares exterior mouldings](https://www.holdspares.com.au/exterior/mouldings-trims/) and [Commodore Auto Dismantlers trim listings](https://www.commodoreautodismantlers.com/collections/exterior-trim-molding).
- Trim vulnerabilities: chrome boot trims and opera‑glass surrounds on older cars fade or split; later black or gloss panels scratch. Expect to replace mouldings on older restorations — parts are available but not always cheap for long‑wheelbase‑specific items.
- Paint durability & corrosion: older HQ/WB era cars face ordinary classic‑car rust risks; later Commodore‑based Statesmans are no less vulnerable around sills, boot lips and exhaust hangers than any long‑lived Aussie sedan. Check common rust hotspots and service history.
- Recalls & formal body/safety campaigns: there are documented recall campaigns across various Statesman/Caprice series, including fuel hose/fuel line issues on some models. Check the Australian government recall database and CarsGuide rollups: [Holden VZ Commodore/WL Statesman recall — vehicle recalls site](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-002702) and the centralised Holden Statesman recall summary: [CarsGuide Holden Statesman recall page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/statesman/problems/recall).
- ADAS quirks: later WM/WN series equipped with driver‑assist systems can throw generic "Service Driver Assist System" alerts when cameras, radars or sensors are blocked, misaligned or have wiring issues. This can disable ABS/ESC/cruise, and owners report intermittent stability/ABS faults that require dealer diagnostic/calibration. In short: don’t buy a car with persistent ADAS warnings unless you have a budget for sensor re‑calibration or module replacement. See the ADAS fault summaries and owner threads: [Service Driver Assist System explanation (CartipsDaily)](https://cartipsdaily.com/service-driver-assist-system) and [AustralianCar.Reviews recalls/faults for WM](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-wm-statesman-caprice-2006-13/).

Interior
- Durability under heavy duty use (rideshare/taxi): Statesman interiors are spacious and robust, but leather/vinyl wear accelerates with high‑occupancy use; electrically operated rear seats and luxury fittings are expensive to repair if abused. Restoration guides show common wear points (armrests, seat bolsters, soft‑trim): [WH Statesman interior restoration guide (TrufitAuto)](https://www.trufitauto.trufitauto.com/blog/wh-statesman-caprice-the-ultimate-interior-restoration-guide).
- Entry/egress: tall door sills and long rear doors make for a stately step in — great for back‑seat passengers, less lovely if you do a lot of park‑and‑hop deliveries. Check door check straps and hinges on long‑wheelbase doors — those hinges take an age of opening/closing, and sloppy checks are expensive to tighten and align.
- Electrical behaviours and infotainment:
  - Holden MyLink and similar head‑units across Commodore/Statesman lines are notorious for screen freezes, complete display failure and Bluetooth flakiness. Owners report dead LCD/control units that also disable air‑con controls on some models — dealer quotes for replacements can be steep (~$1,000–$1,500 in forum reports). Practical repair/third‑party rebuild options exist. See example owner threads and repair shops: [LCD control unit dead on WM — Just Commodores forum](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/lcd-control-unit-dead-no-aircond-radio-etc-on-wm-statesman-help.156927/) and LCD repair services: [Holden MyLink repair — GetElectronic](https://getelectronic.net/products/holden-lcd-touch-screen-mylink).
  - Battery drain patterns: later Statesmans with complex infotainment and alarm systems can suffer parasitic 12V draws if modules go flaky; check for aftermarket amplifiers, alarm modules and known hardy modules before signing on.
  - Screen sizes/hardware versions vary by trim and model year — do a quick Google of the VIN/trim for exact head‑unit specs or inspect in person.

Mechanically
- Engineering reputation: as a Commodore derivative the Statesman borrows proven rear‑wheel‑drive GM hardware. When maintained, drivetrains are robust — but cheap neglect and heavy taxi/driver‑service duty multiplies wear issues.
- High‑priority security vulnerabilities: recent theft techniques like CAN‑bus injection (gaining access to exposed wiring and issuing fake CAN messages to simulate a validated key) are an industry problem; these attacks aren’t limited to a single model and affect older vehicles with exposed wiring and minimal intrusion detection. Owners should be aware and fit physical security measures. See the technical writeups: [The Register on CAN bus theft](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/) and Ken Tindell’s CAN injection explanation: [CAN Injection keyless car theft (Ken Tindell)](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/).
- Recommended anti‑theft steps:
  - Fit a quality steering‑wheel lock and a tracking device.
  - If you want higher protection, have a trusted auto‑electrician fit a relay‑cut immobiliser inline with starter/ignition or an aftermarket CAN intrusion detection module.
  - Park in covered or monitored areas when possible — long‑wheelbase sedans are attractive to car thieves because they’re easy to get away with and often have valuable electronics.

Engines
(Italicised subsections per engine variant — each engine listed in the search results gets its own subsection.)

- *3.3L inline‑6 (older straight‑six)*  
  - Typical era: early Statesman/Commodore derivations across the 1970s–1980s.  
  - Known weak points: age‑related gasket and seal failures, oil leaks from valve covers and rear main seals. Bearings and cam wear on very high‑mile engines are possible. Parts are available from classic spares networks but check for soldered‑on previous repairs. Source: historical engine lists via [TotalEngines](https://www.totalengines.com.au/Vehicles/Holden/Statesman).
  - Preventative maintenance: conservative oil‑change intervals (10,000 km or sooner for older engines), careful cooling‑system maintenance, check timing/valve clearances if service history is unknown.

- *3.8L V6 (L‑series ‘Ecotec’ family and variants)*  
  - Typical era: common in earlier Commodore/Statesman V6 models (1990s/early 2000s).  
  - Failure points: head gasket risks on neglected cooling systems; degraded intake manifold gaskets; ageing rubber coolant hoses. Also watch for oil leaks and camshaft/destruction with poor oil maintenance. Background: older V6s are covered in broad problem rollups such as CarsGuide’s engine problems pages: [Holden Statesman Engine Problems - CarsGuide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/statesman/problems/engine).
  - Preventative maintenance: replace coolant hoses and thermostat proactively; follow conservative oil service (10,000 km). If the car has no receipts for cooling system service, budget for a coolant flush and pressure test.

- *3.6L Alloytec (GM High Feature HFV6 — **LY7**, **LLT** etc)*  
  - Typical era: mid‑2000s long‑wheelbase Statesman/Caprice models used Alloytec (High Feature) V6s. See the GM High Feature page: [GM High Feature engine - Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GM_High_Feature_engine).  
  - Known failure points:
    - Timing‑chain/tensioner issues: owner discussion flags timing‑chain tendency to fail around the ~140,000‑km mark on some early Alloytec installations — a catastrophic and costly repair if ignored. See forum discussions: [VZ Alloytec faults - Just Commodores](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/vz-alloytec-manufacturer-faults-handy-hints.167884/).  
    - PCV and associated oil consumption/valve‑cover leaks: keep an eye on PCV function and valve‑cover gasket condition.  
  - Preventative maintenance: strict oil‑change intervals (I recommend 10,000 km or less for high‑use cars), watch service receipts for timing‑chain/tensioner work after 120–140k km; if buying a >120k‑km Alloytec example, budget for timing‑chain inspection or replacement.

- *LS1 5.7L V8 (Gen III small block)*  
  - Typical era: late‑1990s/early 2000s Statesman/Caprice V8s.  
  - Known failure points: LS1s are generally robust but wear items include rear main seal, oil pan gasket leakage and rocker‑cover gasket leaks; cylinder head and valve issues are not common if oil is maintained. General common issues documented in engine retrospectives: [GM LS1 engine common issues on Autoblog](https://www.autoblog.com/features/gm-ls1-engine-specs-common-issues).  
  - Preventative maintenance: regular oil and filter changes at 10,000 km recommended, and attention to cooling system integrity. Valve‑cover gaskets and rear main seals are common jobs on high‑kilometre examples.

- *5.0L / 6.0L pushrod V8s (older small‑blocks & later L76/L98 variants)*  
  - Typical era: across various Statesman iterations—older pushrod 5.0L and later 6.0L/5.7L derivatives used in high‑end Caprice/Statesman iterations.  
  - Known failure points: fuel‑system recalls and manufacturing defects have been documented — e.g., a 2006 incorrectly manufactured fuel hose for some 6.0‑litre V8s and a 2008 fuel‑line chafing recall affecting some 3.6L V6 cars. Read the recall/owner fault summary: [WM recalls & faults — Australian Car.Reviews](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-wm-statesman-caprice-2006-13/).  
  - Preventative maintenance: rigorous attention to fuel lines and associated insulation/clips, fuel‑filter changes, coolant and oil service on a 10k‑15k km cycle depending on usage.

Transmission
- Torque‑converter automatics (TH700/4‑speed and later 6‑speed units): common across the Statesman run are torque‑converter automatics that behave happily for hundreds of thousands of kilometres if fluids are managed — but there are known failure modes:
  - TH700 (VQ era): generally durable but old and heavy; watch for slipping and fluid contamination if service history is poor. Specs: [VQ TH700 use - vaber.au](https://vaber.au/commodore/vq/vq-specs.html).
  - 6‑speed automatics (6L80/6L80E on later V8s and later six‑speed units on V6s): owner reports and aggregation pages record slipping, harsh shifts, delayed engagement and torque‑converter clutch failures in some models. The WM era summary documents gearbox complaints and owner reports: [Recalls & faults: WM Statesman/Caprice (2006–13)](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-wm-statesman-caprice-2006-13/) and owner threads on 6L80 issues: [6L80E problems in 2008 Caprice (g8nation thread)](https://g8nation.com/showthread.php?t=36976).
- Known mechanical symptoms to watch for in a used buy:
  - Harsh 2–3 upshifts, slipping under load, shuddering on torque converter lockup — these all point to either valve body/TC control solenoid faults, worn clutch packs, or torque converter failure. Forum diagnostics and technical notes exist: [Torque Converter diagnosis on mid‑2000s Holden](http://gm.saturn.team/Holden/Statesman/2005/50/5/4279/6991) (technical note).
- Preventative measures:
  - Request transmission service records; if none, budget for a full fluid and filter service and close monitoring for slip. A proactive fluid/ATF change (and installing a cooler if towing or frequent high‑temp driving) is money well spent. If the car is a V8/6L80 example, be alert for class action/owner complaints around torque‑converter behaviour reported in the press: [Class action on defective transmissions — Maurice Blackburn / Street Machine coverage](https://www.mauriceblackburn.com.au/media-centre/media-statements/2025/car-giant-gm-to-face-class-action-over-defective-transmissions-in-holdens/) and related local news.

Theft & Security (quick callout)
- Modern thieves don’t always pick locks — they inject messages onto the CAN bus at exposed wiring points and impersonate a valid key presence. This is a growing risk for Holdens and many other makes. Hardening strategies: mechanical locks, immobiliser relays, tracking and CAN intrusion detection where available. Read the analysis: [CAN Injection overview — The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/) and [Ken Tindell’s CAN injection paper](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/).

#### 3. Should you buy a used Holden Statesman?
Short answer first: If you want a big, comfortable RWD Australian sedan with excellent parts availability and you’re prepared to be pragmatic about electrical and transmission service, a late‑model Commodore‑derived Statesman (WL/WM era with documented service history) can be a smart, characterful buy. If you want a daily commuter that’s cheap and zero drama, buy a small car. If you want modern reliability and warranty peace of mind, buy newer imported tech.

Depreciation metrics (how they hang on to value)
- The Holden Statesman isn’t a “fast depreciator” like flash‑new compact cars — it’s a specialist large sedan, so its value band is wide and heavily condition‑dependent. Sold price records show examples trading from ~$12k through to $60k for very rare or highly restored cars; this variance makes standardised 3‑year depreciation figures misleading without an exact year/model/condition. See sold price examples: [Australian Muscle Car Sales sold prices](https://www.australianmusclecarsales.com.au/makes/holden/models/statesman-and-caprice/sold-prices).
- Practical metric: expect the usual modern large‑sedan behaviour — steep initial depreciation when new, then slower declines with age and mileage; later, collectability can raise prices of certain low‑run examples. If you buy a 10‑15 year old example, you’re buying into used‑market variability rather than predictable residuals.

Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy — how to model it for a Statesman
- Fuel: real‑world aggregated consumption for the Statesman ranges roughly 7.5–8.5 L/100 km for many V6‑V8 configurations (analyticauto and owner logs provide ranges). See Fuel figures: [Analyticauto Statesman fuel figures](https://analyticauto.com/all/holden/statesman/fuel) and [Fuelly owner logs](https://www.fuelly.com/car/holden/statesman).
- Service & maintenance: routine servicing should follow manufacturer schedules — for older, high‑use cars I suggest conservative service every 10,000 km for oil and filters (instead of stretching to 15,000 km). Hidden big‑ticket items to budget for:
  - Head‑unit/LCD replacement or repair (owner reports ~ $1,000–$1,500 depending on model) — examples: [LCD control unit Dead — Just Commodores forum](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/lcd-control-unit-dead-no-aircond-radio-etc-on-wm-statesman-help.156927/).
  - Transmission repairs (if a torque‑converter/6L80 suffers clutch or TC failure, repairs can be several thousand dollars). See documented transmission complaints: [Recalls & faults: WM — AustralianCar.Reviews](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-wm-statesman-caprice-2006-13/) and the coverage of owner compensation/class action interests: [Maurice Blackburn class action summary](https://www.mauriceblackburn.com.au/media-centre/media-statements/2025/car-giant-gm-to-face-class-action-over-defective-transmissions-in-holdens/).
  - Alloytec timing‑chain issues on high mileage cars — budget accordingly if you buy >120k km: [Alloytec forum notes](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/vz-alloytec-manufacturer-faults-handy-hints.167884/).
- Example TCO quick model (how to run one yourself):
  1. Pick expected annual km (e.g., 15,000 km).
  2. Use fuel consumption (8.5 L/100 km) → 1,275 L/year. Multiply by your local petrol price to get fuel year cost.
  3. Add scheduled servicing (estimate $600–$1,200/year depending on dealer vs independent) and set aside $1,000–$2,000/year for mid‑life corrective maintenance (tyres, brakes, small electrical jobs). For higher‑risk components (transmission, head unit, timing chain), keep a multi‑year repair reserve (e.g., $3,000–$6,000 every 5–7 years).
  4. Finally, factor insurance, rego and depreciation for your region. That’s your TCO.

Hybrid/EV premium — does it ever make sense?
- In practice there were no factory Statesman hybrid/EV variants to choose from — the comparison is theoretical: buying an alternative hybrid (e.g., imported Lexus hybrid large sedan) vs a petrol Statesman. Use a hybrid breakeven approach (KBB-style): compare purchase premium, fuel savings per year (driven by your km/year), maintenance differences and resale. KBB’s hybrid breakeven methodology is useful if you want a calculator to decide: [KBB Hybrid vs Gas break‑even guidance](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/). For the Statesman buyer, unless you do >25,000 km/year or fuel is shockingly expensive, the older petrol Statesman’s low purchase price often outweighs hybrid fuel savings — especially when you add hybrid battery replacement risks on imported hybrids of similar age.

Final verdict & buying recommendation
- Who should buy one: you love big RWD comfort, you like parts availability and you can live with older‑car electrical quirks and the chance of a gearbox or infotainment repair down the track. Great as a stately cruiser, airport‑run car, or for enthusiasts who like to tinker.
- Who should walk away: daily commuters who want modern reliability, warranty cover or worry‑free ownership; if you want low running costs and modern driver aids with low risk, look elsewhere.
- Specific sweet spot recommendation:
  - If you want late‑model comfort without collector fiddliness, pick a WL/WM Statesman/Caprice from ~2004–2010 with documented service history, a healthy service book, and preferably with a V6 Alloytec that’s been timing‑chain‑serviced or has lower kms — the 3.6 Alloytec is a reasonable compromise on fuel and performance if it’s been looked after. See WL series summary: [WL Statesman/Caprice info - Vaber](https://vaber.au/holden/wl/index.html) and WM fault/recall summary: [Recalls & faults WM - AustralianCar.Reviews](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-wm-statesman-caprice-2006-13/).
  - If you’re buying a classic collector (your NSW $29,950 1981 example), treat it like a classic: inspect rust, originality and service history, and expect restoration costs if the car is a show piece.
- My blunt one‑line: buy if you’re after Aussie limo comfort and you’re comfortable with the “older‑car tax” of occasional expensive items (transmission or infotainment), and buy the best‑documented example you can afford — don’t buy the cheapskate special hoping for a miracle.
