#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

The RAM Laramie occupies the critical luxury-weight bridge between utilitarian work trucks and executive passenger vehicles. Produced across multiple generations, the current footprint revolves around the Stellantis light-duty DT platform (2019 onward for 1500 variants) and the heavier-duty J platform architecture (2500/3500 series, dating back to 2019 updates with earlier iterations spanning 2009–2018 internally coded as DR/Dodge Ram platforms). In the hierarchy, the Big Horn handles volume fleet and tradesmen duty, the Laramie delivers premium comfort and tech as the core profit margin trim, and the Limited/Longhorn push into ultra-luxury territory with air suspension and bespoke badging. This model shares core underpinnings, braking modules, electrical architectures, and suspension kinematics with the Jeep Gladiator and Dodge Durango, meaning aftermarket support, wiring harnesses, and electronic control units cross-pollinate across the Stellantis truck/SUV ecosystem.

Onshore, the Australian market sees these primarily as parallel imports or grey-market entries, as RAM never operated an official left-hand-drive franchise here. Local specification centres on the 5.7L HEMI V8 petrol (~291kW/556Nm), the 3.6L Pentastar V6 eTorque mild-hybrid system, the 3.0L EcoDiesel V6 (~208kW/634Nm), and for the heavy haulers, the legendary 6.7L Cummins I6 turbodiesel. All variants ship exclusively with the ZF-sourced eight-speed torque converter automatic. Drive configurations split between rear-wheel drive (2WD) and full-time part-time four-wheel drive systems with low-range transfer cases. Fuel consumption figures reported by independent Australian testers hover around 9.9L/100km under controlled touring conditions, though real-world towing drags that number comfortably into the 16–24L/100km bracket depending on payload and gearing 

Explicit marketplace rivals in Australia include the Ford Ranger Wildtrak/Raptor, Toyota HiLux GR Sport/Rogue, Isuzu D-MAX VX-T, and the Nissan Patrol Y62 for buyers prioritising cabin refinement over open-bed utility. If you want a vehicle that reads more like a loaded SUV until you crack open the tailgate, the Laramie is engineered precisely for that duality 

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used RAM Laramie?

**Exterior:**
Panel gaps on US-built trucks routinely show tighter tolerances on the driver’s side, with bonnet latch misalignments common on pre-2020 models due to thermal expansion cycles. Gloss black plastic cladding, roof rails, and mirror caps scratch catastrophically under high-pressure car washes and degrade rapidly under Australian UV exposure, often delaminating within three to five years. Chrome accents on bumpers, grille surrounds, and step bars suffer from galvanic corrosion when exposed to coastal salt spray or aggressive road salts. Paint thickness is frequently below 120 microns on bonnets and quarter panels, making stone chip repair mandatory before interstate runs.

Body recalls tend to cluster around secondary safety and structural fasteners rather than catastrophic failures, but buyers should verify:
- Hood latch microswitch failures causing incomplete closure warnings or unexpected latches popping under vibration
- Fuel tank strap corrosion leading to chassis rub marks
- Rear camera wiring harness chafing against bed rails
- ADAS calibration drift: Forward Collision Warning (FCW) and Lane Departure Warning (LDW) modules struggle with dust haze, intense glare, and corrugated road camber changes. Earlier firmware versions misinterpreted livestock and farm machinery as obstructions; later OTA or dealer flash updates improved radar/threshold mapping, but a pre-purchase scan for active software IDs is non-negotiable.

**Interior:**
Cabin durability depends heavily on prior duty cycles. Vehicles pulled double shifts as site transport, rental fleets, or rural courier rigs show accelerated wear on bolster stitching, armrest cracking, and center console divider fractures. Step-in height varies significantly between 1500 (moderate, ~620mm) and 2500/3500 HD variants (~750mm), which can strain knees for older buyers or frequent multi-passenger loading. Door check cables are heavy-duty but prone to stretching, allowing doors to swing too wide and strike adjacent vehicles in tight yards.

Electrical Behaviours and Connectivity:
- 12V battery drain is a documented pattern. Telematics modems, always-on Uconnect servers, and keyless entry antennas draw parasitic load (typically 40–80mA) when parked >7 days. A weak starter battery or failing alternator diode stack guarantees no-start mornings.
- Infotainment hardware splits between 8.4-inch capacitive touchscreens (pre-facelift) and 12-inch vertical displays (post-2019 refresh). The 12-inch variant runs Uconnect 4C or 5 architecture, handling Apple CarPlay/Android Auto wirelessly but occasionally suffering Bluetooth multipoint handshake drops or map cache corruption.
- Heated/ventilated seat module relays fail intermittently, usually triggered by moisture ingress through floor pan seals or excessive cargo weight flexing carpet routing.
- Wireless charging pads overheat and throttle output if metal toolboxes or unshielded cables sit near the base console.

**Mechanically:**
American engineering leans towards over-engineered durability masked by loose assembly tolerances. Rust protection is minimal compared to Japanese or Korean equivalents, so underside flash rust after coastal commutes is par for the course. However, chassis points, leaf springs, and drag links scale surprisingly well when inspected annually.

Security Vulnerabilities:
Modern RAM models rely on passive keyless entry (PKE) and CAN bus authentication, making them prime targets for relay amplifier thieves who capture and rebroadcast signal bursts to unlock doors and bypass push-button start. CAN injection exploits remain theoretical for mainstream buyers but highlight the vulnerability of unshielded gateway modules. Mitigation requires layered defense:
- Install a physical steering column lock (hardened steel) and gear lever clamp
- Fit a hardwired GPS tracker with geo-fencing and ignition cut-off capability
- Request dealer programming of “Walk-Away Auto Lock” and disable PKE wake-up events via dealership diagnostic tools
- Add an inline fuel pump kill switch accessible only from beneath the cab for high-risk parking zones

**Engines:**
Maintenance intervals officially float at 15,000 km or 12 months per US standards, but Australian conditions demand compression to 10,000 km for dust-heavy regional routes, frequent short-trip towing, or marine environments. Coolant flushes, filter replacements, and spark plug inspections should follow the shorter window.

- ***5.7L HEMI (E392)*** 
 Common failure points include coolant bypass thermostat housing cracks leading to slow antifreeze loss, MDS (Multi-Displacement System) lifter tick/ping under cold starts due to low oil pressure wait times, and high-pressure fuel rail sensor drift causing rich-rich running. Cooling vent filter blockages in the radiator shroud attract kangaroo debris and wingnut litter, choking airflow. Preventative schedule: 10,000 km oil/filter change using 5W-20 synthetic, spark plug gap verification every 40,000 km, and cooling system pressure test biennially.

- ***3.6L Pentastar eTorque (EGJ/EGB)*** 
 The belt-driven starter-generator (BSG) unit is the Achilles’ heel. Pulley bearing noise, serpentine belt flutter, and capacitor degradation in the control module cause erratic stop-start engagement and occasional limp mode. Throttle body carbon accumulation mimics vacuum leak symptoms, requiring manual cleaning every 30,000 km. Preventative schedule: BSG tensioner inspection at 10,000 km, ETC adaptation reset after any battery disconnect, and ATF/coolant exchange at 80,000 km.

- ***3.0L EcoDiesel (EcoTEQ)*** 
 Turbocharged direct-injection efficiency demands disciplined maintenance. DPF regeneration suppression occurs when drivers consistently run short urban hops, triggering ash loading and backpressure faults. EGR cooler tube fatigue causes internal coolant crossover, manifesting as sweet smells and white exhaust vapor. Turbo vane actuator play develops above 120,000 km, reducing top-end torque. Preventative schedule: 10,000 km diesel-specific oil (ACEA C3 spec), DPF forced regen protocols logged via OBDII, EGT sensor monitoring, and turbo actuator endplay checks at 100,000 km.

- ***6.7L Cummins I6 (ISB6.7/ISX12 Heavy Duty)*** 
 Industrial-grade iron and aluminium construction scales well, but high-pressure fuel delivery remains sensitive. Injector control pressure (ICP) fluctuations cause hard cold starts when piezo injectors foul with sulfur deposits. Exhaust gas recirculation cooler internal leaks dilute engine oil, necessitating frequent dipstick checks. Aftermarket ECU tunes frequently void warranties and stress stock transmission clutches. Preventative schedule: 15,000 km service minimum (drop to 10k for towing), OEM DEF dosing nozzle cleaning, turbocharger bearing backlash verification, and coolant analyzer testing quarterly under heavy load.

**Transmission:**
The ZF 8HP torque converter automatic is widely respected for smooth shift mapping and heavy towing resilience, but it is not immune to neglect. Under sustained hill descent or mountain towing, fluid temperatures regularly breach 120°C, accelerating varnish buildup on clutch packs and valve body bores. Known mechanical behaviors include:
- Solenoid chatter and 2nd/3rd gear hangover when mechatronic fluid degrades or filtration screens partial-blockage occurs
- Firmware-induced hesitation during low-speed crawl (parking maneuvers, off-road rock crawling) resolved by dealer flash updates
- Rear differential cooler line fatigue from repeated vibration cycles, leading to external weeping or total failure if rubber sections aren’t replaced annually
- Shift learning recalibration required after any 12V battery replacement or power interruption to prevent harsh lockup converter engagement

Preventative protocol mandates ZF-compatible ATF exchange every 60,000–80,000 km (not “lifetime”), transmission pan gasket renewal with updated sealing beads, and TorqueFlite/ZF diagnostic scanner verification for adaptive learning values before purchase handover.

#### 3. Should you buy a used RAM Laramie?

Live NSW dealer data snapshot indicates a tightly constrained inventory profile with active listings holding steady at 3 units, priced identically at AUD $130 median, spanning model years 2025–2026, averaging 47 km on the clock, and uniformly equipped with automatic transmissions. While the listed median reflects either a scraper artifact or placeholder configuration, broader Australian parallel import markets realistically place 2018–2020 1500 Laramie variants between $85,000 and $115,000 AUD, while 2500/3500 HD equivalents command $120,000–$165,000 depending on kilometres, diesel adoption, and canopy/trailer prep packages.

Depreciation metrics tell a brutal truth: Laramie models shed approximately 40–45% of their original value within three years, substantially steeper than core segment rivals like the Toyota HiLux (~28%) or Ford Ranger (~30%). That curve stems from niche brand loyalty, sparse official parts distribution, and insurance categorisation pressures. However, once the initial cliff levels off (around year four/fifth), resale stabilises strongly among enthusiasts who value the leather-appointed cabin, commanding presence, and genuine payload capacity.

Total cost of ownership calculus hinges on your actual kilometre mix. Running a 5.7L Hemi or 3.0L EcoDiesel costs more at the bowser than a dual-cab ute, particularly if you routinely tow caravans or equipment trailers exceeding 2,500kg. But if your usage skews toward weekend touring, property management, or mixed business/leisure duties, the mathematical premium evaporates. You’re paying for acoustic insulation, ventilated seating, and a 12-inch digital cockpit that rivals Land Rover or Lexus interiors. Hybrid/EV premiums currently fail the Australian long-tow equation; battery weight compromises payload, charging infrastructure outside capital cities remains patchy, and electricity tariffs rarely offset the ICE fuel disparity unless you’re logging >35,000 km annually purely in metropolitan stop-start loops. Conventional displacement engines win on simplicity, parts availability, and remote-area drivability.

Verdict: Buy one, but be surgical. Target the 2020–2022 facelift cycle post-production mid-life update, specifically the 5.7L Hemi 4×4 Laramie Crew Cab with factory tow package, Alpine audio, and panoramic sunroof. These models sit past the warranty expiration cliff but retain robust remaining component life, benefit from refined Uconnect software, and represent the optimal financial sweet spot where depreciation flattens and luxury-to-utility ratio peaks. Avoid early-gen models with unresolved PKE theft vulnerabilities, high-mileage EcoDiesel units lacking confirmed DPF servicing history, or any variant showing chronic transmission overheating evidence. Verify panel gaps, confirm software IDs, flush fluids to spec, and lock down your security layers. Drive it like what it is: an American brawler wrapped in Italian leather. It’ll carry you home, even if it complains loudly about the potholes all the way there.
