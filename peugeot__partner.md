#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

- Generation: third-generation Peugeot Partner (long-running K9/Partner III family, current refreshes from 2018→present). Common documentation and spec sheets span 2018–2026 model years; the official Stellantis specification guide summarises the range and equipment levels [Stellantis spec guide](https://www.media.stellantis.com/uploads/uk/attachment/5192/peugeot_partner_specification_guide_20231215-65f019bd19226.pdf).
- Internal codes & platform: built on Stellantis’ EMP2-derived small‑van architecture (the K9/EMP2 family shared with Citroën Berlingo, Opel/Vauxhall Combo and Toyota Proace City) — that’s why parts and panels are broadly available across those siblings ([EspacioFurgo summary](https://www.espaciofurgo.com/en/plug/peugeot-partner/), [Carwow](https://www.carwow.co.uk/peugeot/partner-lcv)).
- Product hierarchy: Partner sits below Peugeot’s larger commercial range (e.g., Expert/Traveller, Boxer) and above small city runabouts. Within its class it’s the mainstream compact van offering from Peugeot: practical, payload‑focused and built for work.
- Platform siblings (parts availability advantage): Citroën Berlingo / Vauxhall/Opel Combo / Toyota Proace City — shared underpinnings mean easier sourcing of body and mechanical parts and larger aftermarket support ([Carwow](https://www.carwow.co.uk/peugeot/partner-lcv), [Stellantis spec guide](https://www.media.stellantis.com/uploads/uk/attachment/5192/peugeot_partner_specification_guide_20231215-65f019bd19226.pdf)).
- Engines, outputs, drivetrains and transmissions you’ll see in Australia (market-relevant options from global spec pages and technical listings):
  - Diesel: **1.5 BlueHDi** — commonly offered in ~100 PS and ~130 PS tunes; combined fuel use ~4.4 L/100 km is reported for the 131 hp tune ([Auto‑Data 1.5 BlueHDi](https://www.auto-data.net/en/peugeot-partner-iii-van-1.5-bluehdi-131hp-s-s-34583)).
  - Older diesels sometimes fitted in secondary markets: **1.6 HDi (DV6)** and **2.0 HDi (DW10)** families — common on earlier Partners and close relatives; watch these if you’re buying older stock ([EngineCrux DV6 summary](https://enginecrux.com/citroen-peugeot-1-6-hdi-engine-specs-issues-reliability/), [ProGarageLab DW10 notes](https://progaragelab.com/engine-peugeot-dw10td-2-0-hdi-issues-and-insights/)).
  - Electric: **e‑Partner (50 kWh)** — ~100 kW/136 PS motor, ~260 Nm, WLTP range ~350 km for the 50 kWh pack, single‑speed FWD gearbox for the EV variant ([UltimateSpecs e‑Partner 50kWh](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Peugeot/139363/Peugeot-Partner-2024-Van-L2-e-Partner-50kWh.html), [Peugeot e‑Partner spec PDF](https://commercialvehiclecontracts.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Vehicle-specification-Peugeot-e-PARTNER-VAN-50kWh-800-PROF-AU.pdf)).
  - Transmissions: 6‑speed manuals and an 8‑speed torque‑converter automatic (EAT8/EAT8‑derived units) appear across the range; electric model is single‑speed reduction gear.
  - Drive configuration: Front‑wheel drive across the range.
- Marketplace rivals (what Aussies compare it against): Ford Transit Connect, Volkswagen Caddy, Renault Kangoo / Kangoo E‑Tech, Mercedes Citan and the Stellantis siblings (Berlingo / Combo) — class rivals noted in mainstream reviews ([WhatCar?](https://www.whatcar.com/peugeot/partner/van/review/n20209), [Auto Express](https://www.autoexpress.co.uk/peugeot/partner)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Peugeot Partner?

This is the “what to look for” you’ll actually use when you inspect one. Short version: robust for the job, wonky electronics and some diesel‑system quirks are the recurring theme.

Exterior:
- Panel alignment and paint
  - Generally typical small‑van fit/finish: panels align sensibly and replacement panels are common because of shared bones with Berlingo/Combo. However, exterior trim on Asphalt/top trims (gloss elements, full wheel covers) can scratch and scuff easily — typical of work vans.
  - Paint durability: no mass systemic finish recall in Australia flagged in our sources, but fleet use means chips on high‑impact areas (rear doors, tailgate edge, load‑lid) are common.
- Trim vulnerabilities
  - Black plastic trims and lower bumpers scuff; chrome or body‑coloured bumpers on higher trims show dings — nothing exotic but expect used work‑wear.
- Body recalls / safety hardware
  - There’s a documented recall for some Partners (build 2020–2023) where electric parking brake wiring harness rubbing could lead to EPB failure — a safety item worth checking on the VIN [Car‑Recalls summary](https://car-recalls.eu/peugeot-partner-electric-parking-brake-fail-kba/).
- ADAS & sensor issues
  - Radar/camera-based features (Adaptive Cruise, Active Safety Brake) can report intermittent faults — often caused by dirty sensors, obstructed radars or wiring/connectors. Owners report “Adaptive cruise control fault” messages that sometimes clear after restart but can persist and require diagnostic recalibration [Peugeot Forums](https://www.peugeotforums.com/threads/adaptive-cruise-control-fault-with-amber-spanner.384938/), and community posts recommend cleaning near‑invisible radar films or connectors as a first step ([myCarly radar post](https://www.mycarly.com/community/partner-radar-sensor-error-c6866776-53e0-4273-ab2c-9ba45c18f025/)). If ADAS is important to you, insist on a dealer scan and proof of recent recalibration.

Interior:
- Durable but work‑oriented
  - Cabin materials are functional not premium — made to take scratches, dirt and spills. That’s fine for tradie use but expect wear on seat edges, rubber floor mats and scuffs on the door sills and load‑area inner panels if the van has been used for trades.
- Entry/exit & ergonomics
  - Door sills are van‑high but still accessible for average Aussies; the sliding side door and wide rear doors are standard strengths. The Multi‑Flex front bench (available on non‑crew versions) is handy for a third seat or extra storage — check the mechanism if you get a folding bench.
- Electrical behaviours & infotainment
  - Repeated owner complaints about infotainment freezing/reboots and touchscreen failures; threads document SMEG/SMEG+ head units freezing, long‑press reboots, and in some cases unit replacement or re‑flashing was needed ([PeugeotForums touchscreen threads](https://www.peugeotforums.com/threads/partner-touchscreen-turning-off.350451/page-2), [TSB-style thread](https://www.peugeotforums.com/threads/touchscreen-failure-frozen-unresponsive-tsb.347307/page-2)).
  - MotorEasy / MotorEasy claim data shows a non-trivial share of electrical claims (~12.8% electrical-related claims) for Partner ownership, indicating infotainment and electrical gremlins are a known area ([MotorEasy Partner reliability](https://www.motoreasy.com/peugeot-reliability/partner)).
  - 12V battery drain is a common van complaint when vehicles sit — check for aftermarket accessories and known parasitic draws (alarms, wired chargers, fridges). Ask for a healthy Battery State check and evidence of recent alternator output test.
  - Screen sizes & hardware: screen size varies by trim; higher trims get larger touchscreens and possibly wireless charging accessories (Qi pads are offered as factory or dealer fit — alignment/installation issues are common in aftermarket installs) ([Citroën shop accessories](https://citroen-shop.eu/gb/interior-accessories/3667-wireless-car-charger-citroen-berlingo-k9-peugeot-rifter-k9-partner-k9.html)).
- Rideshare/taxi duty
  - If a Partner has been used in courier/delivery or rideshare style: expect accelerated wear to seats, pedals and door catches; look for missing service history and absent scheduled maintenance stamps.

Mechanically:
- Brand engineering reputation
  - Stellantis vans are workhorses — parts are shared widely and workshops know them. But electronics and complex emission systems have historically been the trouble spots across PSA/Fiat/Opel small diesels.
- Security vulnerabilities (real-world threat)
  - CAN‑bus 'injection' thefts are a cross‑make problem. Research shows attackers can access CAN lines (e.g., via headlight connectors or dedicated connectors) and inject “key validated” frames to bypass immobilisers. Peugeot family vehicles are included in community reconnaissance and you should treat modern Peugeots as targets for advanced thieves unless protected ([CAN Injection overview](https://can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindell_canis_automotive_labs.pdf), NVD entry on CVE‑2023‑29389, community reverse‑engineering on GitHub showing Peugeot CAN access points: [bifferos/peugeot-can-bus](https://github.com/bifferos/peugeot-can-bus)).
- Recommended anti‑theft fixes
  - Physical: wheel locks, lockable steering or gear‑stick devices if you’re worried about opportunistic theft.
  - Digital / CAN‑bus: install a proven aftermarket CAN‑bus immobiliser or PIN‑enabled module (e.g., Autowatch Ghost 2 for Peugeot) to add a required PIN step and mitigate CAN injection/key cloning threats ([Autowatch Ghost 2 product](https://www.carsoundsecurity.com/product/ghost-2-can-bus-immobiliser-by-autowatch-for-peugeot-fully-fitted/)).
  - Keep updated with dealer software/security patches and ensure alarm/remote systems have been fitted correctly.

Engines:
(Create an italicized subsection for every engine variant we discussed; each part is italicized as required.)

- *1.5 BlueHDi (PSA modern 1.5 diesel, ~100–131 PS)*
  - Common failure points:
    - DPF clogging on short‑trip urban use — shows reduced power, soot warnings and increased consumption ([CarNews DPF note](https://carnews.nl/en/car-problems/car-problems-peugeot/peugeot-partner/)).
    - EGR carbon buildup causing rough idle, loss of torque.
    - Turbocharger wear if oil/air filters neglected (bearing wear, oil feed issues).
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil and filter: service every 10,000 km is sensible for Australian fleet use; at minimum follow dealer service book but treat 10k as the working commercial standard. Use recommended low‑ash oils for DPF health.
    - DPF care: occasional higher‑speed runs to allow regen (or consider programmed DPF clean at ~80–120k km if used mostly in town).
    - Air filter replacement: inspect every 20,000 km; change sooner in dusty conditions.
  - Notes & sources: Real‑world consumption and performance referenced in tech pages ([Auto‑Data 1.5 BlueHDi](https://www.auto-data.net/en/peugeot-partner-iii-van-1.5-bluehdi-131hp-s-s-34583)).

- *1.6 HDi (DV6 family)*
  - Common failure points:
    - Cambelt/timing issues — shredding or missed cambelt service can produce catastrophic engine damage on interference engines (check service history for belt changes).
    - Starter motor faults reported on some DV6 installations.
    - DPF/EGR and turbo problems (older design more vulnerable if used in town).
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Timing belt change at manufacturer interval — if history missing, treat as overdue. If you can’t confirm, budget immediately for belt, water pump and tensioner replacement (standard safe policy for a used purchase).
    - Oil change: 10,000 km for hard use; otherwise adhere strictly to service book.
  - Sources: EngineCrux DV6 overview and owner reports ([EngineCrux DV6](https://enginecrux.com/citroen-peugeot-1-6-hdi-engine-specs-issues-reliability/)), Autodoc problem list ([Autodoc Partner problems](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-peugeot-partner)).

- *2.0 HDi (DW10 family)*
  - Common failure points:
    - Turbocharger failures (bearing/oil feed restrictions), injector issues and oil consumption in some units.
    - Timing‑belt related vulnerabilities if intervals ignored.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Timing belt service at dealer‑specified interval; inspect turbo and charge pipes for oil leaks.
    - Fuel system check and injector diagnostic if roughness present.
  - Sources: ProGarageLab DW10 notes ([ProGarageLab DW10](https://progaragelab.com/engine-peugeot-dw10td-2-0-hdi-issues-and-insights/)).

- *e‑Partner (50 kWh electric motor, ~100 kW)*
  - Common failure points:
    - Battery warranty & degradation: Peugeot/Group warranty for e‑Partner specs varies by market; UK e‑Partner warranty notes 8 years/100,000 miles for 70% capacity on some packs — confirm local AU warranty via dealer ([Peugeot UK brochure](https://www.peugeot.co.uk/content/dam/peugeot/uk/b2b/buy/price---spec/pdfs/partner.pdf), e‑Partner technical sheet [UltimateSpecs](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Peugeot/139363/Peugeot-Partner-2024-Van-L2-e-Partner-50kWh.html)).
    - Charging hardware: DC fast‑charge ports and BMS wiring require inspection for usage damage; underfloor battery must be checked for water/impact corrosion if used off‑road.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - High‑voltage system service as per dealer schedule; battery health check at ~5 years or if range drops significantly.
    - Electric motor ancillaries (HV cables, chargers) inspected per manufacturers intervals.
  - Energy use & range: WLTP quoted ~354 km for the 50 kWh L2 spec; real‑world energy use ~17.4 kWh/100 km ([UltimateSpecs e‑Partner](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Peugeot/139363/Peugeot-Partner-2024-Van-L2-e-Partner-50kWh.html)).

Transmission:
- Torque‑converter automatics (8‑speed EAT8 variants)
  - Symptoms & behavior:
    - Early symptoms reported: jerky shifts, incorrect shift selection, torque converter lock‑up problems; owner reports see issues emerging from ~60,000 km and more frequent after 100–150k km in hard commercial use ([Autodoc transmission notes](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-peugeot-partner), [myCarly gearbox thread](https://www.mycarly.com/community/peugeot-partner-automatic-transmission-malfunction-685be75e-3da0-4c38-8169-890dcff4e7da/)).
    - Semi‑automatic/automated gearboxes (EGS/EGC) on some earlier Partners have been flagged for robot arm and pump failures — these are electrical/hydraulic components that can be expensive to repair and are sensitive to battery/charging health (voltage spikes).
  - Known failure modes
    - Valve body wear, torque‑converter clutch failure (slipping/grinding), mechatronic electrical faults.
  - What to check on test drive
    - Warm the gearbox, test for smooth lock‑up at motorway speeds, check for shudder or harsh downshifts under moderate load, and scan for stored transmission DTCs if you can.
  - Maintenance
    - Follow dealer’s transmission service schedule (some EAT boxes are ‘fill for life’ but fleet use benefits from fluid and filter changes every 80–120k km depending on service advice; if in doubt, change fluid sooner).

#### 3. Should you buy a used Peugeot Partner?

- NSW dealer snapshot (live MTA NSW data provided): Active listings: 1 — Price range: AUD $23,990 – $23,990; Median price: AUD $23,990; Year range: 2021 – 2021; Median odometer: 49,279 km; Transmission: Automatic: 1. (This is the local market slice you gave me — use it as the immediate comparable.)
- Asking / marketplace price context
  - The single NSW 2021 automatic example at $23,990 / ~49k km sets the immediate local market ceiling for a 2021 professional‑use unit. Expect other samples to trade either side of that depending on option pack, payload spec (L1 vs L2) and service history.
- Depreciation metrics
  - Broad market studies for this model-line indicate steep early depreciation for light commercial vans — used data sources show the Partner losing roughly 45–50% of its value in the first three years in some markets ([UsedCarStats depreciation summary](https://usedcarstats.co/cars/peugeot-partner)). That aligns with the class behaviour: heavy early depreciation, then a stable plateau once the fleet disposals clear.
  - Practical takeaway for Australia: if you buy a 2021 Partner for ~$24k with ~50k km, expect heavier depreciation if you plan to flip it within 1–3 years — buy for utility and total cost, not quick capital gain.
- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to evaluate ownership maths
  - Step 1: choose your mileage profile (e.g., 15,000 km/yr vs 30,000 km/yr)
  - Step 2: fuel/energy assumptions — use model consumption numbers and local prices to build annual fuel spend. Example consumption baselines:
    - 1.5 BlueHDi (~4.4 L/100 km as reported for 131 hp tune) [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/peugeot-partner-iii-van-1.5-bluehdi-131hp-s-s-34583).
    - e‑Partner (~17.4 kWh/100 km WLTP / real‑world ~18–22 kWh/100 km) [UltimateSpecs e‑Partner](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Peugeot/139363/Peugeot-Partner-2024-Van-L2-e-Partner-50kWh.html), [Peugeot e‑Partner spec PDF](https://commercialvehiclecontracts.co.uk/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Vehicle-specification-Peugeot-e-PARTNER-VAN-50kWh-800-PROF-AU.pdf).
  - Example back‑of‑envelope (you can plug your local fuel/electricity prices):
    - Diesel 1.5 BlueHDi at 4.4 L/100 km:
      - 15,000 km/yr → 660 L/yr. At AUD 1.80/L → ≈ AUD 1,188/yr fuel.
      - 30,000 km/yr → 1,320 L/yr → ≈ AUD 2,376/yr fuel.
    - e‑Partner at 18 kWh/100 km:
      - 15,000 km/yr → 2,700 kWh. At AUD 0.30/kWh (residential off‑peak/ public charging varies) → ≈ AUD 810/yr electricity.
      - 30,000 km/yr → 5,400 kWh → ≈ AUD 1,620/yr electricity.
    - Bottom line: at high annual km, EVs quickly look attractive on “fuel” numbers — but you must include higher capex, likely higher insurance and potential battery warranty costs in your TCO.
  - Maintenance & repairs:
    - ICE diesel: DPF and turbo-related repairs can be expensive if neglected; scheduled servicing and belt changes are non‑negotiable for older DV6/DW10 variants.
    - EV: fewer moving parts → lower routine maintenance; but when HV components or chargers fail, repairs can be costly and dealer‑dependent. Battery warranty terms differ by market — check Australian warranty and any dealer‑backed support (Peugeot globally has varying EV warranty terms — UK spec mentions 8 years/100,000 miles for battery capacity but confirm AU) ([Peugeot UK e‑Partner brochure](https://www.peugeot.co.uk/content/dam/peugeot/uk/b2b/buy/price---spec/pdfs/partner.pdf)).
- Is paying a premium for e‑Partner sensible?
  - If your year‑ly kilometres are high (20k+ km) and you can reliably charge at home/work with low tariffs, the e‑Partner often makes sense on running costs. If you’re a low‑mileage user, the premium capex and uncertain Australian second‑hand EV resale might not pay off unless you value quieter running and zero‑tailpipe emissions.
  - Use the IEA/ICCT TCO tools and fleet running cost pages to compare across your expected kilometres and tariffs ([ICCT TCO tool](https://theicct.org/tco-calculator/), [FleetNews running costs tool](https://www.fleetnews.co.uk/tools/van/running-costs/?Manufacturer=peugeot&Model=partner-light-commercial)).

Final recommendation — buy / don’t buy?
- Buy if:
  - You need a properly practical compact van with good payload and low running costs in diesel form for moderate-to-high annual kays, and you can confirm a clean service history (timing belt, recent DPF/turbo service if diesel) and no persistent ADAS faults.
  - Price expectation: the local NSW snapshot shows a live 2021 automatic at AUD $23,990 and ~49k km. That’s a reasonable market price for a serviced 2021 model — assume ~AUD $20k–$26k depending on L1/L2 and option pack.
- Don’t buy (or negotiate hard) if:
  - The vehicle shows infotainment freeze/reboot symptoms, ADAS amber spanner faults that won’t clear, or there's an incomplete timing‑belt/service history on DV6/DW10/1.6 HDi examples.
  - The 12V battery shows poor health or the gearbox shows jerky behaviour on test drive — both are repairs that can be an expensive surprise if you buy blind.
- Best financial sweet spot:
  - For most Australian buyers: a 2020–2022 Partner with the **1.5 BlueHDi** in the Professional or Professional‑Plus trim, with the 8‑speed auto (if you need auto), full service history including timing belt/major services, and 40–80k km on the clock. That balances modern emissions, lower turbo/DV6 risk (1.5 is an improved design over older 1.6 units), and avoids the immediate premium/uncertainty of the e‑Partner unless you specifically need EV duty. Cross‑check the VIN for the EPB recall and ADAS service history.
- Final, blunt bit of truth
  - The Partner is a sensible toolbox on wheels — cheap to fix when maintained, expensive when ignored. If you’re buying it for work: get the service book, check the cambelt/timing history, look for DPF/boost evidence of past issues, test the gearbox thoroughly, and treat electronics as the likely point of low‑grade irritation rather than ruinous failure. Spend a few hundred dollars on a VIN check + dealer scan before you sign — it pays for itself.
