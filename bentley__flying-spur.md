#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

- Generation, production timeframe & internal codes
  - The modern Flying Spur family you’ll see in Australian driveways today falls into two clear eras:
    - The third‑generation Flying Spur (launched 2019 as a 2020 model) — often referred to in the press as the new Flying Spur built on Bentley’s latest platform architecture. [MotorTrend](https://www.motortrend.com/reviews/2020-bentley-flying-spur-review) and Bentley’s model pages detail the 2019/2020 rethink that brought a far more sophisticated electronics and chassis package.  
    - Earlier Grand Tourer‑derived Flying Spurs (Continental‑based cars from 2005 through the mid‑2010s) remain common on the used market — these are the heavier, more analog W12 era machines that attract bargain hunters and horror‑movie‑budget repair bills. See the 2005 heritage summary at Bentley Newsroom / Wikipedia for lineage context. [Bentley Newsroom (heritage)](https://www.bentleymedia.com/en/heritage-collection/2005-flying-spur), [Wikipedia (2005)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bentley_Flying_Spur_%282005%29)

- Product hierarchy (what sits above/below)
  - The Flying Spur is Bentley’s four‑door grand tourer: more relaxed and limousine‑like than a Continental GT coupe, but positioned slightly below the ultra‑exclusive, handbuilt Mulsanne and the reach‑for‑the‑stars Rolls‑Royce options when money is no object. In everyday luxury sedan terms it competes directly with the Mercedes‑Maybach S‑Class, BMW 7 Series, Audi A8 L and the Rolls‑Royce Ghost/Phantom stratosphere. [TopGear comparison](https://www.topgear.com/car-news/review/bentley-flying-spur-vs-mercedes-maybach-s650), [CarsGuide competitors](https://www.carsguide.com.au/bentley/flying-spur/vs)

- Shared global platform architecture & related models
  - The post‑2019 Flying Spur is built around an extended VW Group MSB platform architecture (Bentley calls it an MSB‑derived/extended solution), the same engineering family that underpins the latest Continental GT. That’s good for parts availability from the Volkswagen‑Audi world, and explains why many drivetrain and electronics components are shared across the luxury group. [MotorTrend](https://www.motortrend.com/reviews/2020-bentley-flying-spur-review), [Bentley model overview](https://www.bentleymedia.com/en/models/flying-spur)

- Market‑specific engine, output, transmission and drive options (Australia)
  - W12 6.0‑litre twin‑turbo — the old heavyweight, highest power outputs for Speed/Mulliner/flagship trims (historic and some pre‑2019 variants). [UltimateSpecs summary](https://www.ultimatespecs.com/car-specs/Bentley/M7315/Flying-Spur)
  - 4.0‑litre twin‑turbo V8 — lighter, more economical V8 alternative (around 500–550 PS depending on tune) used across recent model years. [Bentley newsroom & model pages](https://www.bentleymedia.com/en/models/flying-spur)
  - Ultra Performance Hybrid — a 4.0‑litre twin‑turbo V8 mated to an electric motor in an eight‑speed dual‑clutch layout (PHEV) in latest models; combined power figures are very high and provide a limited EV range suited to city trips. [Bentley newsroom (Hybrid)](https://www.bentleymedia.com/en/models/new-flying-spur), [Bentley brochure 2025 specbook](https://cdn.bentleymotors.com/downloads/en/brochure/global/flying-spur/25MY_FlyingSpur_CustomerBook%28English%29%28Oct24%29.pdf)
  - Gearboxes: ZF 8‑speed torque‑converter automatics are common across many V8/W12 Flying Spurs; latest hybrids integrate an eight‑speed dual‑clutch with the e‑motor in newer models. [Edmunds / Bentley specs](https://www.edmunds.com/bentley/flying-spur/2025/sedan/features-specs/), [Bentley newsroom](https://www.bentleymedia.com/en/models/new-flying-spur)

- Explicit marketplace rivals
  - Mercedes‑Maybach S‑Class, BMW 7 Series (M760i/Alpina territory), Rolls‑Royce Ghost/Phantom, Audi A8 L and Lexus LS in luxury positioning. [TopGear](https://www.topgear.com/car-news/review/bentley-flying-spur-vs-mercedes-maybach-s650), [CarsGuide comparisons](https://www.carsguide.com.au/bentley/flying-spur/vs)

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Bentley Flying Spur?

- Exterior
  - Panel alignment & trim
    - Panel fit and finish on factory cars is generally excellent — that’s Bentley’s job — but expect trouble on cars that have seen independent bodywork. Key weak points: underbody trays and skirt/valance panels get ripped on driveways and speed bumps (expensive to replace). See Go‑Parts’ parts guide on exterior trays and undertrays. [Go‑Parts exterior trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-bentley-continental-gt-bentley-continental-gtc-bentley-flying-spur-2006-2027)
  - Paint durability
    - Factory finishes are high quality, but resprays and repairs on older examples are common. Gloss black and high‑contrast trims show swirls quickly; check for overspray around wheel arches and door seams.
  - Recalls & body safety campaigns
    - Bentley publishes recall campaigns and you should VIN‑check any candidate with the official recall page. [Bentley recalls](https://www.bentleymotors.com/en/pages/recalls.html)
  - ADAS and radar issues (safety systems)
    - Expect recurring ADAS faults: Adaptive Cruise Control (ACC), radar malfunctions and traffic assist warnings are documented across Flying Spur model years. Bentley TSBs and NHTSA records list multiple ACC/radar and AEB bulletins — these are often software or sensor faults but can require expensive radar or camera replacements and calibration. If a car has had windshield removal, insist on an ADAS calibration receipt. [TSB: ACC / radar (2025)](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2025/MC-11015373-0001.pdf), [Bang AutoGlass ADAS calibration note](https://bangautoglass.com/blog/bentley-flying-spur-adas-calibration-calibration-urgent-service-need)

- Interior
  - Cabin durability in heavy use (rideshare/taxi duty)
    - Don’t do taxi duty in a Flying Spur unless you’re an idiot with money to burn. Leather, veneers and rotary‑switch mechanisms take abuse — check for worn seat bolsters, cracked veneer, sagging headliner and non‑returning seat motors on older cars.
  - Entry/exit ergonomics
    - Big doors, high‑mass sills and heavy door checks — luxurious but less forgiving for frequent stops and elderly passengers; check door check arm condition and hydraulic dampers on the rear doors where fitted.
  - Electrical behaviours & infotainment
    - Recurrent infotainment and screen issues: reboots, black screens, frozen displays and complaints about the rotating display (on some models) are well documented. There are NHTSA TSBs and other manufacturer bulletins advising fuse pulls and reboots as temporary remedies — but replacement of the head unit or bracket has been required in persistent cases. Wireless phone‑charging modules also have service bulletins for failures and heat generation. [NHTSA TSB infotainment reset/blank screen (2019)](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2019/MC-10159176-9999.pdf), [Wireless charging bulletins (dot.report)](https://dot.report/bulletins/11012200), [dot.report wireless charging heat](https://dot.report/bulletins/11007603)
  - 12V battery drain
    - Low‑use examples can suffer parasitic drains (infotainment modules and retained accessory power). If a car’s been sitting for long periods, expect the 12V system to be weak; ask for a recent battery health/charging test and alternator output.

- Mechanically
  - Engineering reputation
    - Bentley engineering is top‑tier for performance and luxury, but those systems are complex and expensive to fix. The relationship with VW‑Group components gives parts availability benefits, but labour and calibration remain premium.
  - High‑priority theft & security vulnerabilities
    - Modern keyless systems, OBD reprogramming and CAN‑bus injection thefts are real threats in the right hands. London and international theft advisories show Bentleys targeted by relay attacks and CAN injection. If you park on the street, budget for layered anti‑theft measures: invisible immobiliser, GPS tracker, Faraday pouch for keys and ideally an aftermarket CAN‑bus immobiliser that requires a PIN. [SpareKeyFob London advisory](https://sparekeyfob.com/blog/bentley-flying-spur-keyless-theft-prevention-london), [CAN Injection background](https://can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf), [The Register on headlight CAN injection thefts](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/)
  - Suspension & air suspension
    - Air‑spring and valve block faults are common failure modes on heavier Bentleys. Expect expensive replacement parts or conversion to coil springs if prior owners have deferred repairs. [Owner problem summaries](https://vincheckengine.com/problems/bentley/flying-spur)

- Engines
  - *6.0‑litre W12*
    - Known failure points:
      - **Timing‑chain tensioner** wear/rattle on cold start. If ignored this can skip teeth and lead to catastrophic engine damage — an engine‑out job. [Timing‑chain tensioner guide (Go‑Parts)](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/timing-chain-belt-bentley-continental-bentley-flying-spur-audi-a8-quattro-2003-2024)
      - **Turbochargers**: oil starvation or clogged oil feed lines can kill turbos; turbo failure often necessitates major engine work. [Turbocharger failure guide (Go‑Parts)](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/turbocharger-bentley-continental-bentley-continental-flying-spur-bentley-flying-spur-2004-2013)
      - Oil leaks and high oil consumption on older W12s — make sure the last owner used the correct oil spec and that levels have been monitored. [TSBs and engine advisories](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Technical-Service-Bulletin/2022/Bentley/Flying-Spur/Engine)
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - These motors are happiest with frequent oil changes. For a conservative Australian owner I recommend servicing every 10,000 km with manufacturer‑grade oils and a strict timing‑chain inspection schedule. If you buy an older W12, budget for a potential timing‑chain/tensioner bill.
  - *4.0‑litre twin‑turbo V8*
    - Known failure points:
      - Generally more robust and cheaper to maintain than the W12. Look for intake‑cam actuators, turbocharger wear and intercooler/oil‑smoke symptoms on high‑miles cars.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Regular oil and filter changes every 10,000 km, attention to turbo oil feed cleanliness, and intake cleaning on direct‑injection engines are recommended.
    - Notes:
      - The V8 shares group engineering with other Audi/Volkswagen V8s — parts and know‑how are easier to source. Reliability guides flag this as the more pragmatic choice. [CarChecker V8 reliability note](https://www.carchecker.pro/reports/bentley_flying_spur_mk2_4.0_v8.html)
  - *Ultra Performance Hybrid (4.0‑litre twin‑turbo V8 PHEV)*
    - Known failure points:
      - Relative newcomer: electrified drivetrain complexity adds battery, inverter and software layers — failures here can be expensive.
      - Battery health and high‑voltage system condition are key in Australia’s hot climate; check for thermal management records and warranty status.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Maintain hybrid battery health: regular charging cycles, avoid long uncharged storage, and ensure high‑voltage system inspections are done by a Bentley‑trained tech.
    - Fuel & economy reference:
      - Bentley’s 2025 customer book quotes combined consumption for the PHEV when the battery is discharged at about **10.7 L/100 km** (WLTP combined; consult local figures). The hybrid gives a non‑trivial EV range for local trips and potential emissions/consumption benefits in city use. [Bentley 2025 customer book – fuel figures](https://cdn.bentleymotors.com/downloads/en/brochure/global/flying-spur/25MY_FlyingSpur_CustomerBook%28English%29%28Oct24%29.pdf), [Bentley newsroom Hybrid overview](https://www.bentleymedia.com/en/models/new-flying-spur)

- Transmission
  - Torque‑converter ZF 8‑speed (common in many V8/W12 cars)
    - Known failure modes: shudder at cruise, hard or delayed shifts, leaks and mechatronic or valvebody wear. Many ZF 8HP failures are linked to fluid neglect or adaptation faults. If the unit hasn’t had regular fluid changes you’re risking expensive mechatronic or full rebuild bills. [ZF 8HP problem guide](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/zf-8hp-transmission-problems/)
  - Eight‑speed dual‑clutch (latest Hybrid integration)
    - Dual‑clutch boxes are crisp but less forgiving of abuse. Mechatronic or clutch pack wear can produce jerky takeoffs and high‑cost repairs. The hybrid’s integration adds another layer: electric motor/generator coupling and software — check for software updates and documented TSB fixes.
  - Practical advice
    - Obtain a proper transmission health check: scan for stored transmission DTCs, test for slipping, and insist on a recent gearbox‑service invoice (fluid/filter change). Owner forum anecdotes show that adaptation resets and fluid services often cure early symptoms; full replacements are costly.

- Current used marketplace and price ceiling
  - The Flying Spur’s used price spread is huge: older 2014–2016 cars can show up under $60k while near‑new 2021–2022 examples trade for $117k–$180k+, and special editions higher yet. Market platforms show extreme variance, so treat any “bargain” with scepticism. [CarGurus used price spread](https://www.cargurus.com/Cars/l-Used-Bentley-Flying-Spur-d2288), [Autotrader listings overview](https://www.autotrader.com/cars-for-sale/bentley/flying-spur)
  - The NSW $114,950 2013 example sits in the middle of the global used price chaos — not cheap for an older car, but if genuine low‑km and full Bentley servicing, it might be reasonable.

- Depreciation metrics
  - Bentleys depreciate strongly from new but less catastrophically than some competitors in the ultra‑luxury niche; iSeeCars and CarEdge show five‑year depreciation in the ~40–47% band for Flying Spur. iSeeCars puts 3‑year depreciation at ~28.5% with a 5‑year number around ~41.5%. [iSeeCars resale & depreciation](https://www.iseecars.com/car/bentley-flying-spur/resale-value), [CarEdge Bentley depreciation](https://caredge.com/bentley/flying-spur/depreciation)
  - What that means for you: buy the bargain year (older W12 example) and you can shave a fortune off the price — but expect higher running costs and repair risk. Buy late‑generation/low‑km and you’ll pay preservation premium.

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to think about it
  - Step 1: Purchase price + immediate recommissioning
    - Allocate a buffer of AUD $10k–$30k for a pre‑purchase dealer inspection, necessary service, tyres, brake hardware, battery(s), and any immediate software/recall work. For W12 cars factor in engine timing and turbo inspection.
  - Step 2: Running costs (per year) — a conservative checklist
    - Fuel: fuel consumption for the PHEV when the battery is discharged is listed at about **10.7 L/100 km** (WLTP combined for 2025 PHEV). V8s and W12s will typically be higher in real‑world Australian driving — expect mid‑teens L/100 km for W12 in mixed use. [Bentley 2025 specs (WLTP)](https://cdn.bentleymotors.com/downloads/en/brochure/global/flying-spur/25MY_FlyingSpur_CustomerBook%28English%29%28Oct24%29.pdf)
    - Servicing: Bentley dealer servicing is premium — parts and labour will cost multiples of mainstream luxury brands. Use the dealer network for ADAS/diagnostics. [Bentley model page & dealer servicing expectation](https://www.bentleymotors.com/en/models/flying-spur.html)
    - Insurance & registration: high ­insurance premiums for high value cars; get local quotes.
    - Unexpected repairs: allow an annual contingency (AUD $5k–$15k depending on age/mileage).
  - Step 3: Hybrid vs petrol payback logic
    - Hybrid advantage: the PHEV returns meaningful city electric range and lower urban fuel burn. Bentley’s trim comparison data shows modest economy gains vs the V8 (TrimAtlas notes only a few mpg advantage in some trims). [TrimAtlas V8 vs Hybrid comparison](https://trimatlas.com/compare/bentley/flying-spur/)
    - Practical Australian math (example): Suppose you do 15,000 km/year and you can do 25% of trips on EV charge — the Hybrid’s local‑use fuel savings can be measurable. But the hybrid purchase premium and battery replacement risk need to be balanced. Use the Bentley brochure figures and your actual city/highway split to calculate payback; if you rarely charge or mostly do highway runs, the V8 will often be cheaper to own.
  - Tools & references
    - Use Edmunds’ True Cost to Own and Bentley’s WLTP figures as starting points to build your own TCO model. [Edmunds TCO tool](https://www.edmunds.com/bentley/flying-spur/2025/cost-to-own/), [Bentley fuel figures (customer book)](https://cdn.bentleymotors.com/downloads/en/brochure/global/flying-spur/25MY_FlyingSpur_CustomerBook%28English%29%28Oct24%29.pdf)

- Final buying recommendation — should you buy one?
  - Short answer: Buy only if you understand the risk premium; buy exemplary service history and a full dealer‑checked pre‑purchase inspection.
  - If you want to own a Flying Spur in Australia and avoid being out‑muscled by repair bills:
    - Best value play: a late‑2014 to 2016 V8‑equipped Flying Spur with comprehensive Bentley service history, low‑to‑moderate kilometres and documented ADAS/calibration work. The V8 is the pragmatic pick — cheaper to maintain than a W12 and better long‑term than an unloved W12 shell. Caveat: “late‑2014 to 2016” is a recommendation based on used‑market moats; confirm condition and service receipt. (See general used‑buyer checklist at WhatCar.) [WhatCar used‑buyer advice](https://www.whatcar.com/bentley/flying-spur/saloon/used-review/n17895/advice)
    - Avoid unless you have deep pockets: early W12 cars without complete timing‑chain/tensioner and turbo service documentation. A W12 can be heavenly to drive — and catastrophically expensive if neglected. [W12 reliability & failure notes](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/timing-chain-belt-bentley-continental-bentley-flying-spur-audi-a8-quattro-2003-2024)
    - If you can pay more for modern convenience: buy a third‑generation (post‑2019/2020) low‑km Flying Spur (V8 or Hybrid) — you get the modern chassis, electronics and dealer support. Expect to pay a premium for that peace of mind. [MotorTrend review of 2020 Flying Spur](https://www.motortrend.com/reviews/2020-bentley-flying-spur-review)
  - Specific call to action for the NSW listing (2013, $114,950, 2,013 km)
    - Red flags: a 2013 car with 2,013 km is a classic “too good to be true” odometer story or an extremely well‑preserved dealer demonstrator/collector car. Insist on:
      - Full Bentley dealer service history and VIN confirmation.
      - Independent engine compression/leakdown test, turbo check and timing‑chain noise inspection.
      - ADAS radar/camera health scan and calibration records.
      - Clear title, recall clearance and mechanical inspection by a Bentley‑specialist.
    - If all checks out, $114,950 for a genuine, low‑km Flying Spur with documented dealer service could be a steal. If documentation is thin — walk away.

Final word — blunt larrikin nutshell:
A Flying Spur is one of those cars that will make you feel like a millionaire and cost you like one when things go wrong. If you buy one, buy documented, low‑km, dealer‑served and mentally prepared to spend on inflation‑proofed maintenance. If that NSW 2013 car is honest and fully checked, it might be the last time you get this level of Bentley for that price — but don’t let romance blind you to receipts and inspection reports.
