#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

- Generation and production: The Pajero nameplate spans four major generations (Gen I–IV) produced from the early 1980s through to 2021; global production wound up in 2021 before Mitsubishi announced a likely return in 2026. Australian buyers will most commonly see late Gen III (V60/V70 — early‑2000s) and Gen IV (V80/V90 — late‑2006 → 2021) examples on the market today. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero) • [JDMBUYSELL V80 guide](https://www.jdmbuysell.com/learn/mitsubishi/pajero/v80/)

- Internal alphanumeric codes you’ll see in ads and service history: early/third‑gen designations V20 / V30 / V60 / V70; fourth‑gen family carried V80 / V90 chassis codes (and related L040 references in older histories) — those codes are what workshops and parts suppliers use when quoting components. [JDMBUYSELL](https://www.jdmbuysell.com/learn/mitsubishi/pajero/) • [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero)

- Product ladder: The Pajero sits above Mitsubishi’s smaller crossovers (Outlander/Pajero Sport) as the brand’s full‑size, body‑on‑frame-ish (or hybrid monocoque/ladder) off‑road flagship in many markets. It’s the big, long‑throw 4x4 in Mitsubishi’s range — more hard‑core than an Outlander, less luxe than a Land Cruiser or Range Rover. [CarsGuide comparison](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero/vs)

- Platform architecture and parts availability:
  - Gen III moved to a monocoque with an integrated ladder‑frame and used Mitsubishi’s Super Select II 4WD system (2H / 4H / 4HLc / 4LLc). This hybrid structure explains why it behaves more composed on road than older ladder‑frame rigs but remains very capable off‑road. [JDMBUYSELL gen notes](https://www.jdmbuysell.com/learn/mitsubishi/pajero/)
  - Global name variants (important for parts and salvage shopping): Montero (North America), Shogun (UK) — same family, different badges; these name variants increase parts and used component availability for older Pajeros. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero)

- Market‑specific powertrains and drivetrains (high‑level; local availability varied by year/trim):
  - Diesel: common units include the **2.5‑litre 4D56** turbo‑diesel and larger **3.2 DI‑DC / 4M41** turbo‑diesels (later Gen IV models). [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/mitsubishi-pajero-model-1726) • [EngineScope 4M41 report](https://enginescope.gr/engine/mit-4m41/)
  - Older diesels and markets also used **2.8L 4M40** derivatives. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero)
  - Petrol: V6 petrols such as the **3.0/3.5‑litre V6 (6G72 / 6G74 family variants)** were common in petrol markets. [MotorReviewer 6G72 notes](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=204)
  - Transmissions: 5‑speed manuals and 5‑/4‑/6‑speed torque‑converter automatics are typical across the range; later Pajero Sport derivatives introduced new torque‑converter automatic boxes and even 8‑speed units in some markets. [Auto‑Data](https://www.auto-data.net/en/mitsubishi-pajero-model-1726)
  - Drive configurations: Part‑time and full‑time 4WD variants; Mitsubishi’s Super‑Select allows on‑the‑fly switching between high and low ranges on many trims. [JDMBUYSELL](https://www.jdmbuysell.com/learn/mitsubishi/pajero/)

- Main Australian marketplace rivals: Toyota Prado / LandCruiser family, Nissan Patrol, Isuzu MU‑X / D‑MAX based large SUVs, and large SUVs like the Ford Everest in the same practical space. [CarsGuide rivals listing](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero/vs) • [Zigwheels rivals](https://www.zigwheels.ae/car-feature-stories/mitsubishi-pajero-vs-rivals-price-engine-size-and-features-compared)

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mitsubishi Pajero?

Exterior
- Panel fit and corrosion:
  - Older Pajeros (pre‑2000s / early V60 examples) can show typical age‑related panel sag, sill corrosion and aftermarket repairs — check wheel arches, lower door sills and rear tailgate edges for bubbling or weld‑patch repair. Owner‑forums and body manuals exist for panel work if you need to confirm dimensions. [ProCarManuals body guide](https://procarmanuals.com/category/body/mitsubishi-body/mitsubishi-pajero-body/)
- Trim vulnerabilities:
  - Gloss black plastics and grille inserts age badly; chrome has a habit of pitting on older cars. Inspect sun‑exposed surfaces for crazing and fading.
- Paint durability and recalls:
  - There have been multiple Pajero recalls across years; always VIN‑check for outstanding recalls before buying. Manufacturer VIN recall lookup is the start point. [Mitsubishi recall lookup](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls) • [RecallsList aggregated history](https://www.recallslist.com/mitsubishi/pajero/)
- ADAS & sensor fragility (where fitted):
  - Later Pajeros with forward‑facing sensors and ADAS show radar sensor errors and FCM (Forward Collision Mitigation) messages; owners report radar errors disabling adaptive cruise and collision functions. After body repairs the radar/camera commonly requires recalibration — an expense to factor in. [MyCarly radar error thread](https://www.mycarly.com/community/my-mitsubishi-pajero-radar-sensor-error-anyone-seen-this-before-8b2bda57-6faf-4b84-a900-599cf8ab95e7/) • [I‑CAR calibration note](https://rts.i-car.com/crn-1459.html) • [FCM service explanation](https://cartipsdaily.com/fcm-service-required)

Interior
- Cabin wear:
  - The Pajero’s interior materials are functional, not fragile‑luxury; they stand up well to regional rough use but expect armrest cracking, seat bolsters that rub thin on high‑km examples and roof trim droop on older cars. Owner boards report heavy‑use weaknesses if a Pajero’s been a rideshare or light‑truck. [POCUK electrical forum & community threads](https://www.pocuk.com/forums/)
- Entry/egress and practical ergonomics:
  - Tall door sills and a slightly higher step‑in on wheel‑arch SUVs make ingress/egress easy for off‑roaders but can be a pain for elderly passengers — check door checks and hinges for tightness and rust.
- Electrical gremlins:
  - Infotainment units and HVAC control modules are recurring annoyance points on older and late‑model Pajeros. Threads report dim screens and units needing resets; Whirlpool and owner forums document persistent display complaints. If the vehicle has cameras, check camera clarity and the dimming behaviour in bright sun. [Whirlpool forum dim screen report](https://forums.whirlpool.net.au/archive/3jm6wxj2) • [How‑to reset head‑unit guide](https://anusedcar.com/mitsubishi/how-to-reset-mitsubishi-touch-screen/)
- 12V battery drain & accessory quirks:
  - Owner posts on Pajero forums list parasitic drains linked to aging modules and keyless‑entry circuits. Always check for an aftermarket alarm installation or body‑controller replacement in the history (both can introduce drains). [POCUK forums](https://www.pocuk.com/forums/)

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation:
  - Mitsubishi built these as robust off‑road machines; when cared for they last. But they’re not maintenance‑free and many faults surface after 80–150k km if service regimes weren’t followed. [AUTODOC reliability roundup](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-pajero)
- Theft & security:
  - Pajero immobilisers are factory‑fitted and generally reliable, but modern theft techniques like CAN‑injection exist (these attacks usually target newer keyless cars by accessing wiring behind headlamp clusters). For older Pajeros, physical theft methods (tow‑away, key cloning) are more common than fancy bus injection. Hardening steps: sensor‑guarding, steering‑column immobiliser, mechanical wheel clamp or aftermarket ORCID‑approved immobiliser/telematics. See the technical writeups on CAN injection for context. [CAN Injection overview](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/) • [The Register on CAN bus theft](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/)
- Recommended anti‑theft practical measures:
  - Add a visible mechanical immobiliser/wheel clamp.
  - Fit a secondary immobiliser or certified aftermarket alarm with tilt/ tow detection.
  - If the car has keyless entry, ask a pro to check for upgrades/patches or physical hardening.

Engines
- Intro: The Pajero used a wide spread of petrol and diesel engines across generations; below are the units specifically called out in owner reports and technical breakdowns — each subsection lists common failure points and recommended prevention.

  - *Bold engine names are the codes to search when checking service history or parts listings.*

  - *4D56 — 2.5L turbo‑diesel (long‑running Mitsubishi four‑cylinder)*
    - What goes wrong:
      - Turbocharger wear/failure on high‑km units; injection pump and injector wear if fuel quality/filters have been neglected. Owner‑reported turbo issues pick up around ~150k+ km but can occur earlier with neglected oil/air filters. [EngineExplained 4D56 review](https://www.engineexplained.com/mitsubishi-4d56-2-5-l-engine-review/) • [AUTODOC summary of turbo failures](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-pajero)
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil & filter changes at least every 10,000 km (shorten to 5–7,500 km for heavy towing/ off‑road). Use diesel‑grade oils and monitor turbo oil seals for seepage.
      - Fuel filter replacement per service schedule and pre‑purchase inspection for smoking or black soot at idle.

  - *4M40 / 2.8L diesel family (older medium‑bore turbodiesels used in some markets)*
    - What goes wrong:
      - Age‑related oil leaks and turbo oil seal wear; these engines are durable but expensive to overhaul if neglected. [Wikipedia engine list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero)
    - Prevention:
      - Regular oil/filter, check valve cover and turbo seals, and watch for coolant contamination of engine oil.

  - *4M41 — 3.2 DI‑DC turbo‑diesel (later, larger diesels used in Gen IV)*
    - What goes wrong:
      - High‑pressure fuel pump (HPFP) failures, EGR cooler leaks and turbo oil‑seal degradation are documented. Injector failures / glow plug controller faults have been reported; some issues are fuel–quality and thermal‑stress related. [EngineScope 4M41 reliability](https://enginescope.gr/engine/mit-4m41/) • [What‑Breaks V60 guide](https://what-breaks.com/mitsubishi/pajero-v60)
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Diesel HPFP & injector system checks as part of clutch/major service intervals; follow manufacturer diesel service schedules and use quality diesel fuel.
      - Oil and coolant checks every 10,000 km under Australian operating conditions; replace coolant and flush EGR cooler if owner history suggests high‑heat use.

  - *3.2 DI‑DC (as marketed in some later variants / Pajero Sport derivatives — similar care to 4M41)*
    - What goes wrong:
      - Similar to 4M41: HPFP, injector and EGR issues. [MyEngineSpecs engine list](https://myenginespecs.com/mitsubishi/mitsubishi-pajero-engine-specs-configuration-and-service-intervals/)

  - *6G72 — 3.0L (V6 petrol family)*
    - What goes wrong:
      - Age‑related oil consumption, valve guide wear and ignition‑system service items (coils/plugs) become common as km climbs. Whilst generally durable, a leaky head gasket or cam‑timing device wear on an unserviced engine can be financially painful. [MotorReviewer 6G72 overview](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=204)
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil change every 10,000 km (or 6,000–8,000 km in harsh service), regular spark plug and ignition coil checks, cooling system flush at recommended intervals.

  - *3.5L V6 (various V6 petrols fitted to some markets)*
    - What goes wrong:
      - Higher fuel use (see TCO commentary below) and expected wear on ignition and timing chain/tensioner items at high km.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Standard petrol V6 service intervals; check for timing‑chain rattle and water pump leaks on inspection.

Transmission
- Mechanical behaviour & common failure modes:
  - Torque‑converter automatics (the most common transmission in Australian Pajeros) can show slipping, harsh shift or failure at high‑mileage if fluid has been neglected. AUTODOC and owner threads note erratic shifts and complete failure beyond ~150k km in many cases where fluid/ filter changes were skipped. [AUTODOC automatic transmission issues](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-pajero)
  - CVT and newer automatic tech in some Mitsubishi models (not the older Pajero generations) has its own failure profile — shudder, torque‑converter clutch circuit faults (P0740) on certain Mitsubishi automatics/CVTs have been logged. On‑vehicle service notes for Mitsubishi automatics emphasise correct fluid fill procedures to avoid torque‑converter starvation. [Go‑Parts P0740 guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0740-mitsubishi-various-2007-2020) • [YouCanic CVT troubleshooting](https://www.youcanic.com/mitsubishi-transmission-problems/)
- Preventative maintenance:
  - Transmission fluid and filter service every ~60,000 km as a minimum for heavy or towing use; if you can only get one life‑saving service done, this is it. AUTODOC specifically recommends transmission oil service periodically (around every 60,000 km) to prevent catastrophic failure. [AUTODOC transmission advice](https://www.autodoc.co.uk/info/problems-with-the-mitsubishi-pajero)

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mitsubishi Pajero?

- NSW dealer market snapshot (live):

  - Price range: AUD $0 – $34,990
  - Median price: AUD $28,990
  - Year range: 1999 – 2021
  - Median odometer: 101,625 km
  - Transmission mix: Automatic 3, Manual 2
  - (Data supplied by user snapshot / MTA NSW scraper — treat the $0 as a data quirk / placeholder)

- What that price band means:
  - You can find clean late‑2000s Gen III / early Gen IV wagons around the mid‑$20k to $35k band depending on km, condition and whether the car’s been prepped with bullbars/ARB gear. The NSW median price of AUD $28,990 with around 100k km is realistic for a well‑appointed, mechanically solid example.

- Depreciation metrics:
  - Mitsubishi vehicles, broadly, average around a 52% drop after 5 years per CarEdge’s brand analysis — the Pajero commonly depreciates faster than premium rivals like Toyota Prado / LandCruiser simply because buyers prize Toyota’s perceived durability and brand resale. Factor in model rarity too: very old Pajeros (classic 1980s/1990s) can be niche premiums; modern late‑model Pajeros depreciate into more affordable territory sooner. [CarEdge Mitsubishi depreciation](https://caredge.com/mitsubishi/depreciation) • [JDMBUYSELL listings range](https://www.jdmbuysell.com/for-sale/mitsubishi/pajero/)

- TCO strategy and fuel math (a practical approach)
  - Fuel economy reality: Owner‑reported figures show Pajeros are thirsty on petrol V6s (real world mpg/L values vary), while diesels are considerably more economical. Fuelly’s crowd‑sourced fuel data for Pajero owners is a great resource — expect real‑world diesel consumption somewhere in the mid‑7–9 L/100 km range depending on model and load; petrol V6s typically sit higher. [Fuelly Pajero data](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/pajero) • [Analyticauto fuel tables](https://analyticauto.com/all/mitsubishi/pajero/fuel)
  - How to run the numbers quickly:
    - Step 1: Pick realistic L/100 km for the specific engine. Diesel 3.2 DI‑DC ≈ 7–9 L/100 km; petrol V6 ≈ 11–13 L/100 km (real world can be higher).
    - Step 2: Multiply by annual km. Example: 15,000 km/year × 8 L/100 km = 1,200 L/year. At AUD $1.80/L that's AUD $2,160 fuel/year (adjust to your local fuel price).
    - Step 3: Add servicing/insurance/tyres and an annual provisioning for major items (transmission overhaul, turbo, injection servicing) — older diesels should carry a higher annualised maintenance reserve.
  - Hybrid/modern drivetrain premium: If you’re comparing a modern hybrid SUV versus a used Pajero, KBB’s hybrid vs gas break‑even analysis is the right framework — hybrids command a purchase premium; you must drive enough km or pay local fuel prices high enough to recover that premium. In practical Australian used markets, a cheap diesel Pajero with good mechanicals will often beat a small hybrid crossover on outright practicality and towing, but not on urban fuel costs. [KBB hybrid vs gas break‑even article](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/) • [Analyticauto real fuel consumption](https://analyticauto.com/all/mitsubishi/pajero/fuel)
  - Bottom line on TCO:
    - If you do lots of towing, off‑road work, or need the load/tow platform — a diesel Pajero with documented service is usually the most cost‑effective in the long run.
    - If your driving is mainly urban and you want the absolute lowest running cost, smaller modern hybrids or late‑model petrol turbo SUVs may trump an older petrol V6 Pajero once you factor daily fuel.

- Risks that hit your wallet
  - Turbo and injector jobs on diesels can be four‑figure bills.
  - Major automatic transmission repairs or torque‑converter replacements are expensive — expect $$$ to $$$$ depending on parts and rebuilds.
  - ADAS/sensor recalibration after front impact or major bumper work can cost hundreds to over a thousand dollars.

- Final buying recommendation (the blunt, Aussie takeaway)
  - If you want a Pajero for its core competence — towing, remote travel and hard‑wearing capability — buy a diesel, buy one with a full service history, and don’t be seduced solely by cosmetics. A mid‑to‑late Gen IV (V80) 2008–2015 diesel with ~80–160k km that has documented bolt‑on accessory fitment (ARB/Winch) and recent tranny service is the sweet spot for many buyers: fewer early‑generation rust quirks, modern creature comforts and good parts support. [JDMBUYSELL V80 guide / Final Edition notes](https://www.jdmbuysell.com/learn/mitsubishi/pajero/v80/)
  - Avoid:
    - Cars with incomplete service records and “mystery” engine/transmission lights.
    - Petrol V6s if your wallet cares about fuel — they’re lovely but thirsty.
  - Price target given NSW snapshot:
    - Expect to pay roughly AUD $20k–$35k for decent late‑2000s → early‑2010s examples in NSW. The listed median of AUD $28,990 is a fair market figure for a well‑sorted Pajero with ~100k km in that region (you can sometimes get high‑km or cosmetically rough examples cheaper, but plan for mechanical rework). (NSW dealer data supplied)

Concluding clutchline
- The Pajero is a proper 4x4: rugged, capable and honest about what it is. If you need a genuine off‑road tow rig and can handle the fuel and scheduled maintenance, it’s a brilliant used buy — just do your homework and expect the odd hefty bill if someone skipped recommended service. If your life is mostly urban commute and you hate the idea of diesel maintenance, consider a more modern, smaller SUV or hybrid instead.
- Pre‑purchase checklist (non‑negotiable):
  - VIN recall check at a dealer before purchase. [Mitsubishi recall lookup](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls)
  - Full service history and transmission fluid change records (or plan to do it straight away).
  - Verify turbo, HPFP and injector history if it’s a diesel (ask for receipts).
  - ADAS sensor status and any post‑repair calibration records on later models (radar/camera errors are common after front‑end repairs). [MyCarly radar thread](https://www.mycarly.com/community/my-mitsubishi-pajero-radar-sensor-error-anyone-seen-this-before-8b2bda57-6faf-4b84-a900-599cf8ab95e7/) • [I‑CAR calibration](https://rts.i-car.com/crn-1459.html)
  - Physical test: tow a trailer and test gearboxes under load (listen for slipping, harsh downshifts).
