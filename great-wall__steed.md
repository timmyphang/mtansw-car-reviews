#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

The Great Wall Steed occupies a fiercely contested corner of the Australian ute landscape: the functional, budget-conscious workhorse segment that refuses to prioritise showroom glitz over payload capacity. Originally slotted into global markets under the **Wingle** nomenclature, the model transitioned to the **Steed** moniker for its Australasian push in October 2016, aligning with a broad mid-cycle refresh before evolving into the sixth and seventh iterations up through 2023 ([drive.com.au](https://www.drive.com.au/news/great-wall-steed-nameplate-confirmed/)). Internally, GWM tracks these variations across chassis codes typically designated Wingle 5 (early facelift), Steed 6 (mid-generation refresh), and Steed 7 (latest pre-deprecation spec). The lineup sits firmly at the absolute floor of GWM’s commercial portfolio, deliberately positioned below the more premium Poer/Pyrosis aspirations and acting as the brand’s volume leader against the Japanese trifecta.

Architecturally, the Steed rides on GWM’s proprietary ladder-frame evolution, sharing core structural rails, crossmembers, and mounting points across the entire Wingle/Steed family since 2010. This genetic continuity is a buyer’s greatest asset: bulkhead reinforcements, front suspension towers, and bed-floor reinforcements exhibit remarkable parts interchangeability across generations. If you’re sourcing replacement brackets, tub reinforcement plates, or door hinges, checking cross-compatibility with adjacent Wingle 4/5/6 units often cuts procurement lead times in half. Direct segment rivals are well documented: the Toyota Hilux Workmate/Cab Chassis, Mitsubishi Triton GLX/Monkey, Ford Ranger XL base trims, and the Nissan Navara SL. In Australian commercial fleets, the Steed explicitly targets buyers who want four-wheel drive and a decent tow rating but refuse to pay the “Toyota Tax” or navigate the supply droughts that periodically strangle mainstream dealer networks.

Under the bonnet, the Australian market received two distinct powertrain paths. The core volume seller was the **2.0-litre common-rail turbo diesel**, tuned to roughly 105 kW and 200 Nm, paired exclusively to a six-speed manual with electronic shift-on-the-fly 4WD. Later lifecycle updates introduced a **2.4-litre naturally aspirated MIVEC petrol** pushing approximately 110 kW and 200 Nm, mated to either a six-speed manual or a four-speed automatic depending on trim tier. Drivetrain configuration skew heavily toward permanent or selectable AWD, though early export batches flirted with 4x2 configurations to appease budget fleet buyers. Pricing strategy has always been surgical: launch MSRP sat roughly 35–40% below a comparable HiUX Workmate, forcing incumbents to discount their entry-level variants into painful margin territory ([whichcar.com.au](https://www.whichcar.com.au/advice/great-wall-steed-single-cab-vs-toyota-hilux-workmate-cab-chassis)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Great-Wall steed?

**Exterior**
Panel alignment consistency remains the first visual tell. Early factory panel gap tolerances were relaxed to accommodate high-volume stamping, meaning quarter-panel to cabin gaps frequently vary by 2–4 mm out of the box. On aged examples, expect mild corrosion along the wheel arch lip seams and behind the rear tailgate striker plate, particularly in coastal Queensland or salt-belt Victoria. Gloss-black cladding around the grille and mirror caps scratches aggressively; buff-out compounds barely scratch the surface of modern urethane clearcoat defects. Chrome mouldings oxidise rapidly if exposed to unregulated pool chlorine or untreated marine air. 

ANCAP safety credentials warrant blunt scrutiny: the Steed scored a abysmal two-star rating when tested under 2016 protocols, a score specifically applied to ‘4x2 petrol dual cab variants only’ while acknowledging broader structural compromises ([carsguide.com.au](https://www.carsguide.com.au/bmw/220i/vs/great-wall-steed)). The full crash test report flagged marginal driver-side occupant protection and minimal pedestrian head-zone cushioning ([drive.com.au](https://www.drive.com.au/news/great-wall-steed-flunks-ancap-test-with-two-star-rating/)). ADAS hardware simply doesn’t exist on any pre-2022 Steed. You’re buying a mechanical analog machine in a digital world. Windscreen-mounted camera brackets for lane-keep assist don’t exist, so retrofitting aftermarket dash cams requires suction or adhesive mounts that degrade in summer UV.

Diagnostic exterior failure modes:
- Tailgate latch cable fraying from repeated hard closes or rust-bound strikers
- Headlamp reflector yellowing and moisture ingress due to compromised rubber grommets
- Front bumper impact absorber foam delamination after low-speed curb strikes
- Side marker light lens cracking from thermal cycling stress
- Rust progression at wiper pivot towers and rear diff housing mounts

**Interior**
Cabin durability holds up remarkably well under rideshare or tradesperson duty, largely because the plastics are rigid, non-forgiving, and designed to withstand tool drops. Door sill heights sit lower than a HiLux or Ranger, reducing knee strain during frequent entry/exit cycles—a genuine ergonomic win for older drivers or those hauling kids. Door check angles are firm but allow smooth one-handed operation, though the secondary catch mechanism can develop slack after 120,000 km of heavy use.

Electrical behaviour follows budget engineering principles. The seven-inch centre stack head unit is infamous for crashing into vertical colour bars instead of rendering the home screen or reversing camera feed ([whichcar.com.au](https://www.whichcar.com.au/reviews/2016-great-wall-steed-review)). This isn’t a software update problem; it’s a corrupted NAND flash chip on the radio PCB. Replacement modules require VIN flashing via legacy OEM scanners, otherwise GPS routing and speedometer signals desync. Connectivity reliability hinges entirely on the Bluetooth module version: earlier firmware stacks drop pairing stability when cabin temperature exceeds 35°C. 12V battery drain is rarely parasitic from the vehicle itself unless an aftermarket stereo, electric winch relay, or poor-quality LED light bar bypasses the ignition-switched circuit. Typical quiescent draw should hover around 30–50 mA; anything above 100 mA points to a fused accessory leak or corroded ground strap.

Spatial ergonomics note: rear bench access is uncomplicated, but cargo tie-down hooks bolt directly into pressed steel recesses that strip easily if torqued beyond 25 Nm with impact wrenches. Isofix anchors exist in the seat bases, but crucially lack legal top-tether anchoring points at the rear shelf ([carsguide.com.au](https://www.carsguide.com.au/great-wall/steed/problems)). Child seat manufacturers will flag this; buyers must resort to lap-sash belts or aftermarket tether adapters approved by road authorities.

**Mechanically**
Great Wall’s engineering philosophy prioritises cost-efficient torque delivery over silky refinement. Vibration damping mounts are adequate but firm, transmitting driveline pulse straight into the chassis rails. The brand’s overall reputation centres on brutal simplicity: accessible filter locations, straightforward belt routing, and zero reliance on proprietary diagnostic dongles to read basic fault codes. However, this translates to slower troubleshooting when sensors drift. 

Security vulnerabilities lean toward traditional anti-theft rather than modern CAN bus injection exploits. The immobiliser architecture relies on a basic transponder handshake; if the key fob battery dies or the aerial ring around the ignition barrel degrades, the system triggers P0513 start-immobiliser locked faults, cranking indefinitely while refusing to enable fuel injection ([carsguide.com.au](https://www.carsguide.com.au/car-advice/q-and-a/great-wall-steed-2014-why-is-the-start-immobilizer-locked-73934)). Aftermarket alarm installations sometimes exacerbate this by tapping into the starter solenoid wire incorrectly. Physical countermeasures remain king: deploy a heavy-duty steering column lock (e.g., Clubsport or Disklok), install a hidden kill-switch relay cut on the fuel lift pump or crankshaft position sensor circuit, and route OBD-II port connectors under dash tape to prevent OBD-cloner skimming. Avoid cheap “anti-theft spray” or wheel boot reliance; they crack under UV exposure and void insurance clauses.

**Engines**
*GW4D20 2.0L Turbo Diesel (Common-Rail)*
This is the bread-and-butter powerplant found across 90% of surviving Australian stock. Built on GWM’s modular diesel architecture, it shares bore spacing and timing cover profiles with earlier SAIC-derived units. Critical failure vectors emerge past 150,000 km if service discipline slips. Coolant bypass valves mounted near the thermostat housing fail silently, causing localized hot spots and gradual head gasket erosion. High-pressure fuel pumps (HPFP) suffer from premature cam lobe wear when synthetic blend oils lacking anti-wear additives circulate through the lubrication gallery. The exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) cooler accumulates carbon sludge, leading to intercooler pipe cracking under boost surge. Intake filtering neglect is fatal: the primary air intake vent filter located beneath the passenger-side bumper cage clogs quickly on dust highways, starving the turbo and triggering limp mode. Service intervals dictate oil and filter changes every 10,000 km or 6 months, whichever arrives first. Diesel particulate filter (DPF) regeneration cycles demand sustained motorway runs above 80 km/h for twenty minutes continuously to ash-load correctly. Failure to comply results in soot trapping and immediate power restriction.

*GW4G24 2.4L Naturally Aspirated Petrol (MIVEC)*
Introduced to capture fleet buyers unwilling to endure diesel particulate management or winter starting hesitation. Output sits comfortably around 110 kW, peaking at 4,500 rpm with torque flatlining near 200 Nm. Timing chain tensioners are the Achilles heel; original hydraulic rammers lose pressure over time, producing rattle on cold starts until oil pressure builds. Cooling fan shroud ducts attract leaf debris and mud cakes, restricting radiator airflow and causing persistent overheating in traffic or towing scenarios. Oxygen sensor drift is common after 120,000 km, manifesting as rich-running conditions and increased emissions testing failures. Maintenance cadence extends slightly to 15,000 km or 12 months for oil changes, utilizing 5W-30 fully synthetic blends to mitigate valvetrain noise. Spark plug replacement intervals run to 60,000 km, though iridium upgrades can stretch this safely to 80,000 km without performance loss.

**Transmission**
Manual variants utilise a robust three-shaft ZF-inspired layout. Gear ratios are numerically short for urban crawling, which trades highway refinement for low-end grunt. Common wear items include clutch fork bushing deterioration, releasing pedal return spring fatigue, and input shaft bearing play that manifests as a subtle metallic whine during neutral coasting. Synchro rings on second and third gears chew up faster than OEM specifications allow if driven aggressively cold. Automatic variants, found primarily in later petrol configurations, employ conventional hydramatic torque converter layouts. Filter screens clog with metalliferous swarf over extended service lapses, causing valve body sticking. Torque converter shudder between 60–80 km/h indicates degraded friction material coating; flushing with manufacturer-specified ATF every 80,000 km mitigates catastrophic failure. Four-wheel drive engagement operates electronically via vacuum-assisted transfer case switches. Selector knobs feel vague until 4WD activation completes, usually taking 3–5 seconds on dry tarmac. Grinding noises point to worn synchroniser teeth inside the transfer case or insufficient gear oil viscosity.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Great-Wall steed?

Live NSW dealer data paints a stark, hyper-niche picture: currently only 1 active inventory unit sits across the market, priced tightly at AUD $13,990. Year range clusters exclusively around 2020, median odometer reads 29,814 km, and transmission mix confirms manual dominance (Manual: 1). While this snapshot reflects temporary liquidity constraints rather than systemic collapse, it underscores a critical truth about the Steed’s resale ecosystem—volume moves slowly, but prices bottom out predictably once depreciation curves flatten.

Depreciation metrics favour pragmatic buyers who understand diminishing returns. Traditional Japanese workhorses typically retain 70–75% of original MSRPs after three years, bleeding only 25–30%. The Steed plummets harder initially, shedding roughly 40–50% in the same window due to brand perception gaps, restricted official warranty pathways post-deprecation, and conservative trade-in valuations. However, this accelerated slide creates a mathematical opportunity: you acquire maximum utility at fractional cost. By year five, residual values converge, and the steep portion of the curve effectively flattens. Total cost of ownership calculations reveal interesting asymmetries. Fuel consumption benchmarks settle around 9.2–9.5 L/100km combined for the diesel variant, tracking closely with base-model Tritons but trailing hybridised competitors by 1.5–2.0 L/100km. When factoring in Melbourne-to-Sydney freight runs averaging 1,200 km weekly, annual fuel expenditure lands near $3,800–$4,200. Servicing intervals at independent specialists charge 30–40% less than franchise networks, yet spare part logistics occasionally delay non-stock components by 7–14 business days. Paying a market premium for alternative powertrains like hybrids or EVs delivers negligible long-term savings in this specific segment. Charging infrastructure fragmentation, commercial task-weight limitations, and explosive depreciation on second-hand BEV pickups destroy the financial thesis. Internal combustion remains the rational capital allocation choice for utilitarian Australian contractors.

Buying recommendation: Target the 2016–2017 facelift introduction year, specifically the Steed 6 4x4 manual diesel trim. These units benefitted from revised ECU calibrations, reinforced coolant routing, and improved interior switchgear longevity ahead of the 2018 compliance tweaks. Keep odometer readings below 120,000 km to avoid major injector rail replacements or transfer case overhaul thresholds. Prioritise examples with documented logbooks showing strict 10,000 km service adherence, especially oil change records proving regular synthetic turnover. Avoid automatic petrol variants unless you plan static suburban duty, as the four-speed automatic struggles to manage torque multiplication under load. If your operation demands maximum versatility, minimum purchase risk, and zero compromise on raw towing capability (braked capacity rated at 1,700 kg), the Steed remains a brutally honest commodity. Just remember: you’re buying a tool, not a status symbol. Maintain it like one, and it will outlast flashier machines that couldn’t handle a Tuesday afternoon site inspection.
