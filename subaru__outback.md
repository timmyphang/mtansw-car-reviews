#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation and codes: This review covers the current mid‑generation Subaru Outback (the wagon‑turned‑crossover that’s been built on the Subaru Global Platform since 2020 and refreshed across 2024–2026 model years). Subaru markets the latest Outback through model years 2020–2026 in major variants (including Base/Premium, Onyx, Limited, Touring, Wilderness, XT). See Subaru’s official specs for the 2025/2026 lineups for trim and drivetrain detail: [Subaru 2025 Outback specs](https://www.subaru.com/vehicles/outback/2025/specs-trim.html) and [Subaru 2026 Outback specs](https://www.subaru.com/vehicles/outback/2026/specs-trim.html).

- Product hierarchy: The Outback sits above the Forester in Subaru’s lineup in size and equipment, and below the larger three‑row Ascent/Exiga family in sheer volume and seating layout. The Wilderness/XT variants push the Outback toward more capability‑focused buyers; the Base/Premium trims sit at the commuter/cross‑utility end.

- Platform architecture and siblings: The Outback uses the Subaru Global Platform (SGP), shared across latest Subaru models — Forester, Crosstrek (XV), Impreza and Legacy variants — which helps with parts availability and shared repair knowledge across the dealer network ([Subaru brochure noting SGP & Symmetrical AWD](https://www.subaru.com/content/dam/subaru/downloads/pdf/brochures/2025/2025_Outback_Brochure_071524.pdf)).

- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive:
  - Naturally aspirated 2.5 L flat‑4 boxer (~182 hp / 182–187 PS depending on market) paired to Subaru Lineartronic CVT and full‑time Symmetrical AWD (most common in Australian import/spec used market) ([Edmunds 2024 spec summary](https://www.edmunds.com/subaru/outback/2024/features-specs/); Subaru specs pages).
  - Turbocharged 2.4 L/2.0 L variants in higher trims (sold as **XT** or turbo models globally; examples include the FA24F 2.4L turbo and FA20DIT/FA20F 2.0L turbo in earlier turbo Outbacks) paired to CVT or in some markets different tuning. See FA engine notes: [MotorReviewer FA20DIT summary](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=212) and FA24 turbo discussion ([CherishYourCar on 2.4 turbo](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/subaru-2-4-turbo-engine-problems/)).
  - Drive configuration: Symmetrical AWD standard. Transmission: Lineartronic CVT (Subaru’s modern CVT family).

- Marketplace rivals: Toyota RAV4 (incl. Adventure/Hybrid variants), Mazda CX‑50, Honda Passport/CR‑V, Jeep Cherokee/Grand Cherokee (for Wilderness/XT buyers) and the more wagon‑like Volvo V60 Cross Country or Audi A4 Allroad for buyers thinking premium.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Subaru Outback?

Exterior:
- Panel gaps and trim fitment: Owners report hood/bonnet gap inconsistency and A‑pillar trim release on 2020–2024 models because retaining clips are one‑time‑use; Subaru issued TSBs referencing trim clip failures ([Go‑Parts TSB reference](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/body-a-pillar-trim-panel-subaru-legacy-subaru-outback-2020-2025)). Expect some dealers to re‑adjust hoods and reseat clips — cheap if caught early, annoying if left to rattle.
- Paint and clear coat: Recurrent clear‑coat peeling, especially on roof/bonnet/trunk, shows up across owner reports; once the clear coat fails, rust and peeling can follow and repairs (wet‑sand + re‑clear) run into the thousands. Watch for rough texture, loss of water beading and blistering patches on popular colours — owner summaries and complaints are chronicled here: [EngineCrux paint problems](https://enginecrux.com/subaru-outback-paint-problems-facts-and-insights/) and aggregated complaint records ([CarComplaints Outback body/paint](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Subaru/Outback/body_paint/index.shtml)).
- Exterior trim vulnerabilities: Gloss black and matte cladding scratch easily on Wilderness and Onyx trims. The Safari‑style cladding is practical but chips more visibly; aluminium roof rails and plastic end caps show scuffs with rooftop gear.
- ADAS and safety system quirks: EyeSight (Subaru’s camera‑based ADAS) has a rising volume of complaints in later model refreshes (2024–2026) about false pre‑collision warnings, phantom emergency braking and over‑sensitive lane assist — some owners report that the system can’t be fully suppressed and that phantom interventions occur on highways and under shadows/overpasses. These are serious safety complaints and have even prompted litigation and consolidation of owner complaints: [CarComplaints/EyeSight lawsuit summary](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2026/subaru-eyesight-lawsuit.shtml), [Lemberg Law on EyeSight/2025 Outback complaints](https://lemberglaw.com/2025-subaru-outback-problems-complaints-lemon/), and continued consumer complaint catalogues ([CarRemorse on 2026 problems](https://www.carremorse.com/problems/subaru-outback-2026)). Firmware fixes have been issued in some cases, but owners report mixed results — insist on service history for ADAS calibrations and ask for dealer‑applied updates.

Interior:
- Durability under high use: The Outback’s interior is generally robust (upholstery and plastics hold up better than many soft‑touch rivals), but rideshare/taxi duty exposes the cabin to:
  - Fast‑wear seat bolsters on cloth or leatherette trims.
  - Scratched centre console piano black panels and fraying door seals if loaded with bedding/roof gear frequently.
- Entry/exit ergonomics: Door sills and seat height give good access (a major reason tradies, parents, and older Aussies like it), with lower sill intrusion than big SUVs and wider door check angles — good for repeated ingress/egress.
- Infotainment and electrical weirdness:
  - The STARLINK head units (various years) have a documented history of freezes, ghost touches, delaminating screens and reboot‑loops — particularly in 2018–2019 and recurring in later hardware revisions. If the screen delaminates it’s a physical failure and usually requires replacement: [Kinetic Vehicles on delamination](https://kineticvehicles.com/blogs/news/is-your-subaru-outback-touchscreen-going-crazy-it-might-be-delaminating), and a broader failures guide here: [Go‑Parts audio system guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/audio-system-subaru-legacy-subaru-outback-2013-2024).
  - Wireless charger module: some 2020 owners report intermittent electrical gremlins tied to the wireless pad (radios/infotainment failing when the charger is present or in use) — the factory wireless charger has official installation instructions but also a history of causing faults: [Forum thread on wireless charger issues](https://www.subaruoutback.org/threads/intermittent-electrical-problems-on-2020-outback-with-wireless-charger.531468/) and Subaru’s accessory guide ([Subaru wireless charger install PDF](https://techinfo.subaru.com/stis/doc/accInstallGuide/H1010FN210%20Wireless%20Charger%20-%20Key%20Start%20-%20IxI.pdf)).
  - 12V battery draining: intermittent battery drain has been reported by forum owners, especially in cars with many telematics/accessories or aftermarket fitment. For used buyers: check for battery replacements, parasitic draw tests and whether the car has had multiple ECU reboots (infotainment resets are often a sign of deeper electrical instability). Consumer Reports also notes in‑car electronics are a common trouble spot on recent models ([Consumer Reports 2025 reliability notes](https://www.consumerreports.org/cars/subaru/outback/2025/reliability/)).

Mechanically:
- Engineering reputation: Subaru’s boxer‑AWD recipe is loved for traction and low centre of gravity. In engineering terms, the modern Outback is robust but not immune: the mechanical weak points historically cluster around ageing engine families and CVT drain points.
- Security vulnerabilities:
  - Telematics/web portal risk: security researchers disclosed vulnerabilities in Subaru STARLINK that could expose location histories and, in extreme cases, allow remote unlocking or starting — Subaru issued patches after disclosure, but the event underlines risk tied to telematics and old portal accounts: [TechCrunch coverage of STARLINK vulnerability](https://techcrunch.com/2025/01/23/hackers-found-a-way-to-remotely-unlock-start-and-track-millions-of-subarus/), [AutoNews/Reuters summary](https://www.autonews.com/technology/mobility/an-subaru-hack-sam-curry/), and analysis pieces like [TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/106499-subaru-starlink-vulnerability-exposed-millions-of-vehicles-unauthorized-access.html).
  - CAN bus / physical entry attacks: modern cars with trusted CAN networks can be attacked via exterior wiring (headlight/taillight connectors) — this is not Subaru‑only but applies to any modern vehicle; see explainers on CAN injection attacks and industry writeups: [Dr Ken Tindell CAN injection explainer](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/) and reporting ([The Register on CAN bus attacks](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/)).
  - Mitigation advice (practical, Aussie): fit a physical steering‑wheel club or Securon lock while parked overnight, disable/unlink telematics accounts before sale, update the car’s telematics software at an authorised dealer, install a verified aftermarket immobiliser or CAN intrusion detection alarm from an experienced installer, and keep keys in a Faraday pouch at home to stop relay theft.
- High‑priority aftermarket anti‑theft:
  - Mechanical: deadlocks, steering wheel/column lock.
  - Technical: verified alarm with proven CAN bus intrusion detection and remote‑immobiliser kill; professional fitment only.
  - Administrative: remove stored telematics credentials and insist on dealer applied security updates.

Engines:
- (Each engine subsection below is italicised in the header; engine codes and major components are bolded in‑line.)

*FA20DIT / FA20F — 2.0 L turbo (earlier turbo applications)*
- Known issues: direct injection turbo engines can show carbon build‑up on intake valves (typical of DI engines), occasional oil consumption reports and turbo/intercooler charge plumbing leaks if not serviced. Specialist writeups note FA‑family turbo quirks and need for careful maintenance ([MotorReviewer FA20DIT page](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=212)).
- Failure points:
  - Turbo oil feed/cool return lines and actuator failures on neglected units.
  - Carbon build‑up requiring walnut blasting if symptoms (rough idle, misfire) appear.
- Preventative service:
  - Oil change every 7,500–10,000 km if using synthetic and spirited driving; shorter intervals for towing or high‑load use.
  - Intake cleaning at 120–160k km if symptoms arise.
  - Keep turbo oil lines and hoses checked at every service.

*FA24F — 2.4 L turbo (XT/Ascent/modern turbo Outback)*
- Known issues: reports of turbocharger faults, boost‑related fault codes and occasional intermittent power loss. Owners report turbo related work on high‑km units — see a focused owner/tech write up: [CherishYourCar on 2.4 turbo issues](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/subaru-2-4-turbo-engine-problems/).
- Failure points:
  - Turbocharger actuator, intercooler hose splits, and divergence in boost control solenoids.
  - Heat and oil degradation from poor service history.
- Preventative service:
  - Oil and filter change every 7,500–10,000 km under Australian conditions where frequent short trips are common.
  - Replace intake and boost hoses at signs of softening, and check PCV system regularly.
  - Keep conservative service logs if you plan long tours/towing.

*FB25 — 2.5 L naturally aspirated boxer (common in 2015+ Outbacks)*
- Known issues: modern FB engines are relatively reliable compared to the old EJ family, but owners still report higher than expected oil consumption in some model years and occasional service bulletins. General engine problem roundups and owner threads capture this: [EngineCrux FB/EJ overview](https://enginecrux.com/subaru-outback-engine-problems-facts-and-insights/) and owner forum collection on oil consumption ([SubaruOutback.org on oil consumption](https://www.subaruoutback.org/threads/known-subaru-engine-oil-consumption-issue-service-bulletin.564905/)).
- Failure points:
  - Oil consumption across particular production runs — monitor oil levels between service intervals.
  - Head gasket issues are mostly historical in EJ engines, but always check compression and service records on older cars: [HiRide on head gasket symptoms (EJ era)](https://hiride.com/subaru-outback-head-gasket-leak-symptoms/).
- Preventative service:
  - Regular oil checks; recommended oil change every 10,000 km for normal Australian use, shorter for motorway towing or high load.
  - Valve cover and PCV checks at 60k–100k km to reduce oil blow‑by.
  - Ask seller for service records confirming oil top‑ups or warranty service bulletins.

*EJ family (historic) — older Outbacks pre‑2010s*
- Known issues: the infamous EJ25 head gasket era (late 1990s–2000s) with higher failure rates — check history on any older trade‑ins. Documented extensively here: [Jalopnik on worst EJ years](https://jalopnik.com/2036717/subaru-model-years-with-head-gasket-problems/) and tech writeups from performance shops ([IAG Performance EJ fail article](https://www.iagperformance.com/blog/why-subaru-ej-engines-failand-what-you-can-do-to-prevent-it/)).
- Failure points:
  - Head gaskets, oil pump/galley issues, and coolant leakage.
- Preventative service:
  - On known EJ cars expect head‑gasket checks and possible MLS gasket upgrades as part of a long‑term ownership plan.

Transmission:
- The Outback uses Subaru’s Lineartronic CVT family. Known failure modes across the brand include shudder, chain slip/break, AT TEMP warnings, and torque converter shudder. Several service bulletins and NHTSA/TSB documents have been issued for CVT chain slip and valve body changes on certain model years, and Subaru extended CVT warranty programs for affected units ([NHTSA/TSB PDF on chain slip and recall](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2023/MC-10231303-0001.pdf); Subaru CVT warranty extension bulletin: [MC-11021247-0001](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2025/MC-11021247-0001.pdf)). Aggregated complaint data shows a large volume across Subaru Lineartronic applications: [ProblemsByVin Lineartronic aggregation](https://problemsbyvin.com/transmissions/subaru-lineartronic-cvt/).
- Symptoms to watch during test drive:
  - RPM flare with no corresponding acceleration (chain slip).
  - AT OIL TEMP/gearbox warning lights.
  - Harsh, pronounced shudder on take‑off or low speed lock‑up shudder.
  - Delayed engagement or clunky downshifts (simulated by CVT step shifts).
- What to demand on inspection:
  - Full service history showing CVT fluid changes at correct intervals (many owners say longevity improves with interval CVT fluid attention).
  - Proof of TSB/recall fixes if the car is 2019–2021 turbo models (these were included in certain TSBs).
  - If symptoms exist, insist on diagnostic reports or a recent dealer inspection. Rebuilt CVTs or replacements are expensive — budget multiple thousands if the box needs work ([CarComplaints transmission cost data](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Subaru/Outback/transmission/index.shtml)).

#### 3. Should you buy a used Subaru Outback?

- Marketplace pricing (NSW snapshot): active listings 33; price range AUD $16,990 – $62,990; median price AUD $35,990; year range 2016–2026; median odometer ~50,921 km; automatic heavy in the market (28 of 33 transmissions are automatic). Use these local market facts to anchor negotiations — this is a live NSW dealer snapshot supplied for this review.

- Depreciation picture:
  - The Outback’s long‑term depreciation is steeper than some Japanese rivals: CarEdge and iSeeCars note typical 5‑year depreciation around the 40–50% mark depending on trim and market; FIXD charts show first five years can be close to 50% in a worst case. See [CarEdge depreciation summary](https://caredge.com/subaru/outback/depreciation), [iSeeCars resale comparison](https://www.iseecars.com/car/subaru-outback/resale-value) and [FIXD depreciation curve](https://fixdapp.com/car-buying/subaru-outback-depreciation-rate-curve-graphed/).
  - In simple terms: you’ll usually pay less up front than a RAV4 or CR‑V used equivalent, but you’ll also recover less on resale.

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy:
  - Start with these pillars: depreciation, fuel, servicing/repairs (especially CVT and turbo upkeep), insurance, and tyres.
  - Fuel: real‑world owner data (Fuelly) for the 2025 Outback shows about 24.9 mpg (US) average across owners — roughly 9.4 L/100km — use that as a baseline for normal mixed driving; official EPA or international ratings are comparable and searchable ([Fuelly 2025 Outback](https://www.fuelly.com/car/subaru/outback/2025) and fueleconomy.gov data). For Aussie fuel prices and stop‑start city work your fuel bill will be higher than that combined number.
  - Preventative reserves: set aside AUD $2,500–$4,000 over five years for medium services, belts/hoses, brakes/rotors and small turbo/charging plumbing jobs. Add another AUD $6k–$12k contingency for a CVT replacement if you buy an older model with suspect transmission history — this is worst‑case but real on high‑km cars.
  - Hybrid/turbo premium: Subaru hasn’t pushed a full hybrid Outback in all markets; where hybrid or turbo variants exist they carry a price premium. Use KBB’s hybrid vs gas break‑even logic: hybrids only pay back if you do a lot of urban stop/start kilometres and fuel is very pricey relative to the premium you paid — otherwise the conventional 2.5 petrol often wins on simple TCO given Subaru’s resale patterns ([KBB hybrid vs gas break‑even analysis](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/)) and Edmunds’ True Cost to Own breakdowns ([Edmunds TCO](https://www.edmunds.com/subaru/outback/2025/cost-to-own/)).
  - If you drive long highway kilometres with a trailer/tow load, the turbo XT can be more appropriate despite the premium; for mixed family use in Australia the NA 2.5L tends to be the more cost‑sensible option.

- Buying recommendation (plain blunt Aussie advice):
  - If you want a reliable, practical wagon with real AWD capability and sensible running costs, the Outback is an excellent tool — but buy carefully and expect to inspect electrical and CVT history.
  - Price sweet spot by market snapshot: with NSW median at AUD $35,990 and typical odometers ~50k km, the best financial sweet spot is often late‑2017 to 2020 models with the 2.5L boxer and Premium/Limited trim:
    - Why? They’re modern enough to have EyeSight and contemporary safety kit, but old enough to have absorbed initial teething updates on infotainment and CVT firmware. A 2018–2020 **2.5i Premium/Limited** with clean service history, proof of CVT fluid changes and dealer ADAS updates typically gives the best balance of price, depreciation and remaining life.
  - Avoid (or buy with extreme caution):
    - Vehicles with recurring ADAS fault history (phantom braking) unless the dealer can prove dealership firmware updates and ADAS recalibration were performed.
    - High‑odometer turbo XT models without documented turbo and charge plumbing service — turbo replacements are not cheap.
    - Cars showing infotainment delamination, unless the owner has replaced the head unit — replacement can be expensive.
  - Inspect before you buy:
    - Full Subaru dealer service history with documented CVT fluid/TCM updates and any NHTSA/TSB fixes implemented. Confirm whether recalls or warranty extensions applied (see Subaru TSBs and recalls pages).
    - Test drive for CVT shudder, AT TEMP warnings, phantom braking from EyeSight (test in a range of conditions but be safe), and verify infotainment operation across inputs (Apple CarPlay/Android Auto, backup camera, wireless charger function).
    - Mechanical inspection by an independent Subaru‑savvy mechanic who can scan for historical DTCs (stored fault codes) and look for oil consumption signs, turbo leaks, and transmission fault records.

Final one‑sentence verdict: Buy a Subaru Outback if you want a genuinely practical, year‑round AWD wagon that handles bad roads and school runs better than most SUVs — but only after vetting its CVT and ADAS history, favouring 2018–2020 2.5‑litre Premium/Limited examples with full service records as the best used‑market compromise in NSW price bands (median AUD $35,990, 50,921 km in the live market snapshot provided).
