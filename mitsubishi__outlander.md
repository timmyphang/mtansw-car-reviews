#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation / timeframe / codes: Fourth‑generation Mitsubishi Outlander (global re‑launch from 2021; widely sold 2022–2026 refresh cycle). The current model family in market listings runs from about 2014–2026 in NSW dealer stock (older third‑gen carryovers through early fourth‑gen examples appear in used stock). The 2022+ models are the common “new Outlander” you’ll see on the forecourt and in press coverage [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Outlander), manufacturer pages and technical bulletins [Mitsubishi Motors](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/outlander).
- Where it sits in Mitsubishi’s lineup: The Outlander is Mitsubishi’s mid‑sized family SUV — above the compact ASX/RVR and below the larger Pajero/Montero/Delica type offerings in global markets. There’s also the Outlander PHEV sibling which shares the Outlander name but different electrified underpinnings and equipment focus.
- Platform & parts commonality: Modern Outlanders share CMF‑CD/Renault‑Nissan derived architecture lineage in the recent generation, which helps parts availability and commonality with Nissan Rogue/X‑Trail derivatives and some alliance platform components [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Outlander). That means you’ll find a reasonable spares market and shared accessory fitments in Australia.
- Engines, outputs, transmissions and drive configurations (market‑specific summary):
  - Petrol 2.5‑litre four‑cylinder, ~181 hp (common in 2022–2026 lineups) — Continuously Variable Transmission (CVT) or Mitsubishi’s automatic CVT variants; available in FWD and AWD in various trims [Edmunds](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/outlander/2025/features-specs/).
  - Plug‑in Hybrid (PHEV) — electrified drivetrain combining petrol engine and electric motors; e‑drive behaviour and battery capacity vary by model year and market. See EPA and manufacturer comparisons for efficiency claims [FuelEconomy.gov](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/Find.do?action=sbs&id=48676), [Mitsubishi Motors](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/outlander).
  - Diesel 2.2‑litre (4N14) — sold in some markets; known in technical writeups and owner reports (more common outside Australia but appears in global service literature) [EngineExplained](https://engineexplained.com/mitsubishi-4n14-2-2-l-engine-review/).
  - Older petrol 2.4L engines (4B12) appear in earlier model years and show up in service notes and complaint databases [TimingBeltInfo / Go‑Parts](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0017-mitsubishi-outlander-2008-2020-4b12-2-4l).
- Explicit marketplace rivals in Australia: Toyota RAV4 (and RAV4 Hybrid), Hyundai Santa Fe, Kia Sorento, Honda CR‑V, Mazda CX‑5 — plus mid‑sized three‑row rivals like the Volkswagen Tiguan Allspace depending on trim and passenger requirements [CarsGuide comparison](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/outlander/vs), [KBB/Edmunds comparisons](https://www.edmunds.com/car-comparisons/mitsubishi-outlander-vs-2026-toyota-rav4/).

NSW dealer snapshot (live): 56 active listings; price range AUD $12,990 – $59,990; median price $34,935; year range 2014–2026; median odometer 50,395 km; transmission mix: Automatic 52, CVT 2, Manual 1. Use that as the market baseline for negotiation.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mitsubishi Outlander?
High‑level note: the Outlander is promising on value and packaging, but recurring themes in owner feedback and TSB/recall records keep popping up — infotainment/software, CVT drivability, timing‑chain/tensioner and paint/ corrosion issues. Read on for the checklist you’ll need at inspection.

Exterior:
- Panel alignment & trim:
  - Late‑model Outlanders generally line up well, but owners report loose centre‑console and lower trim panels (2022–2025 models) — cosmetic but noisy and irritating for family use; expect to inspect and verify clips on a test‑drive [Go‑Parts guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/console-trim-panel-mitsubishi-outlander-mitsubishi-outlander-phev-2022-2025).
  - Gloss black/painted trims can scratch; look for scuffs on bumpers and lower sills typical of family SUVs.
- Paint durability & rust:
  - Multiple owner threads and independent writeups flag paint chipping—particularly hood “peppering” and exposed metal that accelerates corrosion if left untreated. Many owners fit PPF or touch up early to avoid spread [NorthTints/enginecrux summaries](https://northtints.com/blogs/ppf/mitsubishi-ppf, https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-outlander-rust-problems-facts-and-insights/).
  - There are subframe corrosion concerns across some Mitsubishi ranges; salt‑belt states (Aust. coastal/southern winter roads) increase risk. If you find heavy surface rust on suspension mounts, subframe or strut towers, get a structural inspection [Go‑Parts subframe rust overview](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/suspension-subframe-mitsubishi-outlander-sport-mitsubishi-rvr-mitsubishi-outlander-2007-2025).
- Recalls & safety system hardware:
  - Infotainment recall(s) impacting 2022+ models for A‑IVI freezing/black screens and rear‑view camera loss: Mitsubishi has issued owner notifications/recalls — check VIN for completeness at dealer [CarComplaints coverage](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2025/mitsubishi-outlander-infotainment-system-problems-recall.shtml) and manufacturer recall tools [Mitsubishi open recalls](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls).

Interior:
- Durability for heavy duty (rideshare, frequent family use):
  - Seats and trim are generally robust but the weak points are soft‑touch console clips and a wireless charging pad assembly that can overheat, stop charging or suffer connector failures — owners report the pad cutting out after a few minutes [Go‑Parts charging pad thread](https://www.outlanderforums.com/threads/charging-pad.524/) and USB/wireless assembly guides [Go‑Parts USB pad guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/usb-port-mitsubishi-outlander-mitsubishi-outlander-phev-nissan-rogue-2021-2025).
  - Headliner sag and scuffs on plastic under heavy use are common cheap fixes but reduce perceived value.
- Entry/egress and space:
  - The Outlander’s 5+2 seating (third row) is competitive for kids; watch third‑row seat latching and carpet wear in used examples. Door sill heights are family‑friendly — not truck‑tall — and door checks are generally robust.
- Electrical & infotainment:
  - Infotainment system: 2022–2025 owners widely reported freezing/black screens on the 9‑inch and newer 12.3‑inch A‑IVI units — so check power‑on, reverse camera, Apple CarPlay/Android Auto connections, and ask for proof of completed recall/service bulletin repairs if it’s a 2022+ car [Go‑Parts infotainment guides](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/audio-system-mitsubishi-outlander-mitsubishi-outlander-phev-2022-2025), [CarComplaints](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2025/mitsubishi-outlander-infotainment-system-problems-recall.shtml).
  - 12V battery drain patterns: owners report occasional parasitic draws after software faults (infotainment/ECU sleeping). If a used car has low battery history or recent replacements, ask for details and a battery health test.
  - Screen sizes vary by trim: the 2024–2026 range can have 9‑inch or 12.3‑inch displays depending on trim and packages — confirm you’re getting the hardware you checked [Mitsubishi trim pages](https://www.mitsubishicars.com/cars-and-suvs/outlander/trims).
  - Connectivity reliability: Bluetooth Dropouts and USB charging faults are among the common minor complaints; carry out a full smartphone pairing test during inspection.

Mechanically:
- Brand engineering reputation:
  - Mitsubishi’s current Outlander is competent mechanically, but not class‑leading. Owners praise packaging and AWD hardware but many recurring defects are electronics/software and CVT drivability rather than catastrophic mechanical design failures [RepairPal / MotorTrend coverage](https://repairpal.com/problems/mitsubishi/outlander, https://www.motortrend.com/cars/mitsubishi/outlander/2026).
- Theft & digital security:
  - Modern Outlanders use CAN networks and smart‑key architectures that are theoretically vulnerable to CAN‑injection and headlight/footwell access attacks that have affected many brands. This is not an Outlander‑specific exploit list, but the risk is real; specialist security writeups recommend layered protection — steering wheel locks, aftermarket immobiliser or CAN‑gateway intrusion detection — if you live in areas with keyless theft activity [TheRegister / Ken Tindell analysis / Millercat mitigation guide](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/, https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/, https://millercat.com/blogs/news/how-to-protect-your-vehicle-against-can-bus-theft).
- Practical anti‑theft measures:
  - Fit a visible steering wheel lock as cheap, effective deterrent.
  - If you’re paranoid (you should be in some suburbs), get a dealer‑fitted immobiliser/CanGate or an aftermarket CAN gateway alarm with intrusion detection wiring in the headlight harness area.

Engines:
- Note: every engine subsection below is specifically italicised and includes the engine code or identifier in bold as the headline.

*_**4B12 – 2.4L petrol (older 2008–2020 models)**_*
- Known faults: timing‑chain stretch/tensioner wear that can lead to cam/crank correlation codes (P0016/P0017) and risk major valve/piston damage if ignored. Oil control valve (OCV) and camshaft phaser faults feature in diagnostic threads [Go‑Parts timing code P0017 discussion](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0017-mitsubishi-outlander-2008-2020-4b12-2-4l).
- Preventative maintenance:
  - Keep oil fresh and at correct spec — frequent oil changes help chain tensioner life. For Australian use, favour a 10,000 km oil change interval if you do lots of short runs/rideshare work; 15,000 km might be acceptable for primarily highway drivers but increases risk on older chains (recommendation based on typical workshop guidance — check service book).
  - At 100,000–150,000 km have timing chain and tensioner visually inspected if noisy; replace tensioner guides on first signs of rattle.

*_**2.5L MIVEC – 4‑cylinder petrol (current 2022–2026 mainstream)**_*
- Known faults: generally more reliable than older 2.4 in owner surveys, but expect typical 2.5L family petrol care points — oil consumption reports are moderate; overheating/cooling issues are occasionally reported in older model discussions. No major class‑wide catastrophic failure trend in the 2.5L as of available TSB/owner data [Edmunds / MotorTrend](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/outlander/2025/features-specs/, https://www.motortrend.com/cars/mitsubishi/outlander/2026).
- Preventative maintenance:
  - Oil service: 10,000 km intervals recommended for stop/start city drivers; up to 15,000 km acceptable for long‑run commuters if oil spec per book is met.
  - Watch for coolant thermostat/water‑pump issues in any petrol engine — check coolant service history every 100,000 km.

*_**4N14 – 2.2L DI‑D diesel**_*
- Known faults: turbocharger issues, DPF (diesel particulate filter) headaches if the vehicle is used short‑run a lot, and excessive oil consumption plus timing‑chain related complaints on some DI‑D family engines [EngineExplained](https://engineexplained.com/mitsubishi-4n14-2-2-l-engine-review/).
- Preventative maintenance:
  - If the Outlander sees lots of urban work, consider a petrol or PHEV — diesels need regular highway runs to self‑regenerate DPF.
  - Injector diagnosis and DPF status checks are essential before purchase; expect higher cost to rectify turbo/injector or DPF replacements.

*_**PHEV drivetrain (Outlander PHEV)**_*
- Known failure points: electrical module cooling, battery health, charger/AEV software bugs and typical hybrid inverter/electronic faults documented in owner threads. The PHEV gives genuinely low fuel use if regularly charged, but mixed‑use owner results vary widely [Fuelly & Mitsubishi PHEV calculator](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/Find.do?action=sbs&id=48676, https://www.mitsubishi-motors.ca/en/electrification/phev-vs-gas-calculator).
- Preventative maintenance:
  - Validate battery State‑of‑Health and charge cycle history — obtain a battery report where possible.
  - Check 12V battery and hybrid cooling circuits; ask for full dealership service history and any software updates applied.

Transmission:
- CVT (continuously variable transmission) and CVT‑related automatics:
  - CVT behaviour: smooth and efficient when healthy; common symptoms of trouble are shudder, surge, loss of drive, and judder on take‑off — owners in many markets report such symptoms. Mitsubishi and independent TSBs instruct dealers to reprogram CVT ECU and follow diagnostic procedures for shudder/surge conditions [TSB2023001 / Mitsubishi Powertrain TSB links](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2022/MC-10208827-0001.pdf, https://mitsubishitechinfo.com/data/TECH_DOCS/2017/52006827/HTML/520068272.htm).
  - Known failure modes include valve body wear, dirty/low fluid, torque converter solenoid faults and ECU software needing updates. There are documented service bulletins requiring software updates (SR‑16‑006 and later numbers) — ask the dealer to show completed TSB work and request the CVT ECU flash record if available.
  - Older torque converter codes like P0740 have been reported on pre‑2014 units — check for transmission fault history, service records and test drives for slipping or harsh engagement [Go‑Parts P0740 info](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0740-mitsubishi-outlander-2007-2013).
- Practical buying check:
  - Test drive from cold and hot. CVT shudder or lag is easiest to spot under light acceleration and when cruising up a short rise. Ask for a specialist transmission code read (not just scanner reset). A recent fluid change with proper CVT fluid and dealer‑flash history is a comfort factor.

ADAS / Driver Assistance and Electronics:
- The adaptive cruise control (ACC) and other ADAS systems have owner reports of unexpected shutdowns, warning lights and inconsistent behaviour — TSBs and NHTSA complaint logs exist for 2024–2025 models [CarsafetyDB / NHTSA entries](https://carsafetydb.com/mitsubishi/outlander/2025/driver-assist, https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle/2025/MITSUBISHI/OUTLANDER/SUV/FWD).
- Dealers have issued TSBs recommending diagnostics and reprogramming/calibration of ADAS sensors — if ADAS features are important to you, demand a full calibration and evidence of completed software updates for forward camera and radars [TSB listing and Go‑Parts ADAS guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/video-camera-mitsubishi-outlander-mitsubishi-outlander-phev-2017-2025, https://www.carcomplaints.com/).
- Bottom line: ADAS is useful but don’t buy a used Outlander for “self‑driving” expectations — verify each feature during inspection.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mitsubishi Outlander?
- NSW used market pricing (use the scraper snapshot above): expect to pay between AUD $12,990 and $59,990 on active listings (56 at time of snapshot); median asking price sits at AUD $34,935 with a median odometer ~50,395 km. Transmission split heavily favours automatic/CVT units — only a handful of manual or old gearbox models remain in stock (Automatic: 52, CVT: 2, Manual: 1).
- Depreciation metrics:
  - iSeeCars reports Outlander depreciation approximately: 3‑year ~27.6%, 5‑year ~47.5% (rough ballpark from aggregated US data) — Outlander loses resale value faster than class leaders like the Toyota RAV4 [iSeeCars depreciation summary](https://www.iseecars.com/car/mitsubishi-outlander/resale-value). Expect stronger depreciation if the car is a PHEV in certain markets or if ADAS/infotainment recalls were not completed.
- TCO strategy & comparison (how to think about costs):
  - Basic TCO variables to capture: purchase price (use NSW median as baseline), finance cost, insurance (check insurer’s Outlander PHEV vs petrol premium), fuel/energy, scheduled maintenance, tyres, and depreciation.
  - For mainstream 2.5L petrol Outlander: factor mid‑range fuel use (urban/combined driving in real‑world will typically put a modern 2.5 SUV in the mid‑8s to low‑10s L/100km depending on load and style — owner and journal sources imply this spread; exact consumption will vary) and moderate service costs. Use KBB/Edmunds/CarEdge TCO pages to model 5‑year ownership [KBB/Edmunds/CarEdge TCO pages](https://www.kbb.com/mitsubishi/outlander/2025/cost-to-own/, https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/outlander-phev/2026/cost-to-own/, https://caredge.com/mitsubishi/outlander/costs).
  - For Outlander PHEV: the math only works if you regularly charge — plugged‑in suburban commuters see dramatic fuel savings and lower running fuel bills; mixed long‑distance users may see much less benefit. Real‑world owner fuel figures on Fuelly show wide variation — some owners achieve very low petrol usage, others average a few litres/100km because of infrequent charging or highway trips [Fuelly Outlander PHEV results](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/outlander_phev). Mitsubishi’s own PHEV vs gas calculator is handy for scenario planning [Mitsubishi PHEV calculator](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.ca/en/electrification/phev-vs-gas-calculator).
  - Maintenance & repair risk premium: factor in potential for non‑trivial repairs if CVT issues reappear, plus the chance of an infotainment module replacement (recall work might be covered if recorded). PHEV owners should budget for hybrid battery assessments if buying older high‑km PHEVs.
- Is paying a premium for PHEV worth it in Australia?
  - If your driving is primarily short suburban runs and you can reliably charge at home/work daily, a PHEV can dramatically cut fuel bills — the Edmunds/KBB TCO tools show lower fuel line items, but the initial premium and potential battery service costs complicate the math [Edmunds/KBB TCO](https://www.edmunds.com/mitsubishi/outlander-phev/2026/cost-to-own/).
  - If you do lots of interstate/highway travel without frequent top‑ups, a petrol 2.5 is a simpler, lower‑risk long‑term ownership proposition. Use Fuelly owner data to model likely real‑world consumption for the PHEV before paying a premium [Fuelly](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/outlander_phev).
- Buying recommendation & sweet spot:
  - If you want pragmatic value and fewer electronic headaches: pick a late‑model (2019–2021) 2.5L with full dealer service history, CVT ECU software update records and no outstanding recalls. Price sweet spot in NSW market: used stock shows good bargains under AUD $30k for 2018–2020 cars with reasonable km; the median $34,935 usually buys you a low‑km 2020–2022 example.
  - Want the PHEV? Buy with a battery health report and evidence the car was regularly charged by the previous owner. PHEV is worth a premium only if your commute allows frequent charging; otherwise the petrol model is the safer financial bet.
  - Avoid: cheap high‑km examples with incomplete service history, unresolved infotainment or CVT TSBs, or visible subframe corrosion. If the dealer cannot show recall/TSB completion, price that into negotiation or walk.
  - Best value trim/year: mid‑trim 2020–2022 2.5L AWD (if you need traction) with a documented service history and recorded CVT/infotainment firmware updates. That combination tends to hit the market sweet spot between price, equipment and reliability risk.

Quick buying checklist (what to demand at inspection)
- Full service history and stamped logbook; ask for dates/records of CVT ECU reprogramming and any infotainment recall repairs.
- VIN‑level recall search and proof of completion (Mitsubishi recall lookup) — don’t rely on “I think it was done” [Mitsubishi recall tool](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls).
- Test drive: cold start to fully hot; evaluate CVT for shudder/surge, check infotainment/wireless charger/USB functionality, test ADAS features (ACC, lane assist) and check for warnings.
- Underbody inspection: check for paint peeling, hood chips, underbody/subframe corrosion as highlighted in owner writeups [EngineCrux / Go‑Parts](https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-outlander-rust-problems-facts-and-insights/, https://www.go-parts.com/garage/suspension-subframe-mitsubishi-outlander-sport-mitsubishi-rvr-mitsubishi-outlander-2007-2025).
- PHEV buyers: request a battery State‑of‑Health report and charging history; plug the car into a charger and check charging acceptance.

Final blunt take: the Outlander is a strongly practical family SUV for undercutting more expensive rivals on price and equipment, and the PHEV can be a suburban money‑saver if you charge. But don’t buy on looks alone — insist on proof of CVT/infotainment/ADAS software completion, a solid service history, and a careful underbody check. Pay a few hundred bucks for a pre‑purchase inspection that focuses on CVT behaviour, timing‑chain noise for older petrols, and a PHEV battery health report. With that, you’ll have a roomy, well‑priced family wagon that’s a sensible, straightforward buy in the Aussie market — and still leaves you petrol money to spend on the next service or a set of good tyres.
