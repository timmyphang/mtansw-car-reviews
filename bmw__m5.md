#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: F90 (F90/F91/F92 chassis family for the 5 Series M‑variants) — production span for the F90 M5 sedan: roughly 2017–2023. The newest full‑model update is the G90 (2024+), which moves the M5 to a plug‑in hybrid architecture. See the BMW timeline and model pages for the family history and the new hybrid M5 details: [Wikipedia — BMW M5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BMW_M5) and [BMW M (G90) overview](https://www.bmw-m.com/en/all-models/overview-m-and-m-performance/bmw-m5-sedan/2024/bmw-m5-sedan.html).
- Product hierarchy: The M5 sits at the top of the 5 Series sedan line — above M Performance 540i/M550i derivatives and underneath BMW’s full‑blown super‑saloon/halo cars (M8, XM, and limited CS/CS‑like special editions). The M5 is BMW M GmbH’s benchmark four‑door weapon in the executive sport sedan segment (BMW M resources: [BMW M generations overview](https://www.bmw-m.com/en/topics/magazine-article-pool/the-generations-of-the-bmw-m5-an-overview.html)).
- Shared platform architecture: The F90 M5 is a heavily reworked 5 Series platform (CLAR‑family related architecture shared across BMW’s executive cars). The new generation (G90) shares underpinnings with the contemporary 5 Series and BMW XM, which helps parts and labour availability across the BMW dealer network and specialist shops. See BMW’s technical pages for model platform context: [BMW USA technical highlights (M5)](https://www.bmwusa.com/vehicles/m-series/m5-series/bmw-m5-sedan-technical-highlights.html) and model notes at [autoevolution](https://www.autoevolution.com/bmw/m5/).
- Market‑specific engine & drivetrain line‑up (Australian used‑market focus)
  - F90 (2018–2023, incl. 2021 listings you’ll see):
    - Primary engine: 4.4‑litre twin‑turbo V8 — **S63** family (standard M5 and Competition outputs vary by tune). Outputs typically around 441–460 kW in Competition guise depending on model year and spec; factory‑paired with an 8‑speed M Steptronic auto.
    - Transmission: ZF‑based 8‑speed torque‑converter automatic (M Steptronic) with M xDrive AWD as standard on later builds; capable of full‑RWD mode for purists.
    - Drive configurations: AWD (M xDrive) with selectable RWD bias.
  - G90 (2024+): Plug‑in hybridised 4.4 V8 + e‑motor; massive power figures and electric assist — see BMW M press material for hybrid outputs and electric range: [BMW M — new M5 (G90) tech](https://www.bmw-m.com/en/all-models/overview-m-and-m-performance/bmw-m5-sedan/2024/bmw-m5-sedan.html).
- Explicit rivals in this segment (what Aussie buyers will compare it against):
  - Audi RS6/RS7 (Avant/Saloon), Mercedes‑AMG E63 S (Saloon/Estate), Porsche Panamera GTS/Turbo, and the new crop of performance SUVs like Porsche Cayenne Turbo / BMW X5 M (if you want space). See rivalry overviews at [Carwow](https://www.carwow.co.uk/editorial/best-cars/alternatives/best-bmw-m5-alternatives) and [TopSpeed list of alternatives](https://www.topspeed.com/bmw-m5-alternatives/).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used BMW M5?
This is where the love affair gets practical. Below are the real‑owner failure patterns and tech‑shop headaches to expect on F90/F‑era M5s and the family lineage. Sources are owner forums, technical guides and recall/TSB documents.

- Exterior
  - Panel fit and trim:
    - Cosmetic fitment/gap complaints show up in owner forums — rear bumper corner sag/gap and occasional panel misalignment after minor impacts or fitment clips breaking are common. See owner threads: [rear bumper fitment thread (M5 board)](https://www.m5board.com/threads/sag-gap-in-rear-bumper-fitment.621514/) and general trim‑peel guidance: [Go‑Parts exterior trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-bmw-5-series-bmw-i5-bmw-m5-2016-2026).
    - Gloss black and painted trim can scratch or peel; some roof‑glass sealing/peel issues have been reported by owners (G90 forum threads): [Bimmerpost thread on weird peeling](https://g90.bimmerpost.com/forums/showthread/2198081/weird-peeling).
  - Paint durability:
    - Typical high‑end BMW paint — good if looked after, but the cost to respray panels or repair clearcoat failure is not cheap. Check for front‑end stone chips (bonnet and lower bumpers) and for overspray/repair marks in the wheel arches.
  - Body recalls / safety system hardware:
    - The M5 has been part of broader BMW recall campaigns over different years. Use BMW recall lookup for VIN‑specific checks: [BMW USA Recall portal](https://my.bmwusa.com/safety-and-emission-recalls/manage). Aggregated recall history is tracked in NHTSA datasets and recall aggregators: [RecallExplained M5 page](https://recallexplained.com/bmw/m5/).
  - ADAS issues:
    - Owners report intermittent “Driving Assistance Reduced” or lane‑assist failures requiring BMW‑level diagnostics or radar/camera cleaning and recalibration. TSBs note camera/radar misreports and the need for dealer calibration: [NHTSA TSB on camera KAFAS issues](https://static.nhtsa.gov/odi/tsbs/2023/MC-10236236-9999.pdf) and [Go‑Parts ADAS failure guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/collision-avoidance-sensor-bmw-8-series-bmw-x5-bmw-x7-2016-2027).
    - Be wary of phantom AEB activations — a few consumer complaints exist: [AboutAutomobile consumer complaint on forward collision](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Consumer-Complaint/2025/BMW/M5/Forward-Collision-Avoidance-Warnings).

- Interior
  - Durability under hard use:
    - Leather bolsters and merino surfaces have owner reports of premature wrinkling/wear; if you intend rideshare or taxi‑style heavy use, expect faster wear on seat bolsters and steering wheel trim (forums: [G90 leather quality thread](https://g90.bimmerpost.com/forums/showthread/2188726/leather-quality)).
    - Door sill height and entry: the M5 is a low executive sedan — easy enough for daily use but wide bolsters and low seating can make repeated ingress/egress a little tighter than a softer 5 Series.
  - Electrical/infotainment quirks:
    - iDrive head unit black screens, reboots or frozen displays aren’t rare; many cases are fixed by software or head unit re‑flash, but hardware failures require replacement (~expensive). See diagnostic guides: [Go‑Parts iDrive head unit failure guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/audio-video-module-bmw-x3-bmw-x5-bmw-m5-2016-2026) and general update advice: [BMW software update page](https://www.bmwusa.com/owners/software-updates.html).
    - Wireless phone chargers (Qi) in centre consoles can overheat or fail intermittently — documented by parts guides: [Go‑Parts wireless charger notes](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/front-center-console-wireless-phone-charger-control-module-bmw-530i-bmw-540i-bmw-x3-2017-2024).
    - 12V battery drain: modern BMWs with many convenience modules are susceptible to parasitic drains if modules get stuck. If a used car shows repeated flat 12V batteries, treat that as a red flag — track history and have a dealer/system scan done.
  - Connectivity & screen sizes across trims:
    - iDrive screen sizes and head unit capabilities varied by model years/options — later cars have larger screens and improved connectivity, but check that Over‑The‑Air (OTA) or dealer I‑Level updates were performed (BMW update guidance: [BMW Software Update guide](https://www.bmwusa.com/owners/software-updates.html)).

- Mechanically
  - Brand engineering reputation:
    - BMW M engineering is high‑performance but high‑maintenance compared with mainstream cars. Expect higher consumable costs (brakes, tyres, filters) and premium parts/labour rates at dealers and specialist workshops.
  - Security vulnerabilities:
    - Keyless‑entry relay attacks are an industry problem; newer vector: CAN injection attacks where attackers access wiring behind lights to spoof the immobiliser. These are not BMW‑specific but affect modern keyless cars. Read up: [The Register on CAN bus theft](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/), and overview theft methods: [Autoblog on headlight CAN injection](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update).
    - Recommended countermeasures:
      - Physical wheel/steering‑wheel locks, locking wheel nuts, or mechanical devices in the transmission tunnel.
      - Aftermarket immobiliser/CAN‑intrusion detection units for long‑term secure parking.
      - OBD port locking and VIN‑specific alarms; keep keys in signal‑blocking pouches at home.
  - Serviceability:
    - Many repairs require BMW factory diagnostics and software calibration (especially ADAS and drivetrain software), which pushes repair bills to dealer or high‑end independent specialists.

- Engines
  - Note: every engine below is listed as an italicised subsection and a short checklist of known failure modes, taken from owner reports and technical reference material.

  - *S63 (4.4L twin‑turbo V8, current F90 M5 family)*
    - Known failure points:
      - Oil leaks — valve cover gaskets and oil filter housing seals can leak over time.
      - VANOS and high‑pressure oil system quirks — solenoids and actuators can fail and throw drivability codes.
      - Excess oil consumption reported on some builds; ignition coils/plugs wear faster on high‑output tunes.
      - Rod bearing wear has been reported in high‑mileage, enthusiast‑driven cars (similar patterns exist across high‑output V8s).
      - Cam/tappet ticking noises — needs proper diagnostic to separate VANOS/tappet vs camshaft wear. Tech discussion: [S63 camshaft guide (Go‑Parts)](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/engine-camshaft-bmw-m5-bmw-m8-bmw-x5-m-2018-2023).
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil changes every 10,000 km (or 12 months) with BMW‑approved synthetic oil; aggressive owners should do 7,500–10,000 km.
      - Replace spark plugs, coils and inspect for oil leaks at 60,000 km intervals as a rule of thumb.
      - Compression/borescope checks on pre‑purchase if the car has >80,000 km or a competition exhaust map.
      - Keep a record of turbo care (cool‑down/idling practices) if the seller is an enthusiast.
    - Sources: [EngineExplained S63 summary](https://www.engineexplained.com/bmw-m5-engine-problems-a-comprehensive-review/) and owner forums.

  - *S63TU (tuned/upgraded variants of S63 on later F90s)*
    - Known failure points:
      - Same families of faults as S63 with added stress from higher outputs — rod bearings and oiling system items need closer monitoring.
      - Mid‑production variations in gearbox/engine calibrations can affect drivability.
    - Preventative maintenance: same as S63, with more frequent inspections if the car has been remapped or track‑abused.

  - *S85 (V10, earlier E60/E61 M5, historical context)*
    - Known failure points (important if you’re cross‑shopping older M5s):
      - Rod‑bearing wear, VANOS pump/oiling faults and throttle actuator issues. These can be catastrophic and expensive if neglected. See deep dive: [S85 problems summary](https://engineexplained.com/bmw-s85-engine-problems-a-comprehensive-review/).
    - Preventative maintenance: tighter oil change cycles, attention to VANOS oil delivery.

  - *S62 (older V8 in E39 M5)*
    - Known failure points: cooling system parts, VANOS, and ignition/injector wear typical of older performance V8s. See historical guide: [E39 M5 common problems](https://southsideeuro.com/blog/bmw-m5-e39-common-problems-solutions/).

  - Service interval summary (practical Australian guidance):
    - General service cadence for performance M engines: 10,000 km or 12 months as a minimum (closer to 7,500–10,000 km for cars used enthusiastically). Use BMW dealer service history to check actual intervals — 15,000 km is common on some BMWs but for M cars stick to 10,000 km to preserve engine health.

- Transmission
  - The F90 M5 uses an 8‑speed torque‑converter automatic (ZF 8HP family) with M Steptronic calibration and M xDrive.
    - Known failure modes:
      - Early 8HP variants had mechatronic or solenoid‑related complaints; BMW ran stronger/updated units across production runs. Forum reporting suggests some builds had torque‑converter shudder or harsh shift issues that improved with later software or hardware units. See community thread on mid‑production ZF changes: [Bimmerpost transmission thread](https://f90.bimmerpost.com/forums/showthread.php?t=2048826) and ZF 8HP guidance: [ZF 8HP common issues](https://bmwinsights.com/zf-8hp-transmission-problems/).
      - Leaks from cooler lines or seals cause low fluid and shift problems; panic not — a professional fluid change or mechatronic rebuild is expensive but fixable.
      - On F10 M5s the DCT (dual‑clutch) had its own legacy issues (not directly relevant to F90 torque‑converter but instructive historically): [F10 DCT thread](https://f10.m5post.com/forums/showthread.php?t=2059667).
    - Maintenance:
      - Transmission fluid and filter service every 80–120,000 km depending on usage — but if you tow, track, or flog the car, shorten the service interval.
      - If a car shows shuddering or slip at cold start, get full transmission diagnostic scans and fluid check. Expect a dealer/independent diagnosis rather than a DIY fix.

- Marketplace pricing and depreciation
  - Entry price up to ceiling: In NSW right now the real dealer ceiling (and median) for a 2021 M5 sits at about AUD 150k per your snapshot. National used prices vary widely; historic depreciation trackers show M5s can fall ~45–55% over five years depending on era and condition. See depreciation data: [CarEdge depreciation summary](https://caredge.com/bmw/m5/depreciation) and [iSeeCars resale analysis](https://www.iseecars.com/car/bmw/m5/resale-value). BMWBlog’s tracker discusses differences between generations and relative values: [BMWBlog depreciation tracker](https://www.bmwblog.com/2026/06/06/bmw-m5-depreciation-tracker-f10-f90-g90/).
  - 3‑year baseline drop: expect a typical M5 (new‑car bought and well‑maintained) to lose somewhere between 30–40% in the first three years if it’s a modern F90 — Competition and low‑km examples will hold better.
- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — how to think about the money
  - Elements to include:
    - Depreciation (largest single cost) — compare the asking price vs indexed trade‑in values (use CarEdge/KBB to get an objective baseline).
    - Fuel: V8 M5s are thirsty; vendor and owner‑reported real‑world consumption varies but expect 10–16 L/100 km for spirited mixed driving (owner data at [Fuelly](https://www.fuelly.com/car/bmw/m5)). The plug‑in G90 M5 can post factory combined figures like 1.6 L/100 km on paper for mixed electric use — real‑world electricity consumption and charge‑behaviour matter: [Auto‑Data G90 fuel figures](https://www.auto-data.net/en/bmw-m5-g90-4.4-v8-727hp-plug-in-hybrid-m-xdrive-m-steptronic-52105) and official US fuel/TCO tools: [Edmunds True Cost to Own](https://www.edmunds.com/bmw/m5/2026/cost-to-own/).
    - Maintenance & repairs: high‑performance big‑brakes, tyres and VIP service labour. Expect annual servicing to cost multiples of an ordinary 5 Series. Factor in potential expensive repairs (engine oil leaks, turbo work, transmission mechatronic) if the car lacks thorough service history.
    - Insurance: M cars attract premium insurance; get quotes before you buy.
  - Hybrid vs pure V8 maths (is paying more for a PHEV M5 worth it?)
    - G90 plug‑in hybrid factory data shows tiny combined fuel numbers when charged (e.g., 1.6–1.7 L/100 km and electric range quotes 60–70 km), but:
      - Those figures assume regular charging and a driving pattern that maximises electric use. If your driving is long interstate runs without frequent charging, you’ll see the petrol engine dominate and gains evaporate. See G90 spec sheet: [Auto‑Data G90 fuel economy](https://www.auto-data.net/en/bmw-m5-g90-4.4-v8-727hp-plug-in-hybrid-m-xdrive-m-steptronic-52105) and Edmunds TCO that models plug‑in behaviour: [Edmunds TCO M5 2026](https://www.edmunds.com/bmw/m5/2026/cost-to-own/).
      - Electricity consumption (~25 kWh/100 km) and local household electricity rates determine whether charging saves money vs fuel. If you do <50–60 km/day roundtrip and can charge at home regularly, the PHEV is mechanically more complex but can lower running fuel cost materially. If you do long stretches of open high‑speed work and can’t recharge, stick to a conventional V8 in second‑hand market math.
- Practical buying checklist & recommended sweet spot
  - Pre‑purchase essentials:
    - Full BMW dealer service history — must be present. Ask for I‑Level and software update evidence.
    - VIN recall check via BMW recall lookup (or NHTSA records for historical context): [BMW recall portal](https://my.bmwusa.com/safety-and-emission-recalls/manage) and aggregated recall history: [VINCheckEngine recalls list](https://vincheckengine.com/recalls/bmw/m5).
    - Specialist inspection: engine compression/smoke test, oil analysis, scan for stored codes (engine, transmission, ADAS), physical inspection for turbo heat‑related leaks, intercooler hoses, and undertray condition.
    - Transmission fluid sample and leak check — many transmission problems start with ignored leaks.
    - ADAS calibration history — ask whether radar/camera modules were recalibrated after any body work.
    - Key security: ensure keys are present and tested. If keys show signal anomalies, budget for new key programming.
  - Spending advice:
    - If you can pay ~AUD 150k and want absolute performance with modern convenience, a 2021 F90 M5 with ~45k km (your NSW listing) can be a very usable car — but you’re buying a sporting tool, not a cheap commute box. Expect higher running costs.
    - Optimal financial sweet spot: late‑model F90 cars (2019–2021) with Competition pack and full dealer service history — they offer matured software updates, stronger mid‑production hardware and still sit well below brand‑new M5 pricing. The single NSW listing at AUD 149,990 for a 2021 example with ~46k km is right in the ballpark for a low‑km Competition example — use it as a reference point to negotiate.
  - Final recommendation (yes/no, and which trim/year)
    - Yes, if:
      - You understand the premium service and repair economics and want a genuine super‑saloon driving experience.
      - You can verify a spotless service history and the car hasn’t been heavily track‑used or remapped.
      - You have a budget for insurance, tyres, brakes and occasional big‑ticket items.
    - No, if:
      - You want a low‑cost, low‑maintenance daily with cheap running costs.
      - You can’t confirm dealer history or the car shows signs of hard use (burnt oil smell, smoke, staggered rev response, clutch/gearbox slip).
    - Best pick: A 2019–2021 F90 M5 Competition with full BMW dealer history and under ~80,000 km is the pragmatic sweet spot. It retains modern tech, the Competition tune improves value retention, and you’ll dodge the steepest early‑generation teething issues while keeping a materially lower price than new G90 PHEV cars.
