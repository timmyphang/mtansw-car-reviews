#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
The 2010 Mazda 6 Classic represents the terminal phase of the second-generation GG chassis platform (GF/GG internal codes), produced between 2002 and 2012 before Mazda pivoted aggressively toward the SkyActiv philosophy. Built on the shared Mazda GD architecture—which famously underpins the Ford Mondeo Mk4, Mercury Milan, Lincoln MKS, and even carried over structural DNA to the Ford Falcon BA/BF lineage—this generation benefits from a cavernous global supply chain. Genuine suspension arms, drivetrain housings, and body panels flow freely through both Mazda Australia’s franchise outlets and independent European/Ford-compatible workshops. 

In the Australian marketplace hierarchy, the GG 6 sits comfortably above the compact Mazda3 and below the mid-size CX-7 SUV. It directly contested the Toyota Camry, Honda Accord, Nissan Altima, and Holden Commodore sedan variants during its prime. For 2010, Australian spec dropped the manual gearbox entirely except for niche imports, defaulting to a five-speed hydraulic automatic paired exclusively with Front-Wheel Drive, though limited All-Wheel Drive allocation existed for the 2.5L variant in wet-climate states. Powertrain options revolved around Mazda’s Vee family: the 2.0L **LF** V6, 2.5L **LP** V6, and a scarce 2.3L **DISI** direct-injection turbo for performance-seeking drivers. Combined fuel consumption ranges from 9.2 to 10.5 L/100km depending on cylinder deactivation software calibration and aerodynamic drag coefficients. Local rivals demanded similar real-world highway efficiency figures, but the Mazda traded outright passenger car space for sharper lateral grip and stiffer torsional rigidity (~25% more bracing than its predecessor). Current market liquidity remains thin but stable; as seen in recent VIC inventory tracking a 239,450km example priced at $13,888 excluding government charges the residual value floor has found its footing. Historical context on the vehicle’s launch year further confirms the broader economic climate shaped buyer expectations while automotive regulatory shifts influenced emissions compliance protocols across domestic fleets 

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used 2010 Mazda 6 Classic?
##### Exterior
Panel alignment on the GG platform holds together remarkably well, but corrosion ingress hides in plain sight. Factory seam sealing degrades after twelve-plus years, allowing moisture migration along rocker panels, wheel arch flares, and boot lip channels. Gloss-black exterior trim pieces—particularly roof rails, mirror caps, and side blade accents—chalk and micro-scratch within three years of coastal UV exposure, necessitating cheap OEM replacers rather than costly respray treatments. Paint durability remains competent, yet bonnet leading edges and front bumper radiators crack under stone impact without aftermarket deflectors installed early in life. ADAS functionality is virtually non-existent on base trims, but top-spec Touring and Maxx variants bolted onto Dynamic Brake Support (DBS) and lane-departure warning modules. These sensors sit behind the windscreen near the rearview mirror mount; rain, road grime, or fogged lens housings trigger false braking interventions or permanent system warnings. Firmware updates never fully resolved sensitivity thresholds—manual lens cleaning and ECU adaptation recalibration remain the only reliable fix.

##### Interior
Cabin resilience against commercial-duty abuse depends entirely on prior owner maintenance. Rear seat foam compression and buckle-tearing fabric are common telltales for rideshare or taxi conversions. Door check angles deliver that beloved British-style heft, but rubber damping bushes harden and stretch past 200,000 km, causing the driver’s door to droop and fail to latch positively at speed. Electrical gremlins typically originate from aftermarket alarm installations tapping directly into the CAN harness, or failing door lock actuators that refuse to rest in the closed position, bleeding the **12V battery** overnight. Infotainment hardware predated Mazda Connect, relying on basic graphic displays or rudimentary 5-inch touchscreens across mid-to-high trims. Smartphone integration demands third-party Bluetooth adapters that suffer multipoint handshake drops, particularly when paired with iOS devices running older OS versions. Climate control rotary dials crack from frequent twisting, and passenger-side glovebox hinges snap under moderate load—simple clip replacements resolve both.

##### Mechanically
Mazda’s Golden Era engineering delivers robust powertrains and predictable handling geometry, but ageing rubber mounts, polyurethane conversion kits, and dried-out shock absorber pistons inevitably surface. Security vulnerabilities target late-GG push-button start models susceptible to relay amplifier exploitation: thieves capture the keyless fob signal from inside your home using two smartphones, then unlock and start the car without physical key proximity. Mitigation requires storing the key in a Faraday pouch, fitting a visible steering column clamp lock, and verifying immobilizer PIN-code authentication status before purchase. Dealer service networks across Australia generally honour the standard 10,000 km/12 month interval cycle, though some metropolitan franchises stretch to 15,000 km during promotional periods—both acceptable if synthetic 5W-30 oils and OEM filtration are maintained rigorously.

Diagnostic failures to inspect for:
- Rust perforation along sill seams and inner arch liners
- Clearcoat delamination on bonnet leading edge and roof rails
- Windscreen-mounted radar/sensor lens clouding or mounting bracket fatigue
- Battery parasitic drain exceeding 50mA when asleep
- Infotainment unit freeze cycles during Bluetooth audio streaming
- Glovebox hinge plastic fatigue and climate control dial cracking

##### Engines
*LF20 / 2.0L V6:* Prone to premature coolant bypass valve thermostat housing fractures, directing pressurised overflow into the intake plenum and manifesting as white exhaust smoke or milky dipstick residue. Crankcase ventilation filters clog faster than standard 10,000 km Australian service windows, forcingPCV diaphragm stress and blue smoke at idle. Upper piston ring tensioners lose elasticity past 150,000 km, increasing oil consumption by 0.5L per 1,000 km. Preventative maintenance mandates checking expansion tank filler cap vacuum ratings and replacing ventilation hoses every third service cycle.

*LP25 / 2.5L V6:* Generally the most resilient configuration, yet vulnerable to PCV diaphragm sludge accumulation from short-trip urban commuting. High-pressure fuel pump (HPFP) timing chain tensioners wear prematurely, producing metallic ticking from the timing cover under cold starts. Coolant circulation improves dramatically after clearing the front splitter-mounted air intake vent filter, which traps leaf litter and highway grit annually. Scheduled servicing should replace spark plugs at 40,000 km and monitor EGR cooler carbon deposition via borescope inspection.

*DISI23 / 2.3L Turbo:* Direct injection methodology deposits heavy carbon fouling on intake valve faces—a non-negotiable maintenance requirement demanding walnut media blasting every 80,000 km. Intercooler piping suffers thermal cycling fatigue, resulting in boost leak whistles under hard acceleration. Variable vane turbo actuator cables seize in humid climates, triggering limp-mode fault codes. Anti-theft measures here are less relevant, but catalytic converter heat shielding corrodes rapidly; replace promptly to avoid cabin fume ingress.

*LH37 / 3.7L V6:* Rare in 2010 allocations but present in late-production runs. Exhibits severe oil consumption due to worn oil control rings and cam phaser rattle during low-RPM tick-over. Cylinder head bolt torque sequence failures manifest as compression leakage across adjacent ports. Recommend avoiding unless recent bottom-end rebuild documentation exists.

All listed powerplants operate on strict 10,000 km intervals aligned with Australian road dust loads and extended-idle traffic conditions. Extending to 15,000 km voids warranty implications on remanufactured units and accelerates valvetrain wear.

##### Transmission
The GG platform utilizes a conventional hydraulic torque converter automatic, calibrated for smooth city driving rather than track abuse. Known mechanical behaviours centre around low-speed shudder between 40–60 km/h, indicating premature torque converter lockup clutch friction material breakdown. Separator plate steel wear generates flaring during wide-open throttle upshifts, particularly if ATF degradation crosses oxidation thresholds. Fluid specification requires Ford WSS-M2C922-A equivalents; generic “full synthetic” swaps alter friction modifier chemistry and exacerbate band glazing. Maintenance protocol demands transmission pan removal, OEM filter replacement, and partial fluid exchange every 60,000 km—ignoring this schedule reliably destroys planetary gearsets and valve body solenoids. Hard shifting from Park to Drive or Reverse correlates directly to degraded throttle position sensor calibration, resolved through dealer scanner relearn procedures.

#### 3. Should you buy a used 2010 Mazda 6 Classic?
NSW dealer market data presents a constrained inventory landscape: currently one active listing captured at AUD $10,990, median odometer reading 113,932 km, transmission mix locked exclusively to 5-Speed Sports Automatic configurations. Pricing ceilings rarely breach $14,500 unless examples boast under 100,000 km, complete logbooks, and dealer-backed warranties. Depreciation curves have flattened significantly; initial value erosion hovered around 88% from original $38k–$42k retail point, outpacing core segment rivals like the Camry by roughly 5–7 percentage points due to diminished brand footprint, yet catching up rapidly on long-term repair expenditure. Total Cost of Ownership analysis reveals predictable mathematics: average highway cruising consumes 8.8 L/100km, suburban stop-start climbs to 11.2 L/100km, yielding a blended 9.8 L/100km baseline. Over a five-year retention period at 15,000 km annual distance, fuel expenses approximate AUD $8,200 at prevailing E10 premium unleaded averages. Paying substantial market premiums for alternative powertrains such as hybrids or EVs produces zero mathematical upside for this vehicle class—upfront capital outlays exceed $20k, battery degradation timelines align with decade-mark replacements costing $6k–$9k, and real-world savings vanish until insurance premiums normalise. Traditional internal combustion retains definitive fiscal superiority here.

Buying recommendation focuses squarely on the 2008–2010 GG facelift window. Opt for the 2.5L V6 variant paired with either FWD or AWD depending on state rainfall patterns, favouring Touring or Maxx trim levels for upgraded interior switchgear quality and revised suspension bushings targeting NVH reduction. The financial sweet spot lands between $9,500 and $12,000 for vehicles presenting consistent 10,000 km service histories, intact factory catalytic converters, and fresh timing cover gaskets. Commission an independent pre-purchase inspection scrutinising crankshaft snout seal integrity, differential carrier bushing wear, radiator end tank brazing lines, and ABS wheel speed sensor reluctor ring condition. Secure documentation proving valve cover bolt torque compliance and alternator belt tensioner bearing replacement. If the paperwork aligns and the odometer reflects honest usage, walk away from every other option in the sub-$15k mid-size sedan tier.
