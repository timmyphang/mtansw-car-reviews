#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation / timeframe / codes: This review covers the modern, standalone Subaru WRX generation (2015–present as a separate model from the Impreza) through the latest facelift and engine updates that carry into the 2024–2026 model years. The recent rework introduced the larger 2.4‑litre turbo boxer in late‑generation WRX models (FA24 family) while earlier post‑2015 cars use FA20 and, before that, EJ‑series engines in older WRX/STI cars. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_WRX) and Subaru’s official pages list the timeline and evolution.
- Product hierarchy: The WRX sits below Subaru’s full STI/flagship performance halo (when STI exists as a separate model) and above the standard Impreza/Liberty in Subaru’s passenger-car lineup. It’s the brand’s entry point to performance AWD sedans — sportier and more driver-focused than the mainstream Impreza but not as expensive or rare as dedicated STI halo cars. See Subaru’s model pages for positioning and trims [Subaru WRX 2025 specs](https://www.subaru.com/vehicles/wrx/2025/specs-trim.html).
- Shared platform / parts availability: The WRX shares Subaru’s global front‑engine, boxer‑engine platform and Symmetrical All‑Wheel Drive hardware with other Subaru passenger cars (Impreza, Crosstrek/XV, Forester families, and BRZ derivatives in parts sharing for later engine families). The commonality helps with parts availability across dealer networks and independent Subaru specialists.
- Market-specific engine & drivetrain list (Australian buyer shorthand)
  - 2.4‑litre turbo boxer — **FA24** family: ~271 hp / 350 Nm (U.S./global spec reference for 2025; check local tune and figures). 6‑speed manual standard; Subaru Performance Transmission (automatic) option; AWD standard. [Subaru 2025 WRX specs](https://www.subaru.com/vehicles/wrx/2025/specs-trim.html)
  - 2.0‑litre turbo boxer — **FA20** (used in earlier generations/variants globally) — manual and automatic avail on some markets (higher revving, lighter internals).
  - Older WRX/STI performance cars: **EJ25** engines (legacy turbo flat‑4) — heavy hitters, but with long‑known service caveats.
  - Transmission choices in the used market you’ll actually see in NSW: 6‑speed manual, Subaru Performance Transmission (6‑speed auto for later WRX), and a small number of cars listed with Continuous Variable (CVT) in the region (NSW dealer snapshot: Automatic 6, CVT 1, Manual 4 out of 13 active listings).
  - Drive configuration: Symmetrical All‑Wheel Drive (AWD) is the WRX hallmark.
- Main rivals in the Australian market: Hyundai i30 N / Elantra N, Honda Civic Si / Type R, Volkswagen Golf R (the AWD, direct competitor), Toyota GR Corolla / GR Yaris (if you’re cross‑shopping for peak fun), and Mazda3 Turbo in some crossover comparisons. [The Car Connection rival list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/subaru_wrx_choices) and cross‑shop guides back this up.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Subaru WRX?
This is the no‑nonsense, spanner‑on‑the‑bench section. If you’re buying a WRX, check these items hard.

Exterior:
- Panel fit and trim
  - Panel alignment is generally OK, but mid‑generation WRX owners report small fitment issues and worn trim clips after rough life — look for mismatched gaps suggesting accident repair. Go‑Parts details common trim failures (peeling rocker panel clips, faded plastics) especially on 2015–2021 cars [Go‑Parts trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-subaru-wrx-subaru-wrx-sti-2018-2021).
  - Gloss black appliqués (B‑pillar trims) on 2015–2021 cars are prone to scratching, haze and peeling; cosmetic but easy to spot and expensive to replace if you want OEM. [Go‑Parts B‑pillar note](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/body-b-pillar-trim-panel-subaru-wrx-subaru-wrx-sti-2015-2021).
- Paint durability and corrosion
  - No fleet‑wide Australian recall for paint that I can cite here; look for local stone chips on the bonnet and lower bumper — WRX owners drive fast, and rock chips are common.
- Safety/ADAS issues
  - Subaru’s driver‑assist stack (EyeSight / radar‑based systems) has been subject to high‑profile complaints and legal action in recent years for phantom braking and unwanted interventions. There was a substantial settlement activity around EyeSight faults and ongoing media reporting into 2026 — check VIN and recall/TSB status before you buy. [CarComplaints summary of the EyeSight lawsuit](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2026/subaru-eyesight-lawsuit.shtml) and [Autoblog report](https://www.autoblog.com/news/subaru-eyesight-lawsuit-says-cars-can-slam-on-the-brakes-for-no-reason). Subaru’s own recall portal is where you confirm VIN‑level remedies: [Subaru Recalls](https://www.subaru.com/recalls.html).
  - Practical check: ensure adaptive cruise, lane‑keep and blind‑spot sensors function correctly; look for dash warnings, spurious lane departure alerts, and recent ACC/ADAS TSB repairs. [AboutAutomobile TSB aggregation](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Technical-Service-Bulletin/2024/Subaru/WRX/Adaptive-Cruise-Control).
- Recalls to be aware of: some WRX model years (and related Subaru platforms) have had driveshaft and other campaign entries — always VIN‑check via Subaru’s recall portal or [RecallExplained](https://recallexplained.com/subaru/wrx/) and [VINCheckEngine](https://vincheckengine.com/recalls/subaru/wrx).

Interior:
- Durability under heavy use
  - If you plan rideshare or heavy daily commute: some WRX interiors use softer trims and bolsters that show wear fast under stinted use. Seats, suede/cloth bolsters and shifter boots are common wear points.
  - Door sills and entry: WRX is a sporty sedan — sills are lower than a 4x4 but some tall drivers will find ingress tighter than an SUV. Check door check angles and hinge wear (doors should close solidly, not flappy).
- Electrical and infotainment
  - Infotainment sizes and hardware vary by trim and year. Recent 2025 models climb to larger screens (e.g., 11.6‑inch on Premium according to Edmunds trim notes), but older cars have smaller units and different hardware revisions; Apple CarPlay/Android Auto reliability is mixed across upgrades. [Edmunds WRX Premium features](https://www.edmunds.com/subaru/wrx/2025/st-402032009/features-specs/).
  - 12‑volt battery drain: like many modern Subarus, aftermarket telematics or Starlink integrations can complicate sleep behaviour — be suspicious of non‑OEM remote starters or alarm kit installs. Check for slow parasitic drains if vehicle sits overnight and watch how long it takes to enter sleep after locking.
  - Connectivity: later model Subaru STARLINK telematics changes have been the subject of security scrutiny (see the STARLINK admin panel research below) and some TSBs note telematics/immobiliser interactions. [AboutAutomobile electrical TSB list](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Technical-Service-Bulletin/2024/Subaru/WRX/Electrical-System).

Mechanically:
- Brand engineering reputation: Subaru’s flat‑4 boxer and AWD systems have a solid enthusiast reputation — reliable if treated well — but history shows engine family‑specific Achilles’ heels driven by both age and enthusiast use (mods/tuning).
- Security and theft vulnerabilities
  - Modern thieves aren’t just jamming keys; CAN‑bus injection via light clusters (headlight wiring) has been proven as a practical attack that can let criminals spoof key presence. This is an industry‑wide weakness that affected many makes and models and is relevant to WRX buyers. [CAN Injection analysis — can-cia.org](https://www.can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf) and coverage at Autoblog explain the method. [Autoblog headlight hack](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update)
  - Subaru STARLINK admin panel access research (2024) demonstrated that cloud telematics exposures can allow tracking and remote commands for enrolled vehicles — check whether any telematics subscription is active and deactivate if you don’t want the additional attack surface. [Sam Curry writeup on STARLINK hack](https://samcurry.net/hacking-subaru).
  - Practical aftermarket mitigations: mechanical steering‑wheel locks, wheel nuts with soldner/locking studs, hood‑lock for the headlight harness area, and dealer/third‑party intrusion detection modules (and disable or reconfigure remote telematics if possible). Consider an RFID immobiliser or an OBD‑port guard / smart alarm with CAN intrusion detection.
- Wiring and electrics
  - TSBs exist for electrical behaviours and telematics; be wary of water ingress in headlight clusters (which can affect radar) and any aftermarket lighting that’s been hacked into the harness.

Engines:
- The following *italicized* subsections cover each engine code you’ll likely encounter in the Australian used WRX market and the common failure points from owner reports and specialist write‑ups.

  * **EJ25**
  - Known failure points: head gasket failures (lifeblood of EJ19/EJ25 lore), ringland failures and short‑block trouble in tuned cars, oil leaks around rocker cover and oil cooler gaskets. Heavy doorslamming and sustained high‑boost tuning can accelerate internal failures. See the EJ vs FA reliability comparisons and forums summarised at [Crawford Performance EJ vs FA](https://crawfordperformance.com/blogs/news/fa20-vs-ej25-reliability) and extended problem lists [ExtendedCarWarranty overview](https://extendedcarwarranty.net/subaru-engine-problems/).
  - Preventative maintenance: conservative tune or stock boost levels; oil changes at least every 10,000 km if driven gently, shorten to 5,000–7,500 km if used hard/track or modified. Compression checks on cars with unknown history; leak‑down testing recommended before purchase.

  * **FA20**
  - Known failure points: direct injection carbon build‑up on intake, rod bearing and connecting‑rod issues under sustained high load if poorly tuned, turbocharger wear on higher‑mileage, and water pump/thermostat failures if cooling maintenance was neglected. Owner guides and comparisons detail FA20 quirks [FA20 vs EJ reliability](https://crawfordperformance.com/blogs/news/fa20-vs-ej25-reliability).
  - Preventative maintenance: use high‑quality synthetic oil, drain and fill every 7,500–10,000 km for spirited drivers; catch can or regular intake cleanings for DI petrol engines; inspect turbo oil feed lines and charge‑pipe clamps.

  * **FA24**
  - Known failure points (early reports): the FA24 introduced in 2022+ WRX is a larger boxer with different tuning; owners and specialist write‑ups have flagged early FA24 reports of turbocharger-related faults, oil consumption signs if maintenance or tuning was ignored, and some short‑block observations in heavily stressed aftermarket builds. See the FA24 guide for early failure signs and best practices [SubiMods FA24 guide](https://subimods.com/blogs/subimodsblog/ultimate-fa24-reliability-guide-subaru-owners).
  - Preventative maintenance: treat the FA24 like a modern turbo — oil and filter changes at least every 10,000 km, consider 7,500 km if you’re hard on the car; keep tune conservative, monitor oil consumption and address any leaks early. Subaru’s factory guidance on service intervals should be followed; see Subaru official specs for the 2.4L turbo [Subaru 2025 WRX Specs](https://www.subaru.com/vehicles/wrx/2025/specs-trim.html).

Transmission:
- Manual 6‑speed: The 6‑speed manuals in WRX cars are durable but expect clutch and diff wear if previous owners were enthusiastic. Inspect for clutch slip, chatter, and smooth gear engagement. Differential and centre transfer case service history is important in AWD cars — check for oil change stamps and mechanical noises on a cold start and during tight turns.
- Subaru Performance Transmission / automatic (SPT): The SPT auto in newer WRX is a torque‑converter automatic tuned for quick shifts; watch for shudder or harsh clutch pack behaviour if previous owner used aggressive launch techniques. Subaru describes SPT as engineered for sport shifting; independent reports highlight that proper fluid maintenance and software updates are essential. [Subaru brochure mentions SPT](https://www.subaru.com/content/dam/subaru/downloads/pdf/brochures/2025/2025_WRX_Brochure_060225.pdf).
- CVT / Lineartronic: While rare on WRX, the NSW snapshot shows CVT appearing in a listing — older Subaru models with the Lineartronic CVT have a documented history of chain wear, valve body issues and torque‑converter shudder in the 120,000–200,000 km window if neglected. These boxes can be expensive to repair/replace; check service records for CVT fluid changes and be extra cautious. [RicksFreeAutoRepair on Subaru CVT](https://ricksfreeautorepairadvice.com/subaru-cvt-transmission-reliability-and-common-issues/) and broader owner complaint aggregates illustrate the failure modes.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Subaru WRX?
- NSW dealer snapshot (live): Active listings 13 — Price range AUD $30,990 – $67,440; Median price AUD $41,995; Year range 2014 – 2025; Median odometer 40,920 km; Transmission mix: Automatic 6, CVT 1, Manual 4. Use that as your local market reality check — the cheap $30k end will be older high‑mileage or modified cars, and the top end heads into near‑new, low‑km territory or limited trims. (NSW MTA data — live snapshot provided by user.)
- Depreciation & pricing reality
  - Typical depreciation: industry trackers estimate the WRX loses roughly 30–33% of value over five years — iSeeCars notes a 29.8% drop at five years, while CarEdge gives a similar picture. WRX depreciation hovers close to compact segment averages, but tuned/modified cars can crater faster in value. [iSeeCars depreciation](https://www.iseecars.com/car/subaru-wrx/resale-value) and [CarEdge estimates](https://caredge.com/subaru/wrx/depreciation).
  - NSW market practical numbers: with a median listing around AUD $41,995 and median km ~41k, a properly maintained 2018–2020 WRX will often trade in the low‑to‑mid $30k range depending on condition and manual vs auto.
- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy — the steps you need to calculate real ownership cost:
  - Step 1: Depreciation — use purchase price minus expected sale price after your ownership term (WRX approx. ~9–10% p.a. depreciation per Ridekick; see [Ridekick estimate](https://www.ridekick.com/depreciation/subaru/wrx)).
  - Step 2: Fuel — realistic combined consumption for turbo WRX cars is not frugal. EPA/industry combined estimates for recent WRX are ~22 mpg (U.S.) which converts to roughly 10.7 L/100 km (235.214/22 ≈ 10.69 L/100km). Use owner logged data too (Fuelly real-world submissions) — WRX owner reports vary with driving technique but expect 9–12 L/100 km in mixed use. [CarWeek spec note ~22 MPG](https://www.carweek.com/research/subaru/wrx/2025/specs) and [Fuelly owner MPG aggregation](https://www.fuelly.com/car/subaru/wrx/all).
  - Step 3: Servicing & maintenance — Subaru recommended servicing and warranty schedules vary by market, but for spirited/older WRX we recommend servicing every 10,000 km (or tighter if used hard) rather than stretching to 15,000 km — engine oil and transmission fluids should be changed more often under performance use.
  - Step 4: Repairs/known big ticket items — set aside for potential turbo, head gasket (older EJ cars), clutch or CVT rebuilds. Factor in an annual allowance for tyres and brake wear (WRX tyres are typically performance rubber that costs more).
  - Step 5: Insurance and registration — performance cars attract higher premiums; be conservative in your quote.
  - Use calculators from Edmunds / KBB / CarEdge to create a 3–5 year TCO model and replace the U.S. fuel cost with your local fuel price and km driven. See Edmunds True Cost to Own for framing, even if US‑centric: [Edmunds TCO for 2025 WRX](https://www.edmunds.com/subaru/wrx/2025/cost-to-own/).
- Is it worth paying a premium for a manual, automatic, or a later FA24 petrol?
  - Manual vs auto: Purists will pay a premium for a clean manual WRX because manuals hold appeal and are less likely to have complex transmission failures. Automatics/SPT are convenient and perform well but can mask previous abuse (launch control, etc.). CVTs should be treated carefully — avoid if you can’t prove CVT service history.
  - FA24 vs FA20/EJ25: The FA24 brings torque and modern features but is a newer engine family — buying later FA24 cars (2022+) means paying more but getting newer safety and infotainment. FA20 is lighter and proven; EJ25 is older but potentially trouble‑prone if you don’t have a full service/tuning history. The FA24’s higher torque may also stress driveline components more, so inspect diffs and drivetrain mounts on those cars.
  - Hybrid/Ev: At time of writing, mainstream WRX remains petrol; hybrid WRX variants might arrive in future (reports of a WRX sportwagon hybrid plan), but for current used buyers there’s no electrified WRX mainstream option to compare. Paying a premium for a hypothetical hybrid WRX today isn’t applicable for the used market. [EV Central piece on potential hybrid WRX sportwagon](https://evcentral.com.au/subaru-wrx-sportwagon-to-add-strong-hybrid-latest-fuel-saving-system-set-to-expand-from-forester-into-performance-passenger-car/).
- Practical buying recommendation (short and blunt)
  - If you want the best balance of reliability, parts availability and value: target a late‑generation FA20 WRX (2018–2021) with a clean service history and manual transmission, or a 2022–2024 FA24 car if you want newer tech and are prepared to pay the premium and verify warranty/recall history. The NSW market median (AUD $41,995, ~41k km) suggests the sweet spot for value is around 2018–2021 cars that haven’t been modified.
  - Avoid: heavily modified/tuned cars without receipts or dyno logs; CVT‑equipped WRX unless you can prove recent CVT fluid service and no shuddering; older EJ engines with unknown rebuild records.
  - Ask for:
    - Full service history and receipts (oil changes every 10k or sooner for performance use).
    - VIN recall check via Subaru recall portal [Subaru Recalls](https://www.subaru.com/recalls.html).
    - Evidence of ADAS/ADAS software or radar sensor repair or calibration if warning lights or ACC issues have occurred. Look up TSBs for adaptive cruise ADAS issues [AboutAutomobile TSBs](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Technical-Service-Bulletin/2024/Subaru/WRX/Adaptive-Cruise-Control).
    - Compression/leak‑down test on EJ/FA20 cars if mileage is high or there are oil consumption signs.
    - Independent inspection by a Subaru specialist — it’s worth $500–1,000 to avoid a $10k mistake.

Final note — straight talk: if you want a WRX because it’s honest about being loud, quick and fun to drive, buy one and enjoy it. If you want low cost, low hassle commuting transport, buy something less temperamental and refillable. As always with WRX: service records, clutch/transmission checks and an independent Subaru specialist inspection will save you grief.
