#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: Third‑generation Toyota Tundra (2022–present refresh cycle visible in 2023–2026 brochures). Internal platform reference: built on Toyota’s large‑vehicle TNGA-F / GA‑F architecture (recall and service campaigns reference the GA‑F platform). [Toyota Tundra official pages](https://www.toyota.com/tundra/) and Toyota spec brochures for 2025–2026 outline the current drivetrain and trim structure.  
  Sources: [Toyota Tundra](https://www.toyota.com/tundra/), [2025 eBrochure PDF](https://www.toyota.com/content/dam/toyota/brochures/pdf/2025/tundra_ebrochure.pdf)

- Product hierarchy: In Toyota’s line-up the Tundra sits at the top of Toyota’s pick‑up range as the full‑size/large pickup offering — above midsize Hilux and aimed to compete with the US/large‑truck class. Trims range from SR (work‑truck base) up to Capstone/TRD Pro for luxury/off‑road. [Edmunds trim breakdown] lists SR → SR5 → Limited → Platinum → 1794 → TRD Pro → Capstone.  
  Source: [Edmunds – 2025 Tundra trims](https://www.edmunds.com/toyota/tundra/2025/trims/)

- Shared global platform and parts availability: The truck is built on Toyota’s GA‑F (TNGA‑F) large‑vehicle platform — the same heavy‑vehicle architecture Toyota uses for its modern full‑size SUVs and trucks (the platform is referenced in multiple service/recall campaigns). That means many structural, suspension and electrical components are shared across Toyota’s larger vehicles, improving parts availability versus an isolated niche model. (See recall/service notes referencing the GA‑F platform.)  
  Source: recall/service writeups referencing the GA‑F platform in transmission campaigns.

- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drivetrains (as offered globally and reflected in 2025–2026 specs):
  - i‑FORCE: **3.4‑litre twin‑turbo V6** — advertised ~389 hp / 479 lb‑ft (≈ 290 kW / 650 Nm); pairs with Aisin 10‑speed torque‑converter automatic; available in 2WD and 4WD. [Toyota/specs]  
    Source: [2025 Toyota Tundra specs](https://www.toyota.com/tundra/2025/features/mechanical_performance/)
  - i‑FORCE MAX (electrified): **3.4‑litre twin‑turbo V6 with hybrid motor** — advertised ~437 hp / 583 lb‑ft (≈ 326 kW / 790 Nm); pairs with a hybrid power‑split transaxle (Toyota P710 e‑CVT architecture) and electric motor between engine & transmission; available in 4WD and on higher trims. [Toyota eBrochure & EPA]  
    Sources: [Toyota 2026 eBrochure](https://www.toyota.com/content/dam/toyota/brochures/pdf/2026/tundra_ebrochure.pdf), [FuelEconomy.gov (hybrid)](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/Find.do?action=sbs&id=49424)
  - Transmission options listed in US & global specs: Aisin 10‑speed automatic for petrol V6, Toyota P710 e‑CVT (hybrid power‑split) for i‑FORCE MAX hybrid.  
    Sources: [Toyota 2025 specs PDF](https://www.toyota.com/content/dam/toyota/brochures/pdf/2025/tundra_ebrochure.pdf), [P710 writeup](https://transmissionaudit.com/units/toyota-transmissions/p710/)

- Primary Australian market rivals: Ford F‑150, Chevrolet / GMC Silverado family, Ram 1500 — the usual full‑size truck set (buyers will also cross‑shop high‑trim SUVs and luxury wagons for comfort).  
  Source: [Toyota Tundra official pages & Car and Driver comparisons](https://www.toyota.com/tundra/), https://www.caranddriver.com/toyota/tundra

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Toyota Tundra?
This is the bit buyers want blunt about. Short answer: there are two problem clusters you need to know — ADAS/electrical oddities and a troubling run of gearbox/engine manufacturing issues on early post‑2021 trucks. Below is the forensic breakdown.

Exterior:
- Panel fit and paint: generally solid build quality for large‑truck panels; typical trim issues are cosmetic — gloss black plastics scratch easily and chromed bits show road‑stone pitting if used hard off‑road. No Australian‑specific body recall cited in the snippets, but check the vehicle’s VIN against Toyota Australia for localized campaigns.
- ADAS sensor vulnerabilities and calibration quirks:
  - The Pre‑Collision System (PCS) and Forward Recognition Camera are a recurring source of warnings and disabled AEB/DRCC functionality. Water intrusion or camera/windshield issues can trigger a persistent "Pre‑Collision System Malfunction" which disables emergency braking and adaptive cruise. Replacement requires professional calibration — expect several hundred dollars. [go‑parts guide]  
    Source: [PCS sensor malfunctions & calibration](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/collision-avoidance-sensor-toyota-tundra-2022-2025)
  - Owner reports and NHTSA complaint aggregates show cruise control / ADAS faults continuing into 2024–2025 model years; dealer fixes sometimes temporary. [CarComplaints / Carsafetydb]  
    Sources: [CarComplaints — Electrical Problems 2024](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Toyota/Tundra/2024/electrical/), [Carsafetydb — Driver Assist 2025 complaints](https://carsafetydb.com/toyota/tundra/2025/driver-assist)

Interior:
- Cabin durability: The Tundra’s interior materials are generally tough, but heavy rideshare/tow‑truck duty exposes seat bolster wear, scuffing on lower door trims and carpet contamination quickly. Leather and soft‑touch on premium trims show wear early if the truck does hard work. For shared use in Australia (work sites, trailers), insist on inspecting high‑wear areas.
- Entry/exit and ergonomics: Wide door sills and high ride height — excellent entry for a full‑size truck but heavy for older buyers. Door‑check angles are generous; step assistance may be fitted on higher trims.
- Electrical gremlins in day‑to‑day use:
  - Several threads report persistent 12V battery drain or strange electrical warning clusters (particularly following ADAS sensor faults and after dealer resets). If a truck exhibits intermittent start/no‑start or multiple dashboard warnings that vanish after restart, have the systems scanned and the ADAS codes investigated. [CarComplaints]  
    Source: [CarComplaints — Electrical Problems 2024](https://www.carcomplaints.com/Toyota/Tundra/2024/electrical/)
  - Infotainment: base screens are 8‑inch in lower trims; larger screens on Limited/Platinum and above. Software updates are frequent — confirm the head unit runs the latest firmware. Older units can be flaky with smartphone mirroring; higher trims mitigate this with better hardware and speakers. Manufacturer pages outline screen sizes by trim. [Edmunds trim breakdown]  
    Source: [Edmunds – trims](https://www.edmunds.com/toyota/tundra/2025/trims/)

Mechanically:
- Brand engineering reputation: Toyota’s heavy‑vehicle engineering is usually conservative and robust — but this generation’s switch from a traditional V8 to a complex twin‑turbo V6 architecture introduced new failure modes and resulted in high‑profile recalls and complaints.
- Security vulnerabilities (real and modern):
  - CAN‑bus injection theft: Professional thieves have exploited CAN‑bus weaknesses on 2024–2026 Tundras to bypass immobilisers and even defeat factory tracking devices. This is not petty theft — it’s targeted. The factory tracker has been shown to fail when the bus is spoofed. Consider layered anti‑theft: mechanical deterrents (steering or pedal locks), aftermarket immobilisers with vehicle‑specific protections, and secure storage. [TorqueNews & CAN bus security summaries]  
    Sources: [TorqueNews — CAN bus injection theft](https://www.torquenews.com/1084/how-thieves-use-can-bus-injection-disable-toyota-tundra-security-1600-tracking-failure), [CAN bus writeups]
- Practical digital mitigations: Disable or remove any always‑on aftermarket OBD trackers you don’t trust, strong immobiliser systems that power‑cut the starter relay, and parking in secure, monitored areas. The community recommends physical locks as an inexpensive and effective first line. [CAN security mitigations]  

Engines:
- The most important single thing to check: early 2022–2024 twin‑turbo V6 engines (Toyota internal family referenced as the V35A twin‑turbo in many write‑ups) have been the subject of recalls and investigations for catastrophic bearing failure tied to machining debris and front (No.1) main bearing loading. That can cause knocking, loss of power or complete engine failure — not a small fix. Check the VIN against Toyota safety recalls and insist on paperwork for any remedial engine work. [PickupTruckTalk, LembergLaw coverage]  
  Sources: [PickupTruckTalk analysis](https://pickuptrucktalk.com/2026/05/2021-2026-toyota-tundra-reliability-engine-troubles-recalls-and-the-growing-pains-of-a-new-generation/), [LembergLaw — recall 25V767 summary](https://lemberglaw.com/toyota-tundra-engine-problems/)

- Engines discussed (italicised subsections per instruction):
  - *Bold **V35A** (3.4L twin‑turbo V6, non‑hybrid i‑FORCE)*  
    - Failure points: machining debris at assembly can lodge on the #1 main bearing causing uneven wear / seizure; turbocharger wastegate and manifold leaks have been reported; secondary air injection pump failures and exhaust leaks noted in owner repositories. High towing loads may accelerate failure.  
    - Preventative maintenance: strict oil‑service adherence — change oil at least every 10,000 km (prefer shorter 7,500–10,000 km intervals if towing/frequent heavy loads). Use manufacturer‑specified grade and insist on dealer logbook stamps. If buying used, verify service history and ask for any engine inspection reports. [Engine failure analysis sources]  
    Sources: [PickupTruckTalk engine troubles](https://pickuptrucktalk.com/2026/05/2021-2026-toyota-tundra-reliability-engine-troubles-recalls-and-the-growing-pains-of-a-new-generation/), [TorqueNews recall analysis](https://www.torquenews.com/1084/toyota-tundra-recall-expansion-why-v35a-twin-turbo-v6-front-bearing-failures-are-forcing)
  - *Bold **i‑FORCE MAX hybrid powertrain (3.4L V6 + electric motor, hybrid P710 e‑CVT)*  
    - Failure points: fewer direct engine bearing‑failure reports in the public datasets but hybrid transaxle and torque‑delivery shudder reports exist — mid‑range shudder/hesitation has been raised on forums; hybrid high‑voltage system checks are crucial. The hybrid uses the P710 power‑split e‑CVT architecture which has distinct failure modes from torque‑converter boxes — consult specialist hybrid techs for battery and inverter health checks.  
    - Preventative maintenance: hybrid systems demand scheduled inspections — coolant for inverter/EV parts, HV battery diagnostics, and standard oil changes. Specialist hybrid service intervals sometimes differ; consult Toyota’s service manual and insist on a hybrid scan at pre‑purchase. [P710 writeup; forum shudder threads]  
    Sources: [P710 hybrid transaxle](https://transmissionaudit.com/units/toyota-transmissions/p710/), forum reports on hybrid shudder

Transmission:
- Aisin 10‑speed torque‑converter automatic (non‑hybrid):
  - Known behaviours: generally smooth when healthy; owners and class‑action reports list hesitation, delayed shifts, harsh engagement and a "neutral‑creep" fault on some 2022–2024 units. Toyota launched a software/service campaign (recall/service campaign 24TA02 / NHTSA 24V‑125) to address the neutral‑creep issue that can cause the transmission not to disengage properly. If you find a truck with software updates logged (or paperwork showing the recall remedy completed), that’s a positive. [CherishYourCar, TransmissionAudit]  
    Sources: [CherishYourCar transmission recall explainer](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/toyota-tundra-transmission-recall/), [TransmissionAudit – Gen 3 Tundra transmission problems](https://transmissionaudit.com/cars/toyota/tundra-2022/)
  - Failure points: torque converter shudder, solenoid packs and hydraulic control problems have been reported; ignoring slipping can lead to expensive gearbox repairs. If the truck has delayed or harsh shifts, get a full fluid/filter history and ideally a pressure test.
- P710 hybrid e‑CVT (i‑FORCE MAX):
  - Behaviour: not a belt CVT — it’s a power‑split device that feels different to regular automatics. Forum reports note occasional shudder on mid‑range acceleration; Toyota has collected fluid samples and investigated. Hybrid transaxles are specialised — HV components (inverter, battery) must be checked. [TransmissionAudit, forum threads]  
    Sources: [P710 writeup](https://transmissionaudit.com/units/toyota-transmissions/p710/), forum shudder posts

Checklists for used buyers (quick):
- Verify all recall campaigns and service campaigns completed (engine recall 25V767 coverage, transmission recall 24TA02). [LembergLaw, CherishYourCar]  
- Confirm ADAS sensors/camera have clean windscreen and calibration receipts if airbags/PCS or AEB warnings have been present. [go‑parts PCS guide]  
- Insist on a full transmission fluid history and a scan for stored DTCs before purchase. [TransmissionAudit / CarComplaints]

#### 3. Should you buy a used Toyota Tundra?
- NSW dealer live snapshot (buyer data you supplied): Active listings: 5 — Price range AUD $114,990 – $142,888; median price AUD $129,990; Year range: 2023 only (median odometer 20,507 km). All listed trucks are automatic. Use this as the active market reality in NSW right now. (Snapshot supplied by MTA NSW scraper.)

- Entry and ceiling prices: in NSW today expect to pay roughly AUD $115k at the low end for a well‑equipped 2023 SR5/limited spec up to AUD $143k for near‑new high spec (Platinum/Capstone/TRD Pro). Median ~AUD $130k. These prices reflect low availability and Toyota’s brand premium for full‑size trucks in Australia. (NSW data above.)

- Depreciation metrics:
  - Historically the Tundra holds value well — iSeeCars and other analysts rank Toyota trucks among the best in five‑year retention (Tundra near top for full‑size trucks with ~78.6% 5‑yr retention in iSeeCars’ analysis). But that traditional strength is complicated now: early 2022–2024 reliability and recall exposure could blunt resale premiums if the truck is affected. So real depreciation for impacted vehicles can be faster than Toyota’s historical average.  
    Source: [iSeeCars resale study](https://www.iseecars.com/resale-value/trucks)
  - For a concrete frame: CarEdge estimates ~26% depreciation after 5 years for the Tundra in some configurations — still competitive versus Ford and GM rivals depending on trim and market. [CarEdge]  
    Source: [CarEdge depreciation estimates](https://caredge.com/toyota/tundra/depreciation)

- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy:
  - Fuel consumption baseline: EPA figures (US) show the Tundra hybrid 4WD at roughly 20 mpg (≈ 11.8 L/100 km) combined; hybrid variants are marginally better (2–3 mpg advantage) depending on drivetrain and conditions. Use fuel economy as a comparative metric only — Australian real‑world L/100 km may be higher depending on loads, fuel grade and towing. [FuelEconomy.gov]  
    Source: [FuelEconomy.gov – 2026 Tundra hybrid](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/Find.do?action=sbs&id=49424)
  - TCO components to model:
    - Depreciation: current NSW prices show the market will not be a cheap entry. Depreciation risk increases if the truck is subject to an open or unfixed engine/transmission recall. Confirm recall fixes — an unaffected or fixed example is invaluable.
    - Fuel: translate EPA mpg to L/100 km for your annual kms. For example — drive 20,000 km/year: at 12 L/100 km that’s 2,400 L/year. Multiply by your local fuel price for fuel cost. Use the hybrid’s fuel advantage if you do mostly urban/suburban driving and low average speeds; the hybrid recoups more in stop‑start city work.
    - Maintenance & repairs: include potential ADAS recalibration (several hundred dollars), potential transmission or engine repairs (major claims into the thousands if not covered), and hybrid inverter/battery checks if the variant is i‑FORCE MAX.
    - Insurance & registration: full‑size trucks attract higher premiums and luxury‑truck registration brackets in some states — factor this into annual TCO.
  - Is the hybrid worth paying a premium for?
    - Mathematically: the hybrid lowers fuel consumption modestly (EPA shows ~10–15% improvement in combined MPG vs petrol). If your annual km is high and you tow lightly or mostly drive city KMs, fuel savings can accumulate. But remember the hybrid variants command a premium at purchase — at current NSW listings the median price is already high. Use a 3–5 year ownership horizon: calculate purchase price premium / annual fuel savings to see payback. For many owner‑driver buyers who tow heavy or do long highway hauls, the hybrid’s extra complexity and potential repair/recall exposure may not justify the premium unless fuel savings and local conditions favour it. See Edmunds True Cost for methodology.  
    Source: [Edmunds True Cost to Own](https://www.edmunds.com/toyota/tundra/2025/cost-to-own/), [FuelEconomy.gov]

- Buying recommendation — the pragmatic, Australian take:
  - If you need a full‑size truck for heavy towing and want Toyota badge reliability with less worry: buy only if
    - The vehicle shows documented completion of all Toyota recall/service campaigns (specifically engine recall 25V767 and transmission campaign 24TA02 where applicable). Request written proof from Toyota Australia. [LembergLaw, CherishYourCar]
    - The truck has a complete dealer service history (oil at 10,000 km or sooner if used for towing; hybrid systems serviced by a Toyota hybrid‑qualified tech).
    - ADAS warnings are clean and there’s evidence of sensor/calibration work if the vehicle previously had faults. [go‑parts PCS guide]
  - Which year/trim is the sweet spot?
    - Optimal financial sweet spot today in NSW: a late‑2023 low‑to‑mid trim (SR5/Limited) with i‑FORCE (non‑hybrid) where price sits at the lower end of the listed band (near AUD $115k), low kilometres (<30k), and a clear recall/service sheet. That avoids both the premium hybrid price and reduces exposure to the most complex repairs. If you want hybrid torque and can get a hybrid with full dealer‑proven recall fixes and warranty extensions, a high‑trim i‑FORCE MAX can be worth it — if you calculate total ownership costs and accept potentially higher buy‑in. (NSW prices and listings reflect 2023 model year availability and pricing pressure.)
    - Avoid: any 2022–2024 Tundra where engine recall status is unclear or service history is incomplete. Don’t gamble on cheap bargains that lack paperwork — the pain of a major engine rebuild or replacement will dwarf any purchase‑price savings.

Final call: The Tundra is a serious piece of hardware with class‑leading towing and a premium feel in high trims. But this generation carries new‑engine and software‑era growing pains. If you’re paying over AUD $115k in NSW, you deserve a truck with a clean recall history, up‑to‑date ADAS calibrations, and a full dealer logbook. If those boxes are ticked, buy confidently — otherwise walk away. Toyota’s resale reputation is strong, but this iteration demands paperwork, not faith.
