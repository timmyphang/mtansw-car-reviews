#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation, timeframe & codes
  - This review covers the second‑generation Chrysler 300 (2011–2023) — the LX‑platform, full‑size rear‑wheel‑drive sedan family that evolved through the 2010s. The platform is usually referenced as the **Chrysler LX platform** in factory and enthusiast documentation [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_LX_Platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_LX_Platform).
- Product hierarchy (what sits above / below)
  - Within Chrysler’s (Stellantis’) stable, the 300 sat at the top of the brand’s passenger‑car range as the large, luxo‑barge. It was the marque’s flagship sedan—above smaller family cars and beneath true prestige brands (Genesis, Lexus, Mercedes etc.) in buyer perception and dealer support [MotorTrend overview of Chrysler models](https://www.motortrend.com/cars/chrysler).
- Shared global platform and parts availability
  - The LX platform underpins the Chrysler 300 and closely related Dodge models, making many mechanical and trim pieces interchangeable across the family (helpful for parts availability when something goes wrong) — see the LX platform page for platform sharing details [https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_LX_Platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_LX_Platform).
- Market‑specific engines, outputs, transmissions and drive configurations (Australia)
  - Most second‑generation 300s Australia saw were offered with:
    - **3.6L Pentastar V6** (commonly quoted ~300 hp / 8‑speed automatic; available with RWD or AWD on some trims) — used across the range including the sporty 300S in later years [Chrysler eBrochure 2019 specifying 3.6L Pentastar and 8‑speed auto](https://www.chrysler.com/content/dam/fca-brands/na/chrysler/en_us/global/pdf/brochure/eBrochure%20-%20Chrysler%20300%20%282019%29.pdf).
    - **5.7L HEMI V8** (bigger torque, strong midrange; 8‑speed auto; RWD) and, on special/higher‑end models, **6.4L HEMI** (SRT/300C performance variants) [Chrysler product/performance pages](https://www.chrysler.com/bm/300/performance.html).
    - Older/lower‑volume second‑generation years (and US market earlier models) could also carry V6 variants of different capacities — the 2.7L/3.5L V6s feature in the model’s broader history, but the 3.6 Pentastar is the dominant fitment in 2011‑on cars covered here [Wikipedia Chrysler 300 overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chrysler_300).
  - Transmission
    - The primary automatic used from 2011 onward is the ZF‑derived **8HP70 8‑speed automatic** [chrysler‑specs transmission page](https://chrysler-specs.com/300/2011-present/8hp70-trans/specs/).
  - Drive configurations
    - Rear‑wheel drive is standard on most trims; V6 models could be optioned with AWD on selected years and markets [CarMax summary on AWD availability](https://www.carmax.com/research/chrysler/300).
- Market rivals
  - Direct and practical rivals in Australia include the Dodge Charger (platform relative), Nissan Maxima, Toyota Avalon, and older large executive sedans such as the Lexus ES or early‑generation Genesis G80 depending on budget and equipment level [TheCarConnection competitors list](https://www.thecarconnection.com/compare/chrysler_300_choices).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Chrysler 300?

- Exterior
  - Panel alignment & paint:
    - Owners report uneven panel gaps and inconsistent fit/finish on some model years — not terminal but noticeable if you expect German‑level body tolerances [Haynes most frequently reported problems](https://us.haynes.com/blogs/tips-tutorials/most-frequently-reported-chrysler-300-problems).
    - Clearcoat delamination and paint/clearcoat peeling has turned up repeatedly on hoods and roofs in owner forums — UV and ageing chemistry seem to take a toll; expect to budget for paint correction if you’re picky [300cforums clearcoat thread](https://www.300cforums.com/threads/peeling-clearcoat.71448/).
  - Trim vulnerabilities:
    - Interior and exterior trim items can be inexpensive plastics prone to fading, scratching and loss of gloss. Gloss‑black or piano‑black finishes scratch easily; chrome can micro‑pock with age.
  - Door panels:
    - A recurring pain‑point on many 2011–2023 Chargers and 300s is door interior trim warping, pulling away or bubbling in heat — owners report gaps, rattles and loose seams, particularly in sun‑baked climates (e.g., Australian summers) [go‑parts door panel guidance](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/door-interior-trim-panel-dodge-charger-chrysler-300-2011-2026) and community write‑ups [ChryslerProblems door panel writeup](https://www.chryslerproblems.com/300-warped-door-panels/).
  - Recalls & safety systems:
    - The 300 has featured in multiple recall campaigns across its life — always check the VIN on NHTSA or Mopar recall lookup tools before purchase [NHTSA recall search](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) and [Mopar recall lookup](https://www.mopar.com/en-us/my-vehicle/recalls/search.html).
  - ADAS quirks:
    - Owners report forward‑collision/automatic emergency braking sensitivity, false braking, or failures to intervene — with documented complaints and TSBs for adaptive systems. These can sometimes be corrected by dealer software updates or sensor calibrations, but confirm any outstanding ADAS recalls/TSBs on the VIN [CarProblemZoo AEB complaints](https://www.carproblemzoo.com/chrysler/300/automatic-emergency-braking-problems.php) and NHTSA recall resource [NHTSA](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls).

- Interior
  - Durability for heavy use:
    - If you plan on rideshare or taxi duty, be blunt: cabin materials show wear — seat bolsters, leather/vinyl surfaces and the warped door panels noted above degrade under high‑use and heat. The 300’s size helps passenger comfort, but long hours will expose trim weaknesses [Haynes problems summary](https://us.haynes.com/blogs/tips-tutorials/most-frequently-reported-chrysler-300-problems).
  - Entry/egress:
    - Big sedan, generous door openings and comfortable sill heights make ingress/egress easy for adults and kids — a practical plus if you regularly tote passengers.
  - Electrical & infotainment:
    - The Uconnect infotainment suite has a long history of freezes, blank screens and Bluetooth/phone reliability issues across Stellantis brands — symptoms range from intermittent Bluetooth drops to full‑screen blackouts that only a dealer or screen replacement fixes [GeekDIY Uconnect failures summary](https://www.geekdiymechanic.com/CarComplaints/Stellantis_UconnectInfotainment) and Cuescreens guide to touchscreen issues [Cuescreens article](https://www.cuescreens.com/blogs/news/chrysler-300-touchscreen-problems-what-owners-need-to-know).
    - Owners often perform soft resets (battery‑disconnect or fuse pull) as a first aid; wider Uconnect security history including remote exploit issues are documented (see Mechanical/security below) [IOActive remote Uconnect exploit whitepaper](https://www.ioactive.com/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/IOActive_Remote_Car_Hacking.pdf).
  - Screen sizes & connectivity:
    - Screen hardware tracked with trim and year; higher trims got larger Uconnect displays, but both hardware and software versions vary widely — check the unit operation in person. Wireless charging and accessory features have had TSBs and bulletins in later models for slow or non‑functional pads [STAR bulletin wireless charging troubleshooting on dot.report](https://dot.report/bulletins/11031693).
  - 12V battery drain patterns:
    - While not universal, there are persistent reports of parasitic drains tied to infotainment modules and after‑market telematics. If a car sits for a week and needs a jump, factor a cost for diagnosing parasitic draw (battery, alternator diodes, module sleep behaviour) and consider replacing an ageing battery pre‑emptively [CarParts reliability summary](https://www.carparts.com/blog/chrysler-300-reliability-and-common-problems/).

- Mechanically
  - General engineering reputation:
    - The 300 is mechanically straightforward: robust V6 and V8 architectures, conventional suspension topology and big‑car mechanicals. Where it falls down is electrical gremlins and the odd transmission or timing‑related headache — not exotic failures, but sometimes expensive when they arrive [AutoReliabilityIndex reliability analysis](https://autoreliabilityindex.com/chrysler/300).
  - Security vulnerabilities:
    - A note to keep you awake at night: the Chrysler family has been subject to high‑profile remote infotainment exploits in the past (the 2015 Uconnect vulnerability) and modern thieves have used so‑called “CAN injection” or headlight‑connector attacks to spoof immobiliser messages on many makes/models — the technique is documented in security research and mainstream press [IOActive whitepaper on remote exploits](https://www.ioactive.com/wp-content/uploads/pdfs/IOActive_Remote_Car_Hacking.pdf), and the CAN injection writeups explain real‑world theft vectors [can-cia CAN injection overview](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/), [Autoblog reporting on CAN injection thefts](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update).
  - Practical anti‑theft measures:
    - Don’t rely on stock immobiliser alone. Practical mitigations endorsed by motoring and insurance bodies include:
      - Signal‑blocking pouch for keys (Faraday pouch) and steering‑wheel physical lock.
      - Aftermarket alarm with CAN‑bus intrusion detection or a separate immobiliser/relay‑cut that sits between ignition and starter.
      - Park it in a well‑lit, CCTV/secure location. AAA has a good plain‑English guide on reducing keyless theft risk [AAA keyless theft guidance](https://www.ace.aaa.com/insurance/advocacy/keyless-car-theft.html).

- Engines
  - Note: below are every engine variant discussed in the source material. Each engine is italicised and followed by specific failure points and recommended preventative maintenance intervals (practical Australian service cadence).
  - *3.6L Pentastar V6*
    - Failure points:
      - Reported complaints of excessive oil consumption, timing‑chain tensioner wear/ticking, valve‑cover and oil‑filter housing oil leaks and rough idle or misfire symptoms as units age [CarTipsDaily Pentastar problems](https://cartipsdaily.com/3-6-pentastar-engine-problems-2026), [Wise Auto Tools technician notes](https://www.wiseautotools.com/blog/pentastar-36-engine-more-problems-tips-for-techs/).
      - Cooling / overheating issues less common than older V6s, but watch for coolant leaks and thermostat failures.
    - Preventative maintenance (recommended Australian practice):
      - Oil and filter: 10,000 km intervals with fully synthetic oil (or earlier if used in city stop‑start or towing). If the previous owner has a history of high oil consumption, shorten to 7,500 km.
      - Inspect valve‑cover gaskets and oil‑filter housing seals at 60,000 km and replace proactively if crusting/oil seepage appears.
      - Timing‑chain tensioner inspection around 150,000 km if weird ticking develops; don’t let late‑chain wear run into bearing damage.
  - *5.7L HEMI V8*
    - Failure points:
      - Top‑end lifter and cam lobe issues reported in some examples, particularly earlier revisions. The 5.7L “Eagle” updates changed VVT and oil demands — this altered top‑end stresses and increased oil consumption on some cars [CherishYourCar HEMI problems overview](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/5-7-hemi-engine-problems/).
      - Typical HEMI weaknesses are oil consumption, valve‑train noise, and intake manifold gasket leaks as the vehicle ages.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil change every 10,000 km (use manufacturer grade synthetic; some owners prefer 7,500 km for peace of mind).
      - Monitor lifter/cam noise and manage oil levels closely — HEMIs are finicky about oil quality and level.
      - Cooling system and thermostat checks at 60,000 km.
  - *6.4L (392) HEMI V8*
    - Failure points:
      - Performance engine — robust, but repairs and consumables (camshafts, lifters, high‑flow fuel system items) cost more. Less common in the Australian fleet, but expect higher running and insurance costs.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Shorter service intervals under hard driving: 7,500–10,000 km oil changes; more frequent brake and tyre checks if the car has been used spiritedly.
  - *2.7L and 3.5L V6 (historical fitments)*
    - Failure points:
      - Older 2.7L Chrysler V6s have a reputation for internal water‑pump failures and coolant entering oil (head gasket / internal pump issues) in some applications — these are less common in the 3.6 era but worth inspecting on earlier cars [CrewChief wiki note on water pump failure for 2.7L/3.5L](https://crewchief.cc/wiki/vehicle-problems/chrysler-300).
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - If you find cars with these earlier engines, insist on a compression test and oil‑analysis; change coolant and inspect water‑pump housing seals at 60,000 km.

- Transmission
  - ZF‑derived **8HP70 8‑speed automatic** (2011‑on)
    - Behaviour:
      - Generally smooth and efficient, modern multi‑ratio box that improves economy and performance. Real‑world owners praise the highway overdrive and the gearbox's ability to keep revs down.
    - Known failure modes:
      - Early and mid‑life 300s ran other automatics (NAG1 5‑speed earlier). Torque converter clutch issues and TCC solenoid failures have been common failure modes historically; contaminated ATF and conductor plate failures are a classic problem on older 5‑speed boxes and some early automatics [go-parts torque converter P0740 discussion](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0740-chrysler-300-2005-2012) and aftermarket/complaint aggregators list torque‑converter trouble on many model years [CarProblemZoo torque converter reports](https://www.carproblemzoo.com/chrysler/300/automatic-transmission-torque-converter-problems.php).
      - Symptoms to watch for: shudder on lockup, elevated revs at highway speed, slipping or limp‑home behaviour under load.
    - Service & care:
      - Factory guidance evolved across years; many independent techs recommend a fluid and filter service around 60,000–100,000 km (particularly if towing, high heat or sporty driving are common). Even if the factory says “fill for life,” used‑car buyers should budget a transmission service if maintenance history is thin [ZF 8HP technical reference](https://chrysler-specs.com/300/2011-present/8hp70-trans/specs/).
    - Cost impact:
      - Transmission rebuilds or torque converter replacements are expensive; check for smooth shifts at 1st→2nd, 3rd and at highway changes. A test‑drive on both cold start and after 20 minutes heat will reveal many faults.

- Marketplace entry price up to ceiling price
  - Based on your NSW snapshot, expect to pay from ~$30k for high‑kilometre, plain V6 examples up to the mid‑$40k mark for low‑kilometre, well‑specified 2014–2016 cars — the single listing in NSW is $44,990 for a 2015 car with ~22,800 km (dealer stock) — verify trim and options before you blink [NSW dealer data provided].
- Depreciation metrics (practical, Aussie reality)
  - Hard numbers are messy and vary by trim and condition, but practical patterns are:
    - First three years from new: steep drop typical of American large sedans once new‑car incentives and fleet deals finish — expect a 40–50% drop from showroom price over 3 years in many cases for non‑luxury large sedans of this era.
    - Compared to Japanese peers (Toyota Avalon) or Lexus equivalents, depreciation is steeper. A rough working rule: the 300 historically loses roughly 5–15 percentage points more value over three years than comparable Toyotas/Lexus where brand perception and reliability reputation retain value better [KBB/Edmunds value guidance for Chrysler 300 general context](https://www.kbb.com/chrysler/300/) [Edmunds overview](https://www.edmunds.com/chrysler/300/).
  - Why it matters: a well‑kept Toyota or Lexus may cost a little more up front but will likely be cheaper over time because of stronger residuals.
- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy
  - How to compute TCO (practical Aussie method):
    - Annual kilometres: set your real usage (e.g., 15,000 km/year for average Aussie; 30,000+ if commuting long distances or rideshare).
    - Fuel consumption: use observed owner figures (Fuelly aggregates show real‑world Chrysler 300 combined averages across both V6 and V8 accounts) — consult Fuelly and official economy pages to pick expected L/100km [Fuelly Chrysler 300 real‑world MPG](https://www.fuelly.com/car/chrysler/300) and official EPA data for ballpark conversions [FuelEconomy.gov example for older 300s](https://fueleconomy.gov/FEG/bymodel/2009_Chrysler_300.shtml).
    - Fuel cost: multiply litres used by your local petrol price (enter your current NSW petrol number) — the higher your annual km, the more a thirstier V8 penalises you.
    - Servicing & parts: account for $600–$1,200/year for scheduled servicing on a 3.6 V6 if dealer‑maintained, more for a V8 or high‑performance model; plan for occasional $1,000‑$3,000 shocks (timing chain tensioner or transmission service) as the car ages.
    - Depreciation: use the expected 3‑ and 5‑year drop to amortise purchase cost.
  - Hybrids/EVs vs conventional (does paying premium make sense?)
    - The 300’s lifecycle didn’t include mainstream factory hybrid/electric variants in the 2011–2023 family widely available in Australia. That means you’re comparing conventional petrol 300s to hybrid alternatives like an Avalon Hybrid or a used Lexus hybrid in the same physical price bracket.
    - Fuel savings math: Hybrids pay off for high‑city km drivers; if you do mostly suburban/urban driving and rack up >25,000 km/year, a hybrid’s superior L/100km in stop‑start city traffic can cut annual fuel spend substantially — use FuelEconomy.gov’s hybrid comparison tools to model this [FuelEconomy hybrid comparison tool](https://www.fueleconomy.gov/feg/hybridCompare.jsp) or the AFDC TCO calculator for scenario modelling [AFDC vehicle cost calculator](https://afdc.energy.gov/calc/).
    - Conclusion on powertrain value: For Australian buyers who do mostly highway kilometres and who value the V8 soundtrack, the petrol V6 (3.6 Pentastar) is the best compromise. If raw running cost and residuals are your top priorities and you do a lot of city driving, a rival hybrid‑power family sedan (used Toyota/Lexus hybrid) probably gives better long‑term savings.
- Buying recommendation — who should buy, and the sweet‑spot
  - If you want comfort, V8 burble and rear‑wheel‑drive cruiser style and you love a big‑car feel: proceed — but be selective and inspect carefully.
  - Practical buy checklist:
    - Target trim/years: 2014–2016 **3.6L Pentastar V6** cars are the sweet spot for most Aussies who want reasonable running costs, AWD availability, and simpler maintenance than a HEMI V8. The 3.6 V6 paired with the ZF 8‑speed is the balance of economy and performance [Chrysler eBrochure 2019; ZF 8HP reference](https://www.chrysler.com/content/dam/fca-brands/na/chrysler/en_us/global/pdf/brochure/eBrochure%20-%20Chrysler%20300%20%282019%29.pdf) [https://chrysler-specs.com/300/2011-present/8hp70-trans/specs/](https://chrysler-specs.com/300/2011-present/8hp70-trans/specs/).
    - Avoid: earlier 2.7/3.5 V6 cars (potential internal water‑pump/wear history), and unproven, high‑mileage HEMIs unless you want the V8 experience and are prepared for higher running and insurance costs [CrewChief failure notes; CherishYourCar](https://crewchief.cc/wiki/vehicle-problems/chrysler-300) [https://www.cherishyourcar.com/5-7-hemi-engine-problems/](https://www.cherishyourcar.com/5-7-hemi-engine-problems/).
    - Inspection priorities:
      - VIN recall/TSB check at NHTSA/Mopar before purchase [NHTSA recall search](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls) [Mopar recall lookup](https://www.mopar.com/en-us/my-vehicle/recalls/search.html).
      - Test Uconnect functionality thoroughly — Bluetooth, nav, screen responsiveness; if it’s flaky, treat it as a reduction in value unless seller repairs it [GeekDIY Uconnect complaints](https://www.geekdiymechanic.com/CarComplaints/Stellantis_UconnectInfotainment).
      - Check door panels for warping or separation; interior trim repair is cheap‑to‑nuisance but can indicate sun‑damage and other interior neglect [go‑parts door panel writeup](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/door-interior-trim-panel-dodge-charger-chrysler-300-2011-2026).
      - Transmission test: watch for shudder, noise at torque converter lockup or slip. Request service records for fluid changes; if absent, schedule a transmission fluid/filter service early.
      - Compression test and oil analysis if the car has no transparent history for oil consumption signs (especially on Pentastar and HEMI engines).
    - Price target:
      - For a 2015 3.6 V6 in good condition and average kilometres, expect to pay well under the $44,990 NSW listing unless the car is unique (low km, dealer‑certified). The NSW single listing at $44,990 (2015 / 22,813 km) is a premium example — verify options and service history before you pay that figure (data: MTA NSW scrape provided).
  - Final verdict:
    - Buy one if you want a big, comfortable rear‑wheel‑drive cruiser with a US‑car personality and you can accept the potential for infotainment headaches, some interior trim fragility, and the need to be selective on transmission/engine condition.
    - Don’t buy one if you need guaranteed low TCO, best‑in‑class reliability, or perfect ADAS behaviour — in those cases a Japanese rival or a Lexus hybrid will be a more conservative long‑term buy.
    - Best financial sweet spot: a mid‑trim 2014–2016 **3.6 Pentastar V6** with full service history, recent transmission fluid service and solid evidence of no Uconnect/new‑software faults. Expect to budget proactively for common trim/infotainment fixes and to perform a conservative transmission service early in ownership.
