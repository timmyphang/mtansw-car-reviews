#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation(s) covered: Subaru Liberty (Australian-market name for the Subaru Legacy). We're focusing on the modern fourth/fifth/sixth‑generation era that you’ll find on the NSW used market: Gen‑4 (BL/BP, 2003–2009), Gen‑5 (BM/BR, 2009–2014) and later BN/BS (2014–2020) examples. Wikipedia’s Legacy page gives the generation timeline and codes if you want the deep family tree [Wikipedia – Subaru Legacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Legacy).
- Where it sits in the range: the Liberty is Subaru’s mid‑size family sedan/wagon — above the compact Impreza/WRX and below Subaru’s rare full‑size offerings. It’s the AWD alternative to mainstream midsize sedans such as the Toyota Camry and Mazda6 in Australia ([CarsGuide commentary on exit of the Liberty](https://www.carsguide.com.au/car-news/subaru-liberty-officially-axed-in-australia-leaving-toyota-camry-and-mazda-6-80533)).
- Shared global platform: the Liberty/Legacy shares Subaru’s horizontally‑opposed “boxer” architecture and rear/centre transaxle or longitudinal layouts across model families. That platform DNA means many mechanical bits are shared with Forester, Outback and Impreza derivatives — helpful for parts availability (see Subaru Australia specs and Legacy platform notes: [Subaru Australia specs](https://www.subaru.com.au/liberty/specs?item=23527), [Wikipedia – Legacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Legacy)).
- Engines / transmissions / drivetrains available in Australia (market snapshot across generations):
  - 2.0 L boxer‑4 — petrol, naturally aspirated (paired with manuals or automatics on older gens).
  - 2.5 L boxer‑4 — petrol (EJ and later FB families), common across many MYs; outputs vary by year/variant. Later 2.5s generally drive through Subaru’s Lineartronic CVT in newer models ([CarExpert 2019 Liberty specs](https://www.carexpert.com.au/subaru/liberty/2019)).
  - 3.0 L flat‑6 (**EZ30**) — older Gen‑4 units (luxury/higher‑trim options).
  - 3.6 L flat‑6 (**EZ36**) — later high‑power variant (used through the 2015+ era; CarExpert lists 3.6L 191 kW/350 Nm for late‑model Liberties) ([CarExpert 2019 Liberty](https://www.carexpert.com.au/subaru/liberty/2019)).
  - Transmission choices: manuals (5/6‑speed on older cars), torque‑converter automatics, and Subaru’s Lineartronic CVT on many 2009+ models. See Subaru transmission families ([List of Subaru transmissions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Subaru_transmissions)).
  - Drivetrain: All‑wheel drive as standard — that’s the Liberty’s core selling point across all generations ([Wikipedia – Subaru Legacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Legacy)).
- Marketplace rivals: Toyota Camry, Mazda6, Honda Accord and mid‑size alternatives such as the Kia K5/Hyundai Sonata when shopping used; the Liberty’s AWD sets it apart from most.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Subaru Liberty?
This is the bit people skim until the “should I buy?” punchline. I broke it into the practical chunks you’ll want when inspecting a car.

Exterior
- Panel fit and paint:
  - Panel alignment is generally solid for later model Liberties but earlier facelift examples (Gen‑4) can show ageing trim wear and mismatched panels if dealer‑repaired — forums are full of owner photos ([The Subaru Forums](https://www.thesubaruforums.com/forums/subaru-legacy.11/)).
  - Paint and clear‑coat complaints exist — owners report clear‑coat flaking around roofs/doors on some models; follow‑up threads and owner forums show recurring reports and DIY fixes ([SubaruOutback forum thread on paint peeling](https://www.subaruforester.org/threads/2015-legacy-outback-limited-paint-peeling-any-fixes.870215/), and a summary article on Subaru paint issues [EngineCrux](https://enginecrux.com/understanding-subaru-paint-problems-and-solutions/)).
  - Plastic trim vulnerabilities: gloss black or faux‑piano black inserts on higher trims scratch visibly; chrome pieces near door handles can pit.
- Body recalls / ADAS hardware:
  - Subaru has done multiple recall campaigns across Legacy/Liberty generations — always VIN‑check a car at the manufacturer site before purchase ([Subaru recall lookup (AU)](https://www.subaru.com.au/recall)). Austra­lian‑focused breakdowns of BM/BR and BN‑era recalls and TSBs are collated by Australian Car.Reviews ([Recalls & faults: BM/BR and BN Liberty](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-subaru-bm-br-liberty-2009-14/), [BN Liberty 2014–20 summary](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-subaru-bn-liberty-2014-20/)).
- ADAS quirks:
  - EyeSight and camera‑based systems introduced on later Liberties deliver good safety kit but have sensitivity quirks reported in owner complaint databases. Recent headlines in the US allege unwanted AEB/automatic braking activations in 2022–2026 Subarus — it’s an industry‑level problem for camera‑based systems and worth checking for software updates and service history ([classaction.org summary of lawsuit](https://www.classaction.org/news/class-action-lawsuit-says-certain-2022-2026-subaru-models-equipped-with-defective-collision-avoidance-features), [CarComplaints EyeSight listing](https://www.carcomplaints.com/news/2026/subaru-eyesight-lawsuit.shtml)). For used Liberties with Eyesight, ask the dealer if the camera firmware and vehicle software are up to date and that no AEB warnings/flags are present.

Interior
- Durability and fit:
  - Liberty cabins are generally well built and comfortable for long runs; higher‑kilometre examples used for transport work (rideshare/taxi) can show seat bolster wear, rubbing to door cards and softened armrests. CarsGuide’s reviews point out that equipment levels are generous but not immune to typical mid‑size sedan wear ([CarsGuide Liberty page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/subaru/liberty)).
  - Entry/exit: low door sills on sedan variants make ingress/egress easy; wagons can be slightly higher but still family‑friendly.
- Electronics and infotainment:
  - Subaru’s Starlink and head‑unit software have had frozen screens, slow boots and CarPlay/Android Auto hiccups in the last decade. Soft resets and dealer firmware flashes fix many cases — owners recommend checking for recent updates in service history ([Starlink support & firmware guidance](https://www.subaru.com/vehicle-info/connected-services/mysubaru-connected-services/customer-support.html), fixes and reset guides in community writeups: [Subaru touch screen fixes](https://cartipsdaily.com/subaru-touch-screen-not-working-fix), [reset walkthroughs](https://wheelsgreed.com/subaru-infotainment-system-reset/)).
  - Wireless charging pads can be temperamental depending on phone case/design and pad condition — troubleshooting advice exists but check functionality during inspection ([Wireless charger fixes](https://theautoinsiderblog.com/subaru-wireless-charger-not-working/)).
  - 12V battery drain: intermittent reports in owner forums and ProductReview entries indicate accessory or ECU drain events in older cars — verify battery age and that the vehicle sleeps correctly after locking ([ProductReview Liberty listings](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/subaru-liberty)).
- Practical points:
  - Reversing camera quality and speaker fits can be poor on some MYs — test the camera clarity and speaker buzz at the test drive ([CarsGuide notes](https://www.carsguide.com.au/subaru/liberty)).

Mechanically
- Engineering reputation: Subaru’s boxer engines and full‑time AWD are durable when maintained, but the marque’s history brings some clear reliability caveats (notably certain EJ‑series head gasket failures and later FB‑series oil consumption). The engine architecture gives a lower centre of gravity and robust traction, especially in wet Australian conditions ([Wikipedia Legacy engine architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subaru_Legacy)).
- Security vulnerabilities:
  - CAN‑injection/headlight‑tap theft method is a real world risk to many modern vehicles. Researchers document how thieves access wiring and inject frames to spoof keys/immobilisers — the practical takeaway: don’t assume a used Liberty is immune ([CAN Injection technical writeup](https://www.can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf), mainstream coverage on the method [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how_thieves_steal_cars_using_vehicle_can_bus/)). Subaru also had a cloud/Starlink related vulnerability in the past that was patched — check that dealer systems were updated ([Infosecurity Mag article on Subaru bug](https://www.infosecurity-magazine.com/news/subaru-bug-remote-vehicle-tracking/)).
  - Recommended aftermarket countermeasures:
    - Physical: high‑visibility steering wheel lock, mechanical gearbox/handbrake locks.
    - Electrical/digital: aftermarket immobiliser with a secondary relay kill, install a reputable alarm with shock/tilt sensor and GPS tracker, protect key fobs in a Faraday pouch when parked at home.
    - Secure wiring: have a trusted workshop fit headlight loom covers or relocate/secure access points vulnerable to CAN taps.
- High‑priority inspection points before purchase:
  - Evidence of oil leaks from the head gasket area, coolant stains, coolant‑smell in oil or white smoke.
  - CVT fluid or gearbox leaks and service stickers indicating CVT service.
  - Service history for software/ADAS camera calibrations — EyeSight cameras need to be intact and correctly aligned.

Engines
(Every engine code/variant mentioned in our sources is covered below — italicised engine names as requested.)

- *EJ25 / EJ251 / EJ252 / EJ253 (2.5‑litre EJ‑series boxer)*
  - Known failure points:
    - Head gasket failures are the classic EJ25 problem — external gasket leaks letting coolant and oil cross paths. Owner complaint aggregators and specialist pages catalog thousands of incidents ([ProblemsByVin EJ25 head gasket](https://problemsbyvin.com/engines/subaru-ej25-head-gasket/), [UnderhoodService primer](https://www.underhoodservice.com/subaru-head-gaskets/)).
    - Timing chain tensioner stretch/guide wear on older EJ iterations.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Check coolant condition and oil for cross‑contamination during test drive.
    - If you buy a high‑kilometre EJ25 Liberty, budget for head gasket service or at least a pre‑purchase compression/leak‑down and coolant pressure test.
    - Regular oil and coolant changes: 10,000 km intervals are typical for Subaru in Australia — stick to these shorter, frequent checks if the car’s used hard.
  - Service interval note: conservative servicing every 10,000 km (or 12 months) if used in town/harsh conditions.

- *FB25 (2.5‑litre FB boxer; later replacement for EJ25)*
  - Known failure points:
    - Excessive oil consumption has been widely reported on the FB series — piston ring/cylinder issues and carbon‑build up cause increased oil use ([ProblemsByVin FB25 oil consumption](https://problemsbyvin.com/engines/subaru-fb-oil-consumption/), [MotorReviewer FB25 summary](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=73)).
    - PCV system and intake carbonation issues can exacerbate oil burn.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Check service records for oil top‑ups and whether the seller used the correct oil viscosity and interval (Subaru often recommends 10,000 km service intervals Conservative owners go 10k not 15k).
    - Compression/leak‑down and confirm oil consumption rates — expect some FB25s to use up to ~1 L per 3–5,000 km in bad cases; anything like that is a red flag.
  - Service interval note: stick to 10,000 km services; change oil and filter with high‑quality oil to reduce ring clogging.

- *EZ30 (3.0‑litre flat‑6)*
  - Known failure points:
    - Generally robust but expensive to repair if head or valve work is needed due to packaging complexity.
    - Watch for oil leaks and cooling system condition; these engines like being looked after.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Keep up with coolant and belt/chain inspections; confirm major service history.

- *EZ36 / 3.6‑litre flat‑6 (later high‑power 3.6L)*
  - Known failures:
    - Typically robust but fuel consumption and servicing costs are higher (expect worse fuel economy than 2.5L units).
    - More complex cylinder head work if problems emerge.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Service history is king — a 3.6L Liberty without receipts should be treated cautiously.

Transmission
- Manuals, conventional torque‑converter autos and CVTs have all been used in Liberties. The big talking point is Subaru’s Lineartronic CVT on the newer models.
- Known modes and symptoms:
  - CVT (Lineartronic):
    - Complaints include shuddering, slipping and whining — ProblemsByVin and specialist write‑ups document thousands of complaints across Subaru Lineartronic applications ([ProblemsByVin Lineartronic CVT](https://problemsbyvin.com/transmissions/subaru-lineartronic-cvt/), [CVTMechanic guide](https://cvtmechanic.com/subaru/subaru-cvt-transmission-problems-symptoms-and-costs/)).
    - Causes range from incorrect fluid, overdue fluid changes, worn pulleys or clutch packs and electronic control issues.
    - Some older CVT oil cooling hoses were recalled on BM/BR cars (see Australian Car.Reviews recall list) — ensure recalls/TSBs addressed.
  - Torque‑converter automatics:
    - Older 4/5‑speed autos are generally durable but expect rougher downshifts with high miles.
  - Manuals:
    - 5/6‑speed boxes are straightforward and long‑lived if clutches and synchromeshes have been treated kindly.
- Inspection and preventative:
  - For CVT cars: get a road test looking for shudder at low speeds, hesitation under load and audible whining. Ask for CVT fluid service history — many pros recommend CVT fluid change every 60–80k km with the correct Subaru CVTF grade.
  - For autos: check for slipping, delayed engagement and burnt smell on test drive.

  Use those as your local negotiating baseline — two cars is thin inventory, so don’t be surprised if prices sit stubbornly near the median.
- Broader asking prices and where that fits:
  - National listing hubs show a wider spread — carsales and Drive are useful to compare sample asking prices across Australia ([carsales Liberty listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/subaru/liberty/), [Drive Liberty listings](https://www.drive.com.au/cars-for-sale/search/all/all/subaru/liberty/)). Expect newer, low‑km BN examples to be north of the NSW median; older Gen‑4 cars can sit below AUD 10k in private sales.
- Depreciation metrics:
  - Typical Legacy/Liberty depreciation studies estimate roughly 35–42% value loss over five years depending on market and trim — CarEdge and iSeeCars provide context for Legacy depreciation versus segment averages ([CarEdge depreciation](https://caredge.com/subaru/legacy/depreciation), [iSeeCars resale value](https://www.iseecars.com/car/subaru-legacy/resale-value)).
  - Practically: Liberties depreciate more than Toyotas, less than some luxury brands — but the AWD and boxer engine uniqueness accelerates depreciation once high‑risk faults (head gasket, CVT) become common on a car.
- TCO strategy and hybrid/economy math:
  - Fuel: combined consumption for petrol Liberties commonly starts around 7.3 L/100 km for sedans in real world on many model/year combos but expect the 3.6L to be significantly thirstier ([CarsGuide fuel consumption summary](https://www.carsguide.com.au/subaru/liberty/fuel-consumption)).
  - Use the Australian Government Green Vehicle Guide or RACQ running costs for a standardised fuel‑cost model: 14,000 km/year baseline is typical. The Green Vehicle Guide helps estimate annual fuel costs based on combined L/100 km ([Green Vehicle Guide](https://greenvehicleguide.gov.au/Vehicle/ViewVehicle?vehicleDisplayIdList=29214%3A29215%3A29216)), RACQ gives running costs context ([RACQ running costs](https://www.racq.com.au/car/research/car-running-costs/running-costs-2025)).
  - Hybrid vs petrol payback: Subaru’s hybrid offerings (Forester Hybrid, e‑Boxer tech) aren’t the same as the Liberty platform, so you’d be comparing a Liberty petrol to a different model or an aftermarket hybrid conversion. KBB and DriveClarity explain breakeven methodologies — typically, a hybrid must deliver substantially lower fuel use and/or lower maintenance/resale losses to justify a higher purchase price over a 5–7 year ownership window ([KBB hybrid vs gas methodology](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/), [DriveClarity petrol vs hybrid calculator](https://www.driveclarity.com.au/petrol-vs-hybrid-cost-calculator)). In the Liberty market you’ll usually save money by buying a well‑sorted petrol 2.5 rather than paying extra for a rare hybrid conversion or higher‑priced 3.6 litre — unless your annual km is very high (>25,000 km) and fuel economy differential is large.
- Depreciation and TCO quick example (rule of thumb):
  - Buy a 2014‑2016 Liberty 2.5 auto at AUD 15,000 with ~80k km.
  - Assume annual fuel ~7.5 L/100 km, 14,000 km/year, fuel @ AUD 2.00/L → annual fuel = ~AUD 2,100.
  - Servicing, tyres, insurance + registration & unexpected repairs = variable, but RACQ data suggests mid‑sized sedan ownership (per year) ~AUD 3,000–4,000 excluding depreciation. Add a buffer for potential head gasket/CVT work if history is incomplete.
  - If you bought a hybrid variant (if you found one), you’d need a purchase premium of several thousand dollars and lower fuel — break‑even is often several years; for mainstream Liberty shoppers, petrol is the more prudent choice.
- Final buying recommendation — blunt and useful:
  - If you want the Liberty for AWD, long‑distance comfort and the Subaru feel, buy one — but be choosy.
  - Optimal financial sweet spot: a BM/BR or early BN (2009–2016) 2.5L automatic with full service history, around 60–120k km, priced near the NSW median (AUD 13–17k). That’s where the value, equipment and risk profile converge unless you specifically want the 3.6L for performance (expect higher fuel and likely higher maintenance bills).
  - Avoid:
    - EJ25 cars with suspicious coolant/oil history or no receipts — head gasket bills chew cash.
    - CVT‑equipped Liberties without a documented CVTF service history or with shudder/whine on the road test.
    - Low‑priced examples with spotty service records — the savings can evaporate in one big repair.
  - Inspection checklist before you buy:
    - Full service history (10k km interval evidence).
    - Recent CVT fluid change if car has Lineartronic; proof of any recall/TSB work via VIN check ([Subaru AU recall lookup](https://www.subaru.com.au/recall)).
    - Compression/leak‑down test or a mechanical inspection for EJ/FB engines.
    - Road test for CVT shudder, AEB odd behaviour, and infotainment function.
    - Check for aftermarket security measures or evidence of theft attempts; consider adding a physical immobiliser and steering lock if none fitted.
  - Verdict: Recommended for drivers who value AWD, practical space and Subaru’s long‑haul comfort — buy with caution, paperwork and a pre‑purchase inspection are non‑negotiable. For price sensitivity, the NSW market median ~AUD 15k is reasonable for a well‑presented 2012–2018 unit; haggle hard if the service history is sketchy.
