#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation(s): the Jeep Compass you’ll see in NSW classifieds is a moving target. Older used stock (2013–2025 in the current NSW dealer snapshot) covers the first and second-generation Compasses sold globally; Jeep unveiled an all‑new Compass for 2026 built on Stellantis’ STLA Medium architecture. See Jeep/Stellantis for the new‑generation platform details. ([Jeep 2026 overview](https://www.jeep.com/compass.html); [Stellantis press release](https://media.stellantis.com/em-en/jeep/press/introducing-the-all-new-jeep-compass-redefining-adventure-with-distinctive-design-legendary-capability-versatility-and-cutting-edge-technology))

- Internal alphanumeric/platform references you should know:
  - STLA Medium — the architecture for the 2026 Compass and future Stellantis models ([Stellantis PR](https://media.stellantis.com/em-en/jeep/press/introducing-the-all-new-jeep-compass-redefining-adventure-with-distinctive-design-legendary-capability-versatility-and-cutting-edge-technology)).
  - Historic powerplants commonly found in used Australian stock: the **2.4L Tigershark (MultiAir)** and the smaller **1.4L MultiAir turbo**; newer late‑model/2024–2026 cars shift to a **2.0L turbo** with an 8‑speed automatic on the STLA cars (see Stellantis/Edmunds spec pages).

- Product hierarchy: Compass sits between the compact Renegade and the larger Cherokee in Jeep’s range — the Renegade is the city‑sized off‑roader below it; Cherokee and Grand Cherokee sit above. Autoblog’s model comparison is a handy cross‑reference. ([Autoblog Compass vs Cherokee](https://www.autoblog.com/features/jeep-compass-vs-jeep-cherokee))

- Shared global foundations and parts trickle-down:
  - The 2026 Compass is STLA Medium; that platform is shared across future Stellantis midsize electrified products (helpful for long‑term parts availability and dealer knowledge). ([Stellantis PR](https://media.stellantis.com/em-en/jeep/press/introducing-the-all-new-jeep-compass-redefining-adventure-with-distinctive-design-legendary-capability-versatility-and-cutting-edge-technology))
  - Older Compasses share engines and transmissions with Cherokee, Renegade and some Fiat/Chrysler siblings — this helps sourcing common consumables and some mechanical parts (see donor‑car guides for 2.4L Tigershark parts interchange). ([Go‑Parts donor guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/parts-cars-trucks-ram-promaster-city-jeep-cherokee-jeep-compass-2013-2022))

- Market-specific engines / outputs / transmissions / drive layouts commonly seen in Australian used stock:
  - **2.0‑litre turbo inline‑4** (newer Compass 2024–2026 listings): ≈200‑hp / 221 lb‑ft; paired to an **8‑speed automatic**, standard AWD/4x4 on most trims. ([Edmunds 2025 specs](https://www.edmunds.com/jeep/compass/2025/features-specs/); [Stellantis fleet PDF specs](https://www.stellantisfleet.com/content/dam/fca-fleet/na/fleet/en_us/shopping-tools/brochures-literature/docs/buyers-guide/2026/26DOMMOP_FBG_Compass.pdf))

  - **1.4L MultiAir turbo** — used in some markets/years, small‑capacity turbo with variable valve tech.
  - Transmission mix historically: early Compasses (2007–2016 US models) used a **Jatco CVT** in some spec levels; later models moved to **ZF 9‑speed** automatics or Aisin 6‑speeds depending on year and market. NSW dealer snapshot shows only automatics in the live scrape (Automatic: 7). #### 2. What goes wrong with a used Jeep Compass?
Below is the hard diagnostic truth, organised so you can walk into an inspection armed with questions and a torch.

- Exterior:
  - Panel alignment & trim: owner threads and parts guides report persistent trim‑panel fitment problems on 2017–2021 Compasses — notably loose C‑pillar trim pieces and panels that can work loose and rattle or expose gaps. Cosmetic, but can lead to wind noise and water ingress over time. ([Go‑Parts C‑pillar trim guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/body-c-pillar-trim-panel-jeep-compass-2017-2021))
  - Paint durability: there are documented owner complaints across FCA brands about peeling paint and flaking on exposed areas — not unique to Compass but worth checking high‑wear panels (hood, roof, wheelarches). ([cypaint paint issues overview](https://cypaint.com/article/does-jeep-chrysler-have-issues-with-peeling-paint))
  - Recalls / detached trim: Stellantis/Wagoneer campaigns have flagged exterior trim detachment risks on some recent models — check every VIN for open recalls via Mopar or NHTSA equivalents. ([Go‑Parts trims recall commentary](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/exterior-trim-panel-jeep-wagoneer-jeep-grand-wagoneer-jeep-compass-2021-2026); [Mopar recall lookup](https://www.mopar.com/jeep/en-us/my-vehicle/recalls/search.html))
  - ADAS sensor sensitivity: forward‑facing radar/camera modules on 2022–2026 models have shown quirks — radar vertical misalignment and FCW/ACC unavailability can appear, sometimes requiring sensor adjustment or TSB‑level fixes; owners have reported random “FCW/ACC unavailable” messages. These are not “drive‑it‑away” disasters, but they matter if you expect reliable autonomous safety aids. ([NHTSA complaint grid](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle/2025/JEEP/COMPASS/SUV/AWD); [TSB C14A4 ACC sensor adjustment](https://dot.report/bulletins/11019762); [FCW off investigation TSB](https://dot.report/bulletins/11018267))
  - Reality check: if the seller brags about “excellent ADAS,” check for stored messages in the dash, ask for dealer printouts after a firmware/TSB visit, and test ACC/AEB in a quiet, safe environment.

- Interior:
  - Cabin durability: Compasses are fine for normal family use, but several reports flag cheap cabin plastics and terminals that don’t age well under hard commercial duty (rideshare/taxi). Door checks and lower sills should be inspected for beaten entry points on second‑hand units.
  - Entry/exit ergonomics: door sill height and door checks are typical of a compact SUV — accessible but check door check springs and lower seals for wear if the vehicle has been a fleet unit.
  - Infotainment & electrical gremlins:
    - Widespread Uconnect/infotainment complaints for 2020–2024 cars include freezing, blank screens and backup‑camera failure; dealers have used software flashes and sometimes hardware replacements. ([Uconnect freezing report](https://www.buymoparwarranty.com/blog/2020-2024-jeep-compass-interior-tech-issues-uconnect-backup-camera-screen-blackouts/))
    - Wireless charging failures attracted a TSB (S2308000034) covering “no/slow wireless charging” across Stellantis models — diagnostic procedure exists so don’t accept “it’s intermittent” as a deal breaker without proof of TSB work. ([Wireless charging TSB summary](https://carandproblems.com/bulletins/jeep/compass/11025641-2026-jeep-compass-wireless-charging-not-working); [DOT report bulletin](https://dot.report/bulletins/11031693))
    - 12V battery drain: owners report parasitic draws after aftermarket installs or failed modules; always ask for a recent battery health test and whether the car has had accessory work.
  - Practical checklist at inspection:
    - Boot and glovebox lids, seams around screen for condensation, camera clarity and reverse‑camera activation.
    - Confirm the vehicle has had Uconnect updates and request dealer printouts for any software flashes.

- Mechanically:
  - Brand engineering reputation: Jeep’s compact SUVs trade off some interior finesse for off‑road nous; that said, maintenance and early detection matter — the Compass has a mixed reliability reputation with frequent entries for engine, transmission and electrical systems. (RepairPal/WhatCar summarise this trend.) ([RepairPal problems list](https://repairpal.com/problems/jeep/compass); [WhatCar reliability](https://www.whatcar.com/jeep/compass/4x4/used-review/n19197/reliability))
  - Security & theft vulnerabilities:
    - Modern Compasses (and many cars) can be vulnerable to “CAN injection” attacks where thieves access external wiring (e.g., headlight connectors) to inject messages into the vehicle CAN bus and bypass immobilisers. This is industry‑wide, not just Jeep — but it’s real and getting common. Protect the car with physical measures (steering wheel lock, locking wheel nuts), garage parking where possible, and ask your insurer about approved immobiliser upgrades or tracking devices. ([CAN injection technical writeup](https://www.can-cia.org/fileadmin/cia/documents/publications/cnlm/june_2023/cnlm_23-2_p20_the_can_injection_attack_ken_tindel_canis_automotive_labs.pdf); [Autoblog theft coverage](https://www.autoblog.com/carbuying/vehicle-headlight-can-bus-injection-theft-method-update))
    - Check for past ignition/anti‑theft TSBs on the VIN. ([Aggregated TSBs](https://www.aboutautomobile.com/Technical-Service-Bulletin/2023/Jeep/Compass/Ignition-Anti-Theft))
  - Suggested aftermarket counter‑measures:
    - Install an engine‑immobiliser/kill switch on a concealed circuit.
    - Fit an active GPS tracker with geofence alerts.
    - Consider a simple CAN‑bus intrusion detector if you plan to keep a later model in high‑risk areas.

- Engines:
  - Below are italicised engine variant subsections. Read every single one; each has distinct failure modes.

  *2.0‑litre turbo (new‑generation STLA era — 2024+)*  
  - Known failure points: relatively new to the Compass range in Aus; early reports focus on software/calibration and turbo plumbing inspection after prolonged use. Long‑term reliability dataset is limited for the 2024–2026 models. Manufacturer docs list an 8‑speed automatic and AWD as the typical pairing. ([Stellantis fleet PDF](https://www.stellantisfleet.com/content/dam/fca-fleet/na/fleet/en_us/shopping-tools/brochures-literature/docs/buyers-guide/2026/26DOMMOP_FBG_Compass.pdf); [Edmunds 2025 specs](https://www.edmunds.com/jeep/compass/2025/features-specs/))
  - Preventative maintenance: stick to dealer‑recommended service intervals — expect regular oil and filter changes and watch for turbo oil feed lines at ~100,000 km inspection windows. Because this is a modern direct‑injection turbo, plan oil changes every 10,000–15,000 km with high‑quality synthetic oil; if you drive hard or tow, drop that to ~10,000 km.

  *2.4L Tigershark (MultiAir)*  
  - Known failure points:
    - Excessive oil consumption is well documented; some engines exhibit high oil burn by 30,000–80,000 km without external leaks. ([MotorReviewer 2.4L Tigershark problems](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=184); [Go‑Parts donor guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/parts-cars-trucks-ram-promaster-city-jeep-cherokee-jeep-compass-2013-2022))
    - Cam phaser/timing chain wear and intake/head gasket trouble in some cases — watch for misfires and oil pressure warnings.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil and filter every 10,000 km (or sooner if you do lots of short runs). If the car has over 80,000 km and a history of spotty oil checks, budget for compression and leak‑down checks.
    - Inspect PCV and intake‑system filters; a clogged PCV can exacerbate oil‑burn.
  - Practical test‑items at inspection:
    - Check oil level and condition; smell for fuel dilution.
    - Compression test or evidence of low compression misfires on at‑home test drives.

  *1.4L MultiAir turbo*  
  - Known failure points:
    - Variable valve module and turbo system complexity can lead to misfire codes, turbo lag complaints and earlier-than-expected component wear compared with a simple NA engine.
  - Preventative maintenance:
    - Oil and spark plugs at tighter intervals (10,000–15,000 km recommended depending on use), ensure turbo inlet and intercooler plumbing are free from oil residues.

- Transmission:
  - Mechanical behaviour and known failure modes vary by generation:
    - Older models (first‑gen & early second‑gen) using Jatco CVT (JF011E) suffered overheating, slippage and premature wear under stress — classic CVT symptoms: judder, overheating and loss of drive. If you see a CVT‑equipped Compass in the ad, consider walking away unless it has comprehensive service/repair records. ([Transmission CVT issues overview](https://transmissionanswers.com/jeep-compass-cvt-transmission-problems-causes-and-costs/); [cartipsdaily transmission recall article](https://cartipsdaily.com/jeep-compass-transmission-recall))
    - ZF 9‑speed (used in later models) has its own quirks — rough shifts at low speeds and software‑dependent behaviour; dealer TSBs and updates often fix shift logic. Aisin 6‑speed variants are more mechanically simple and tend to be robust.
    - Recent manufacturer communications and part‑return notices indicate torque converter/transmission assemblies for 2024–2026 Compasses have been under review at dealer parts level — check for open campaigns on every VIN. ([DOT/parts bulletin reference](https://dot.report/bulletins/11030259))
  - Practical checks for a test drive:
    - Cold start: watch for harsh engagement, clunks or shudder when moving off.
    - Acceleration: no slipping, hesitation or delayed downshifts.
    - Servicing: ask for transmission service receipts (fluid/filter changes) — many automatics need fluid service more often than owners realise.

- Current entry to ceiling pricing (NSW live snapshot): AUD $11,990 up to $61,481; expect the cheaper cars to be older first‑gen or high‑mileage units, and the top end to be near‑new STLA or limited‑edition models with options.

- Depreciation metrics:
  - Industry estimates peg Compass depreciation roughly in line with or slightly worse than the compact SUV segment:
    - iSeeCars: ~32% drop after 3 years for the Compass. ([iSeeCars resale value](https://www.iseecars.com/car/jeep-compass/resale-value))
    - CarEdge/CarDepreciation show 44% depreciation at five years as a reasonable guideline. ([CarEdge depreciation](https://caredge.com/jeep/compass/depreciation))
  - Reality check for buyers: the Compass doesn’t hold value like a Toyota RAV4 or Honda CR‑V; if you buy new, expect steeper depreciation in the first 3–5 years versus segment stalwarts.

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy and maths (how to decide petrol vs hybrid vs plug‑in):
  - TCO strategy: add purchase price + finance + insurance + maintenance/servicing + fuel + likely repairs + registration over your ownership horizon (3 or 5 years). For used buys, the repair and reliability premium matters more than the theoretical fuel economy gap.
  - Fuel example (worked method, not a hard prediction): take real combined consumption for a Compass 2.0 AWD at roughly 8–9 L/100 km (manufacturers’ combined figures for compact turbo AWDs sit in this band — see Edmunds/Stellantis specs for the modern 2.0 engine). Use your local fuel price to calculate annual running costs:
    - Annual km example: 15,000 km. At 8.5 L/100 km = 1,275 L/year. At $1.70/L that’s ~$2,168/year fuel. Swap in your actual fuel price to get real numbers. ([Edmunds 2025 specs reference](https://www.edmunds.com/jeep/compass/2025/features-specs/); [Stellantis 2026 buyers’ guide](https://www.stellantisfleet.com/content/dam/fca-fleet/na/fleet/en_us/shopping-tools/brochures-literature/docs/buyers-guide/2026/26DOMMOP_FBG_Compass.pdf))
  - Hybrid/PHEV premium vs savings:
    - Stellantis confirms e‑Hybrid and plug‑in options on the new Compass lineup; those reduce urban fuel bills but cost more up front. ([Stellantis PR on electrified options](https://media.stellantis.com/em-en/jeep/press/introducing-the-all-new-jeep-compass-redefining-adventure-with-distinctive-design-legendary-capability-versatility-and-cutting-edge-technology))
    - For a buyer of used stock (2013–2025), electrified Compass options are rare — the premium for a late‑model hybrid must be weighed against: your annual km, access to charging, likely battery warranty/health and long‑term servicing availability. If you do >20,000 km/year mostly urban, a PHEV or hybrid can be worth the premium. If your driving is mixed or long highway runs, the payback window lengthens dramatically.
  - TCO tip: when comparing a petrol Compass and a hybrid/PHEV, calculate total fuel + servicing + expected battery/pack replacement risk / warranty coverage over 5 years — hybrids often lower running costs but raise complexity and potential repair bills after warranty.

- Buying recommendation — blunt, practical:
  - If you want a cheap, compact Jeep with character and don’t mind a few quirks: the NSW market median of AUD $24,990 for a Compass (median odometer ~30,000 km) is a decent starting point to hunt for well‑spec 2017–2021 models with full service history.
  - Which model year/trim is the sweet spot?
    - Target: mid‑life second‑generation models (roughly 2018–2021 in NSW stock) with documented service history and preferably a **ZF/Aisin automatic** rather than early CVT units. These years have the balance of price, kit and incremental software fixes. Avoid early‑first‑generation CVT models and be cautious with cars showing Uconnect/ADAS unresolved faults.
    - If you want the newest tech and dealer support, buy a 2024–2025/2026 STLA‑based Compass only if you can confirm recent TSBs have been applied and ADAS systems are healthy; early adopters can face software teething issues.
  - Red flags to walk away on:
    - No service history or evidence of irregular oil changes on Tigershark engines.
    - CVT models with any sign of judder, slipping or hot‑drive warning history.
    - Persistent Uconnect screen freezes or repeated ADAS “unavailable” messages that the seller cannot show a dealer attempt to fix.
    - Aftermarket wiring or undocumented alarm/immobiliser installs (possible theft vector or hacking risk).
  - Practical bargaining points:
    - Use known faults as leverage: ask for a dealer‑issued printout showing completed TSB work (infotainment/ADAS), and demand an independent mechanical inspection focusing on oil consumption and transmission operation.
    - Factor in likely short‑term spend: budget AUD $1,000–$2,500 in the first 12 months for high‑probability items (tyres, minor suspension wear, and addressing any infotainment/12V battery issues you uncover).

- Final verdict — yes/no:
  - Yes — if: you want a compact SUV with genuine Jeep character, standard AWD on many trims, and you can secure a well‑serviced 2018–2021 example for roughly the NSW median AUD $24,990 (or cheaper if high‑mileage). You get good capability and kit for the money, and parts are reasonably available because of platform commonality with other Stellantis products.
  - No — if: you expect showroom polish, near‑bulletproof reliability, or a low‑maintenance daily fleet car with the cheapest lifetime running cost. The Compass can be more volatile on reliability and depreciation than Japanese rivals; if you want the most dependable residual or lowest long‑term headaches, look at a CR‑V or RAV4 instead.
  - Best single buy call: a 2018–2021 Compass in **Latitude/Limited** trim with a documented service history, non‑CVT automatic and clear ADAS/infotainment service records. That’s the sweet spot for kit vs reliability vs price in the NSW used market.
