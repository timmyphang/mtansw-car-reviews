#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning

The Mercedes-Benz EQE (internal chassis codes **V295** for the sedan, **V296** for the SUV) arrived in Australia in early 2023, representing the brand’s first dedicated high-volume electric saloon and crossover built specifically to bridge the gap between the entry-level EQA/EQB and the flagship EQS limousine. If you visualise the classic E-Class ladder, the EQE occupies that exact social and functional rung—except there’s no combustion heritage to lean on, just raw silicon and software. It shares the global **EVA2 (Electric Vehicle Architecture 2)** skateboard platform, meaning you get excellent parts interoperability with the EQS, upcoming EQG, and select AMG performance derivatives. This common DNA translates to widespread availability of suspension knuckles, thermal exchange modules, and high-voltage cabling across the Melbourne-Sydney-Brisbane dealer corridor.

In the Australian market, Mercedes opted for a tightly controlled, fully electric lineup. There are no hybrid or plug-in variants, just varying levels of motorisation and battery tuning. The entry point starts with the **EQE 300+**, stepping up to the **EQE 350+**, and culminating in the **EQE 500 4MATIC**. For those who want their luxury with a violent appetite, the **AMG EQE 53 4MATIC+** punches past 470 kW. All units utilise a single-motor rear-wheel-drive layout by default, with the 4MATIC badge denoting a twin-motor all-wheel-drive setup. Transmissions are fundamentally fixed-ratio single-speed reduction gears (architecturally functionally analogous to an eCVT), paired exclusively with an electronic automatic shift pattern. The explicit marketplace rivals you’ll be cross-shopping are the Tesla Model Y Performance/Long Range, BMW’s iX3 and i4 Gran Coupé, Audi’s Q4 e-tron and Q8 e-tron, and the incoming Polestar 3. As CarsGuide rightly notes, while the EQE “delivers a lot of the technology of the brand's EQS flagship EV limo counterpart”, the practicality debate for Australian families remains firmly attached to rear-seat legroom and boot volume [https://www.carsguide.com.au/mercedes-benz/eqe/vs/ssangyong-torres]. Newcomers like Lucid and Porsche add noise, but the EQE competes squarely in that $95k–$135k new showroom bracket where prestige matters as much as kilometre-per-kilowatt-hour efficiency.

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mercedes-Benz Eqe?

**Exterior**
Panel gaps on early 2023 build runs show minor inconsistency around the front clamshell and lower rocker skirts, usually a result of the adhesive-heavy EVA2 assembly rather than structural misalignment. Gloss black aero-acoustic wheel arch extensions and the flush-active door handle actuators scratch if you wash with cheap microfibres or park nose-first into shopping centre bumpers. Paint durability is generally solid—Mercedes’ basecoat/clearcoat chemistry holds up against Queensland UV and Sydney salt air—but watch the lower valance for stone chips that expose primer if the vehicle hasn’t had ceramic coating applied. Safety system (ADAS) behaviour revolves around the stereo camera and front radar cluster tucked behind the upper grille emblem. Early firmware struggles with lane-centering aggression on undulating Pacific Highway stretches, frequently triggering false ‘Lane Keeping Assist Unavailable’ warnings. Mercedes pushed multiple MBUX OTA updates targeting adaptive sensitivity thresholds, so verify your vehicle’s control module version before purchase; post-2023 software iterations significantly quieted the false-positive rate. Panel alignment consistency improves markedly after the mid-cycle refresh, but the pixel-light matrix arrays remain expensive to replace if road debris bypasses the aerodynamic splitter.

**Interior**
Rideshare and corporate fleet duty exposes two weak points: bolster compression on the Nappa leather seats within 40,000 km, and heat-related capacitive ghost-touching on the central display. The cabin is exceptionally quiet, utilising acoustic laminated glass and foam-filled tyres, but door sill heights are deliberately low, making ingress/egress effortless for elderly passengers or parents juggling prams—the check angles on the doors also provide generous opening arcs that don’t pinch coats. Electrical gremlins mostly manifest as 12V auxiliary battery drainage. The telematics unit, always-on digital key, and background OTA download protocols keep the CAN bus partially active; leave it parked for fourteen days without a trickle charger or Bluetooth sleep-mode activation, and you’ll wake to a dead small battery. Infotainment hardware splits by trim: lower grades get dual 10.25-inch floating screens, while mid and top-spec vehicles receive the controversial 56-inch Hyperscreen (a 12.8-inch angled centre console panel flanked by 12.3-inch driver and 9.3-inch passenger displays). Connectivity reliability hinges on the 5G modem and Wi-Fi hotspot; expect intermittent Spotify offline sync failures until the server cache is manually purged. As reviewers have pointed out, the interface packs immense functionality but leans heavily into nested sub-menus rather than intuitive shortcuts, which frustrates passengers navigating climate or seat massage routines [https://www.greencarguide.co.uk/car-reviews-and-road-tests/mercedes-benz-eqe-suv-review/]. Crash safety credentials are uncompromised, scoring full five stars across Euro NCAP testing protocols, but rear headroom in the sedan variant feels claustrophobic for adults over 185 cm [https://www.honestjohn.co.uk/mercedes-benz/eqe/].

**Mechanically**
Engineering reputation centres on plush isolation, precision steering weighting, and complex integration of drive-by-wire throttle mapping. Underneath that polish, Mercedes leverages a sophisticated thermal exchange manifold and predictive battery preconditioning that minimises degradation—if maintained correctly. Security-wise, BLUFORCE keyless entry and remote start convenience introduces a moderate CAN-bus injection risk. Pre-2023 Mercedes architectures were flagged by auto-theft taskforces for relay-style signal amplification targeting the UWB key fob handshake. Counter-measures are straightforward: store your key in a certified Faraday pouch overnight, fit a physical steering column lock (the obvious deterrent works 90% of opportunistic thefts), and consider an aftermarket OBD-II immobiliser that enforces a PIN-authenticated startup sequence before allowing the contactor to close. Do not rely solely on factory tracking; the Mercedes me app is brilliant when connected but vulnerable to SIM-swapping incidents if your account recovery is weak.

**Engines**
*EQE 300+* 
The 185 kW rear-mounted synchronous motor delivers a smooth, linear torque curve but sits closest to the thermal bottleneck. Early production cohorts experienced recurring **coolant bypass valve** sticking, which manifests as uneven cabin heating and intermittent range loss in sub-10°C environments. The primary failure node is the **low-voltage water pump** and associated **radiator fan** circuitry; if the fan refuses to cycle or grinds on engagement, the inverter thermal window closes aggressively, limiting charge acceptance [https://repairpal.com/symptom/mercedes-benz/eqe-500/radiator-fan-not-working]. Preventative maintenance interval falls strictly at 20,000 km or twelve months (whichever arrives first), focusing on cabin air filter replacement, brake fluid hygroscopy testing, and high-voltage insulation resistance verification. No engine oil changes exist.

*EQE 350+* 
Upsizing to 215 kW tightens the acceleration envelope but strains the **high-pressure cooling vent filter** located beneath the front bumper intake. Debris accumulation here forces the radiators to work harder, accelerating coolant breakdown and potentially triggering low-flow warnings. Service schedule remains biennial/20,000 km, but mandate an annual thermographic scan of the motor controller heatsinks to catch delamination before it cascades into inverter fault codes. Watch for **charge port locking actuator** wear; owners report slow ejection cycles after 35,000 km, requiring manual override jigs at independent workshops.

*EQE 500 4MATIC* 
Dual-motor configuration pushes output to 300 kW with symmetrical AWD bias. The added complexity introduces tandem inverter modules and a front axle disconnect clutch. Diagnostic databases consistently flag **battery management system (BMS) temperature sensor** drift and **charging relay contact pitting** as primary check-engine triggers. Ignoring these causes accelerated cell balancing inefficiencies and reduces usable range by up to 12% over eighteen months [https://www.mercedesdallas.com/2023-mercedes-benz-eqe-suv-check-engine-light.htm]. Maintenance requires brake caliper slide grease renewal at 30,000 km (regen-blending wears pads unevenly) and DC fast-charging protocol recalibration every 40,000 km to preserve lithium-ion crystal lattice integrity.

*AMG EQE 53 4MATIC+* 
Pushing 471 kW and 950 Nm, the AMG variant relies on track-tuned inverters and a sport differential programmed for aggressive torque vectoring. Failure points skew toward mechanical stress rather than electronics: **rear axle bearing preload** fatigue under sustained highway cruising, **regenerative braking modulation valve** hesitation when switching between one-pedal and friction braking, and excessive **cabin ventilation filter** loading from high-performance tire compound dust. Service intervals compress to 15,000 km for brake fluid and torque converter cooler flushing (even though it’s an EV, the AMG-specific hydraulic brake booster shares plumbing architecture with earlier ICE platforms). Expect faster tyre consumption and elevated insurance brackets; the hardware is bulletproof if driven legally, but the electronics demand dealer-level flashing after any aftermarket accessory installation.

**Transmission**
Every EQE variant utilises a **single-speed fixed-ratio reduction gear** housed alongside the electric motor(s). Functionally operating as a simplified eCVT, it eliminates traditional torque converters and dual-clutch friction plates, replacing them with a hardened steel pinion set lubricated by synthetic gear oil rated for 150,000 km lifespan. Known failure behaviours are uncommon but severe when they occur: **shunt connector corrosion** between the inverter pack and gearbox housing leads to intermittent phase-loss whining under load, usually triggered by floodwater intrusion or repeated deep puddle crossings. Regen calibration drift causes notchy deceleration pulses below 20 km/h, typically resolved via dealer reflash of the brake-control module. Differential gear whine emerges after 60,000 km in high-mileage examples, indicating insufficient lubrication film thickness from infrequent inspections. While technically sealed-for-life, independent specialist guidance recommends draining and inspecting gear oil every 60,000 km to catch microscopic copper wear particles before they degrade viscosity ratings.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mercedes-Benz Eqe?

Live NSW dealer inventory currently shows a single active snapshot priced at **AUD $66,800**, representing a **2023 build** sitting at **50,952 km** with an **automatic** transmission mix. That figure sets the realistic floor for compliant, unmodified specimens entering the secondary market. Ceiling prices for higher-optioned SUV variants or AMG editions hover between **$88,000–$105,000** depending on residual warranty coverage and Hyperscreen inclusion. You aren’t getting bargain-bin pricing here; luxury EV depreciation follows a steep initial curve before plateauing. Expect roughly **38–42% baseline value erosion over three years**, which tracks closely with BMW’s iX3 and Audi’s Q8 e-tron, though marginally steeper than Tesla’s retained values thanks to stricter service network dependency. The math favours patience: buying at year three unlocks the deepest discount while leaving ample useful life on the traction battery.

Total cost of ownership calculations must factor in Australia’s patchy public charging infrastructure versus home solar arbitrage. Using a conservative commercial electricity rate of **$0.35/kWh**, the EQE consumes approximately **18.5–21.0 kWh/100km** in mixed urban/highway driving. That translates to roughly **$6.50–$7.35 per 100 km**, versus **$14.00–$16.50** for a comparable 2.0-litre diesel or 3.0-litre petrol executive SUV running 14–16 L/100km. Over a standard 25,000 km annual commute, you’re looking at **$2,200–$2,800 annual fuel savings**. However, those savings vanish if you chase the 4MATIC premium or AMG branding. Insurance premiums spike by 18–22% on AWD and performance variants, and dealer labour rates ($320–$385/hr) combined with proprietary diagnostic tool subscriptions erode operational savings within twenty-four months. Paying extra for electric powertrain sophistication only pays off mathematically if you log high kilometres and possess home solar storage; otherwise, the conventional 350+ RWD variant captures 90% of the efficiency benefit at a fraction of the acquisition tax.

Buy a late-2023 or early-2024 EQE 350+ with confirmed MBUX firmware revision v23.x or newer, ideally under 65,000 km, and backed by remaining manufacturer EV battery assurance. Avoid launch-week stock unless the previous owner provides documented proof of all thermal management and HVAC software flashes. The optimal financial sweet spot lands squarely on the **non-AMG 350+ sedan or SUV**—it dials back unnecessary weight, simplifies the cooling topology, retains the core luxury experience, and depreciates at a healthier second-hand trajectory. If you insist on four-wheel drive, cap your spend at the 500 4MATIC and demand a comprehensive high-voltage insulation test report before handing over cash. Drive it smart, keep it plugged in, and let the engineers at Affalterbach worry about the rest.
