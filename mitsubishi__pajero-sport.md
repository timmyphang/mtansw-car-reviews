#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation: third-generation Pajero Sport (Phase II refresh, model years circa 2016–2024 in Australia; the line continues into 2025 with spec updates). It's the body‑on‑frame, ladder‑chassis Pajero Sport that shares underpinnings with Mitsubishi’s Triton ute. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitsubishi_Pajero_Sport) / Mitsubishi Australia spec page [mitsubishi‑motors.com.au](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.com.au/vehicles/pajero-sport.html).
- Internal codes: commonly referred to as “Pajero Sport (3rd gen) / Phase‑II” in public documentation — Mitsubishi’s local pages and brochure updates list it as the continuing Pajero Sport line used in Australia through the 2020s. See the official Mitsubishi Australia model page [mitsubishi-motors.com.au/vehicles/pajero-sport.html](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.com.au/vehicles/pajero-sport.html).
- Product hierarchy: sits above the compact Outlander in Mitsubishi’s SUV range and below full‑size ladder‑frame wagons (e.g., the heavy off‑road Patrol in the parts of the world that still sell both). The Pajero Sport is the brand’s mid‑size, seven‑seat ladder‑frame SUV offering — the family‑4x4/ute‑based alternative to mainstream unibody seven‑seat SUVs.
- Shared platform architecture: ladder‑frame chassis shared with the Mitsubishi Triton ute (Triton parts, driveline items and many suspension components are communally sourced) — this helps parts availability and aftermarket support in Australia where Triton spares are common.
- Powertrain line-up in Australia (market‑specific, recent years):
  - **2.4L MiVEC turbo‑diesel** four‑cylinder, common‑rail direct injection — rated ~133 kW and 430 Nm in recent spec sheets — paired with a **8‑speed automatic**. **Super Select II 4WD** selectable system on higher grades and strong towing capability (up to ~3.1 tonnes in Australian spec). [Mitsubishi Australia](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.com.au/vehicles/pajero-sport.html) / official spec sheet [mitsubishi-motors.co.za PDF](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/14-MAY-25-Pajero-Sport-Spec-Sheet-2025.pdf) / CarExpert summary for 2023 [carexpert.com.au](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/2023-mitsubishi-pajero-sport-price-and-specs).
  - Historically and in other markets you’ll also see older diesel variants (2.5L **4D56**) and petrol V6s (e.g., **6G72**), but these are much more common on older Pajero/Pajero‑Sport models or regional trims. See the engine guides and references on 4D56 and 6G72 [EngineCrux / Autoparts references](https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-4d56-engine-overview-specs-and-reliability/) and [motorreviewer (6G72)](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=204).
- Marketplace rivals: Toyota Fortuner, Isuzu MU‑X, Ford Everest, Nissan Pathfinder/Patrol in some spec fights, Chevrolet/GM Trailblazer, plus premium alternatives (Jeep Grand Cherokee L, Hyundai Palisade/Santa Fe in buyer crossovers). CarExpert and Drive comparisons list these rivals in Australian comparison pieces [drive.com.au](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/family-4x4-suv-comparison-ford-everest-v-isuzu-mu-x-v-mitsubishi-pajero-sport-v-toyota-fortuner-v-nissan-patrol/) / CarsGuide comparisons [carsguide.com.au](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero-sport/vs).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Mitsubishi Pajero Sport?
Below is the honest, mechanic‑level autopsy: what commonly fails, what to look for at inspection, and what you can fix in a weekend (or should never try).

- Exterior:
  - Panel fit and trim: Generally decent — it’s a ute‑based body so bonnet and front guards sit in familiar ladder‑frame tolerances. Expect stone chips on lower cladding, bumper scuffs and occasional paint fretting on towbar areas. Owners often report bumper scuffs from off‑road work and impact with tail‑gates when towing.
  - Trim vulnerabilities: gloss black/soft‑touch plastic surrounding screens and air vents can scratch and show sun‑fade on Victorian summer roofs; chrome bits age faster around roof rails and towbar mounts.
  - Paint durability & corrosion: no systemic Australian‑wide body corrosion epidemic, but the underbody and rear cross‑member around tow coupling are work‑hardened zones — check for surface rust and previous repairs if the vehicle has been used in coastal or salt‑affected areas.
  - Recalls & safety systems: always run a recall check on the VIN — Mitsubishi posts open recall tools and NHTSA has searchable recall info for imported examples [Mitsubishi owners’ open recalls](https://owners.mitsubishicars.com/s/services/open-recalls) / NHTSA [nhtsa.gov/recalls](https://www.nhtsa.gov/recalls). ADAS hardware (radar and camera) sits behind the windscreen and bumper: improper windscreen replacement or bumper repairs can knock out the Forward Collision Mitigation and adaptive cruise logic and often needs dealer recalibration. I‑CAR guidance notes ADAS sensors are sensitive to mis‑repair [I‑CAR Mitsubishi ADAS guidance](https://rts.i-car.com/oem-information/mitsubishi/mitsubishi-adas.html).

- Interior:
  - Wear & heavy use: the Pajero Sport is used as rideshare, tradie vehicle and family hauler — seat foam and trim show wear where kids or pets have been in and out. Leather on higher trims creases faster than you might like; base vinyls stand up better to grime.
  - Entry/egress and ergonomics: door sills are a bit higher than a station wagon (ladder‑frame ride height), so older knees appreciate a step or running boards. Door checks are robust but check for torn door seals, bent scuff plates or loose grab handles on high‑km examples.
  - Electrical behaviours: owners report occasional 12V battery drain/infotainment quirks — frozen screens, audio dropouts or radios rebooting, particularly on older head‑unit firmware. ProductReview and forum threads contain repeated notes of intermittent infotainment failures and worst‑case head‑unit replacements [ProductReview listings](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/mitsubishi-pajero-sport) / Team‑BHP owner threads [team-bhp.com](https://www.team-bhp.com/forum/long-term-ownership-reviews/156038-my-mitsubishi-pajero-sport-comprehensive-review-26.html).
  - Infotainment & connectivity: screen sizes and hardware differ by model year and trim — sluggish factory Android HUs have been reported, poor Bluetooth hands‑free call quality in some owner's accounts, and some owners successfully replaced or updated head units to fix behaviour. DIY reset/reseat procedures for a dead/blank screen are commonly documented on YouTube and forum threads [YouTube infotainment reset](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9eYLjfRJ5RM) / Team‑BHP [team-bhp link](https://www.team-bhp.com/forum/car-entertainment/304757-got-android-touchscreen-hu-my-pajero-but-its-too-slow-and-call-quality-rubbish-now-what.html).

- Mechanically:
  - Brand engineering reputation: Mitsubishi’s ladder‑frame SUVs are workmanlike — mechanically simple, tough under load and with a large parts pool because of Triton shared components. That said, every brand has wear items — check service history.
  - Security vulnerabilities: like many modern cars, keyless and CAN‑bus‑related theft techniques exist. Research shows thieves exploiting CAN‑bus and OBD wiring, and headlight/lighting wiring points have been used to inject messages and bypass immobilisers. This is not Pajero‑specific but applies to modern vehicles generally — take physical deterrents seriously. Sources: The Register on CAN bus attacks and MillerCAT mitigation advice [The Register CAN bus theft](https://www.theregister.com/security/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/) / MillerCAT owner guidance [millercat.com](https://millercat.com/blogs/news/how-to-protect-your-vehicle-against-can-bus-theft).
  - Suggested counter‑measures:
    - Fit a quality mechanical steering lock when parked (cheap and effective).
    - Use an OBD port blocker / tamper‑evident screw (thieves often connect to OBD to program devices).
    - For high‑risk areas, install an aftermarket immobiliser or CAN intrusion detection unit.
    - Keep keys in a Faraday pouch at home to prevent relay theft.
    - Check that any security gateway or immobiliser fault codes have been cleared by the dealer — MechanicWiki notes 2021+ Pajero Sport uses a Security Gateway requiring proper diagnostic tools [MechanicWiki Security Gateway note](https://www.mechanicwiki.com/2026/06/how-to-program-security-gateway.html).

- Engines:
  Per the research results there are several diesel and petrol engines associated with Pajero/Pajero‑Sport lines over the years. Each one below is given as an italicised subsection with known failure points and recommended service cadence guidance (pragmatic approach for Australian conditions: towing, hot temperatures, long highway runs).

  - *2.4L MiVEC turbo‑diesel (recent Australian models, common‑rail)*  
    - Known strengths: modern emissions control, better torque curve and economy than older 2.5 diesels; common in 2020s Pajero Sport Australian specification. **Key parts:** turbo, DPF/high‑pressure fuel pump, EGR system. [Mitsubishi official specs & CarExpert](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.com.au/vehicles/pajero-sport.html) / [carexpert.com.au](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/2023-mitsubishi-pajero-sport-price-and-specs).  
    - Typical failure points:
      - Turbocharger wear if oil changes are neglected.
      - DPF/EGR fouling on short‑trip urban use — watch for regen failures or power derate lights.
      - High‑pressure fuel pump/injector sensitivity to poor fuel quality.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil change at least every 10,000 km for towing/heavy use; for light private use 10–15,000 km is acceptable if using correct grade and OEM filter (follow Mitsubishi manual/service book).
      - Fuel‑filter and air‑filter replacement at 20–30,000 km under dusty conditions; for coastal/towing use, shorten interval.
      - DPF/EGR inspections >100,000 km or if regen faults appear.
      - Use high‑quality diesel and a reputable service to avoid injector/HFP problems.

  - *2.5L 4D56 diesel (older/other market variants)*  
    - Failure points: oil leaks, turbocharger wear, fuel delivery components and timing guide/chain wear on very high‑km units. See deep dives on the 4D56 reliability profile [EngineCrux / autopartswd 4D56 guides](https://enginecrux.com/mitsubishi-4d56-engine-overview-specs-and-reliability/) / [autopartswd](https://autopartswd.com/mitsubishi-4d56-engine-guide/).  
    - Preventative maintenance: oil and filter every 10,000–15,000 km (10k if towing), check turbo oil feed pipes and EGR systems; timing components should be inspected if symptoms of chain noise appear (owners report issues after 100–150k km if neglected).

  - *4M41 diesel (timing‑chain concerns referenced in service writeups for some Mitsubishi diesels)*  
    - Failure points: timing‑chain guide wear leading to catastrophic failure if ignored — technical shops advise inspection and proactivity [Andrews High Tech timing chain note](https://andrewshightech.com.au/mitsubishi-timing-chain-failure/).  
    - Preventative maintenance: listen for rattle at cold start, have timing guides inspected if high‑mileage (100k+), maintain oil quality to reduce wear.

  - *3.0L 6G72 V6 petrol (older petrol variants on Pajero family)*  
    - Failure points: oil leaks, coolant issues, timing belt/valve train wear on long‑run units [motorreviewer 6G72 summary](https://www.motorreviewer.com/engine.php?engine_id=204).  
    - Preventative maintenance: follow belt replacement intervals in the manual (earlier petrols need belt swaps by schedule), coolant system checks, regular oil changes every 10–15k km.

  Note: Australian Pajero Sport buyers in the last 5 model years will overwhelmingly encounter the 2.4L MiVEC diesel + 8‑speed auto combo — it’s the nucleus to focus on, particularly for towing ownership.

- Transmission:
  - Modern local spec (2020s onward) uses an **8‑speed automatic torque‑converter** transmission — smooth ratios and better gearing for towing and economy. Official specs list the 8‑speed coupling with the 2.4 diesel in Australian lineups [Mitsubishi spec sheet](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.co.za/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/14-MAY-25-Pajero-Sport-Spec-Sheet-2025.pdf) / [Mitsubishi Australia](https://www.mitsubishi-motors.com.au/vehicles/pajero-sport.html).
  - Known failure modes and owner complaints:
    - Lock‑up shudder or surging (torque‑converter behaviour) reported by owners in some threads — often cured by fluid and filter service or software update but can escalate to converter replacement if ignored. ProductReview and owner forums document torque‑converter‑type shudder symptoms [ProductReview threads / forum reports](https://www.productreview.com.au/listings/mitsubishi-pajero-sport/q-and-a).
    - Electronic shifting glitches: some owners reported odd up/downshifts that the dealer resolved via software/ECU updates — always check for outstanding TSBs or reprogramming history.
    - For older Pajero models, gearbox types vary (earlier 6‑speed autos or even CVTs in other Mitsubishi models) — the key is to verify service history and ensure transmission fluid has been changed at recommended intervals. CarsGuide maintains a page on transmission issues for the Pajero Sport [CarsGuide transmissions hub](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero-sport/problems/transmission).

#### 3. Should you buy a used Mitsubishi Pajero Sport?
- NSW dealer market snapshot (live data provided):  
  - Active listings: 24  
  - Price range: AUD $0 – $52,490  
  - Median price: AUD $39,939  
  - Year range: 2016 – 2024  
  - Median odometer: 61,891 km  
  - Transmission mix: Automatic: 24 (all autos)  
  Use the above as your starting search window — there are bargains if a unit has a reason for the low asking price (salvage, repo, or dealer special).

- Current market entry and ceiling price: based on the NSW snapshot, expect to pay from low‑$30k for older, higher‑km GLX/GLS models through to low‑$50k for a near‑new Exceed/GSR with low kilometres. CarsGuide and carsales price guides for 2024 show new‑car RRP bands into the $39–60k territory depending on trim, which aligns with used pricing behavior [carsguide 2024 pricing](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero-sport/2024) / [carsales research](https://www.carsales.com.au/research/mitsubishi/pajero-sport/2024/).
- Depreciation metrics:
  - Mitsubishi as a brand often depreciates around the industry average — CarEdge gives a brand depreciation guide noting Mitsubishi vehicles can lose ~50% value in five years as a broad headline [CarEdge Mitsubishi depreciation](https://caredge.com/mitsubishi/depreciation). For the Pajero Sport specifically, expect a meaningful 3‑year drop compared with segment heavyweights (Toyota Fortuner holds value slightly better historically). Bottom line: the Pajero Sport depreciates faster than the Toyota Fortuner but not catastrophically — it’s a reasonable value if you buy used after the initial owner has taken the biggest hit.
- TCO strategy (how to calculate and what the numbers mean)
  - Essential cost buckets to build your ownership model:
    - Depreciation (biggest single line item for a used vehicle unless you buy nearly new).
    - Fuel: use real‑world consumption benchmarks from owners (Fuelly aggregates show widely varying real‑world economy depending on load; expect urban/highway combined of roughly 8–11 L/100 km for the 2.4 diesel depending on load and driving style) [Fuelly Pajero Sport data](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/pajero_sport) / CarsGuide fuel pages [carsguide fuel page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero-sport/fuel-consumption).
    - Servicing & consumables: brakes, tyres, DPF services and periodic diesel servicing. Use the Mitsubishi owner manual and dealer service schedule as baseline (follow manufacturer schedule — in Australia lean to 10,000 km intervals for heavy/towing use). [Mitsubishi manual](https://www.manualslib.com/manual/2195082/Mitsubishi-Motors-Pajero-Sport.html).
    - Insurance, rego and unexpected repairs (engine, turbo, transmission).
  - Example decision method (quick, practical):
    - Pick your expected average km per year (e.g., 15,000–25,000 km for a tow/holiday family).
    - Use an expected real‑world fuel number (say 9 L/100 km for a mixed driving diesel Pajero Sport, adjusted from Fuelly samples).
    - Multiply litres/year = km/year × L/100km. Multiply by your local diesel price to get annual fuel spend.
    - Add annual service estimate (for a diesel ladder‑frame, budget $700–$1,200/year depending on dealer vs independent).
    - Add a running reserve for consumables & potential DPF/EGR work every 3–5 years.
    - Compare this total to the extra premium required for a Fortuner/Isuzu or a hybrid alternative (for this segment, hybrids are rare; paying up for a hybrid generally only pays off if you do extreme urban stop/start driving and the hybrid is offered as a factory variant).
  - Hybrid/EV premium question: the Pajero Sport’s segment in Australia has limited hybrid or EV variants — most direct rivals remain diesel ladder‑frame wagons. If you’re tempted by hybrid tech in the broader seven‑seat market (like Hyundai/Kia hybrids), the math only favors the premium if you do very high urban stop/start km and can capture fuel savings and low maintenance. For the Pajero Sport specifically, buying the efficient modern 2.4 diesel and maintaining it sensibly is usually the better numeric call for towing/four‑wheel‑driving buyers (diesel torque and towing figures are the money‑makers). Fuel and maintenance data aggregated from owners suggests diesel long‑term economy remains the practical winner in this class [Fuelly](https://www.fuelly.com/car/mitsubishi/pajero_sport) / CarsGuide [carsguide fuel page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/mitsubishi/pajero-sport/fuel-consumption).

- Final recommendation — buy or walk?
  - Buy if:
    - You need a seven‑seat ladder‑frame SUV with genuine towing chops (3.1t nominal) and you value mechanical simplicity and common parts. The Pajero Sport is one of the cheaper, well‑equipped entries in this pocket of the market.
    - You find a clean, fully‑serviced 2019–2022 model with the **2.4L MiVEC diesel + 8‑speed auto**, verified service records, recent DPF/DPF regen history and no ADAS/calibration flags. These model years have matured pricing, good parts availability and the newer powertrain benefits.
  - Walk (or negotiate hard) if:
    - The car shows signs of neglected DPF/EGR care (constant regen or limp mode). DPF replacements or injector/high‑pressure fuel pump jobs are expensive.
    - There’s a history of gearbox software issues without proof of dealer updates — insist on a test drive to feel for shudder/jerk and ask the dealer for any TSB/ECU reflash evidence.
    - ADAS faults are present (FCM/ACC errors) with no evidence of correct recalibration after windscreen or bumper repairs.
  - Best financial sweet spot: target a mid‑range **GLS/Exceed** from about 2019–2022 if you can find one with ~50–120k km and a full service history. These have the useful equipment without the dealer‑new premium and still carry modern safety and drivetrain hardware. NSW median price ($39,939) suggests a realistic buying sweet spot around late‑2018 to 2021 units with ~60k km for good value — you’ll avoid the steepest early depreciation and still get contemporary drivability. (Use the NSW market snapshot numbers above as your negotiation anchors.)

Short, blunt summary for the test drive: insist on a clean service ledger, a test that includes highway pull (look for torque converter shudder), screen/ADAS clear of warnings, and a VIN recall check. If that lines up, you’re getting one of the most stubbornly useful ladder‑frame seven‑seaters on the market — not the prettiest, but very capable and cheaper to buy used than a Fortuner. If it’s been neglected? Walk away — diesel systems and modern turbos bite deep into your wallet if ignored.
