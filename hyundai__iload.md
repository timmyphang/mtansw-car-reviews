#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generation / codes / production window  
  The vehicle under scrutiny is the Hyundai iLoad (the cargo variant of Hyundai’s Starex/H‑1 family). The iLoad badge in Australia covers the TQ-series/“Starex” derivative that’s been sold here from 2008 through to recent refreshes — the listings in the NSW dealer snapshot are predominantly late‑generation 2018–2021 builds. Globally you’ll also see the same basic van sold as the H‑1, Starex, i800 and sometimes as the iMax in passenger form — that common lineage is useful when sourcing parts and workshop knowledge ([Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/?redirect=no&title=Hyundai_iLoad)).

- Position in the Hyundai line-up  
  The iLoad sits as Hyundai’s mid‑size light commercial cargo van offering — below larger commercial trucks and above smaller city vans. The passenger equivalents (iMax / i800 / iLoad’s people-mover cousins) sit above it in terms of creature comforts but the iLoad is all-business.

- Shared platform / other models on same foundation  
  Under the skin the iLoad is closely related to the Starex/H‑1 family and shares mechanicals with Hyundai’s iMax / i800 passenger variants. That means parts and workshop knowledge often cross over from passenger-car dealerships and independent van specialists — an advantage for parts availability and servicing. Official Hyundai specification and brochure PDFs give the mechanical basics and show the single primary engine choice sold here: the 2.5‑litre CRDi turbo‑diesel ([Hyundai iLoad Specifications Sheet PDF](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/models/iload/docs/Hyundai_iLoad_Specifications_Sheet.pdf)).

- Market-specific powertrain list (Australia)  
  - Engine: 2.5‑litre turbo‑diesel CRDi — the D4CB family (2,497 cc). Different tunes reported across years (roughly 100–125 kW / 343 Nm depending on market and tune — see [Zigwheels](https://www.zigwheels.com.au/new-cars/hyundai/i-load/specifications) and Hyundai spec PDF).  
  - Transmission: 6‑speed manual (where fitted) and 5‑ or 6‑speed torque‑converter automatic boxes depending on year and market spec ([Hyundai spec PDF](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/models/iload/docs/Hyundai_iLoad_Specifications_Sheet.pdf)).  
  - Drive: Rear‑wheel drive with heavy‑duty leaf rear suspension and MacPherson front struts (book specs within the brochure PDF).  
  Fuel consumption examples from Hyundai: combined 7.6 L/100km (manual) and 8.8 L/100km (automatic) for the CRDi 2.5 ([Hyundai spec PDF](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/models/iload/docs/Hyundai_iLoad_Specifications_Sheet.pdf); CarsGuide also collates similar real‑world figures: [CarsGuide fuel consumption](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/iload/fuel-consumption)).

- Marketplace rivals  
  The iLoad sits in the busy mid‑size van segment. Direct rivals are Toyota HiAce, Ford Transit Custom, Renault Trafic, Volkswagen Transporter, Fiat Scudo / Talento and Mercedes‑Benz Vito. Market share context: HiAce dominates the segment in Australia while the iLoad holds a solid slice — CarsGuide’s market notes are useful reading ([CarsGuide comparison](https://www.carsguide.com.au/tradies/ford-transit-custom-hyundai-iload-toyota-hiace-lwb-80531), [CarsGuide vs HiAce](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/iload/vs/toyota-hiace)).

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Hyundai iLoad?

Exterior
- Panel alignment & paint: The iLoad is built tough for tradie work, but cosmetic issues show up on used examples. Owners report clearcoat bubbling and delamination across some Hyundai lines — and the fallout around paint quality has been widely covered ([Drive paint-peeling coverage](https://www.drive.com.au/news/hyundai-owners-angry-over-paint-peeling-problem/)). Hyundai has since issued manufacturer bulletins and warranty extensions in some markets for affected paint types (see referenced NHTSA technical bulletin regarding warranty extension for paint issues).
- Trim vulnerabilities: Expect scuffs and ripped lower door trims on tradie vans. Gloss black plastics and rub strips on higher‑spec passenger cousins scratch easily; replacement parts are cheap but colour‑match can be a pain on older faded paint.
- Recalls of note: A specific Australian recall covered iLoad/iMax models (2016–2018 builds) where the outer sheath of a cable between the fuse box and alternator can deteriorate and expose wiring — fire risk. Hyundai recalled roughly 9,826 vehicles in that campaign ([VehicleRecalls.gov.au REC‑006247](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-006247); coverage at [CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/hyundai-iload-imax-recalled-due-to-fire-risk)). If you’re looking at a 2016–2018 iLoad, check VIN against Hyundai’s recall database ([Hyundai Australia recalls page](https://www.hyundai.com/au/en/owning/safety-recalls)).

Interior
- Durability under tradie/rideshare duty: The iLoad’s cabin is derived from Hyundai passenger cars and is generally robust, but expect hard‑use wear: upholstery fray, ripped seat seams, worn switchgear and cheaper plastics showing stress at door‑handles and storage cubbies. HonestJohn and other long‑term reviews note it’s “carlike” but not invincible ([HonestJohn iLoad review](https://vans.honestjohn.co.uk/hyundai/iload/)).
- Ergonomics / entry & exit: Door sills sit higher than a hatchback but lower than a larger truck — reasonable for daily loading/unloading. Pay attention to door check arms and hinges; sagging doors are common after a heavy life.
- Electrical / infotainment:  
  - Infotainment quirks: Hyundai infotainment screens have a documented history of freezes, blank screens and random reboots across a range of late‑model Hyundais (and Kia siblings). Owners report needing resets or dealer re‑flashes; DIY resets sometimes work but persistent faults can mean replacing the head‑unit ([Hyundai forum threads](https://www.hyundai-forums.com/threads/infotainment-screen-freeze.666964/), Go‑Parts docs on 2020–2025 blank screens: [Go‑Parts guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/infotainment-display-kia-ev6-hyundai-elantra-hyundai-kona-2020-2025)).  
  - Connectivity & wireless charging: If the van has wireless charging, it’s finicky about phone placement and software control — check the owner manual for correct use and fuse locations ([Hyundai owners manual wireless charging page](https://ownersmanual.hyundai.com/docview/webhelp/Hyundai/0e169f84-6c7e-4286-a068-f3dbfd5bbb12/id5598fa5cc62.html)).  
  - 12V battery drains: Vans used as fitout power supplies or with aftermarket lighting/alarms can develop parasitic drains. Check for aftermarket wiring done through the cabin loom; a quick test with a multimeter will reveal if a persistent 12V draw exists after “sleep” periods.
- Practical checks at inspection: operate every switch, test window and door locks multiple times, warm the van and check blower noise and A/C performance. On tradie vans, the wiring under the floor and behind panels commonly gets dodgy tap‑ins — verify any additions were professionally crimped and fused.

Mechanically
- Brand engineering reputation: Hyundai’s commercial van has been a workhorse. The iLoad is built around a durable rear‑drive, ladder‑frame style layout with heavy leaf springs and a torque‑biased drivetrain — engineered for cargo hauling more than plush comfort. Servicing is widely supported across Hyundai dealerships as well as independent diesel specialists.
- Security vulnerabilities: Like many modern vehicles, Hyundai product lines have been targeted by “relay” and CAN‑injection style attacks when physical access is possible. The broader auto industry has documented techniques where attackers access lighting connectors and inject ‘key present’ messages onto the CAN bus. Hyundai offers an anti‑theft software upgrade for many models; owners should check eligibility and install such dealer updates ([Hyundai anti‑theft upgrade site](https://hyundaiantitheft.com/)). Technical write‑ups on CAN injection illustrate the risk and entry vectors (Ken Tindell / Canis Automotive Labs and commentary by security writers — see [Ken Tindell CAN Injection article](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/), and general background from Schneier’s blog).
- Recommended anti‑theft countermeasures: physical steering lock, hard‑wired GPS tracker with immobiliser, dealer anti‑theft software updates, sealing headlamp access and fitting an in‑line OBD2 lock or removing the OBD2 plug when unattended if practical. These combined measures mitigate both opportunistic and targeted attacks.

Engines
- Overview: In Australia the iLoad is overwhelmingly the 2.5‑litre diesel. Owner and workshop reports repeatedly point to diesel‑specific wear items: turbocharger wear, injector seat leaks and timing chain/tensioner concerns on high‑mileage units. See deep dives on the D4CB engine family for specifics ([AutopartsWD D4CB guide](https://autopartswd.com/d4cb-engine-guide/), [EngineExplained D4CB summary](https://www.engineexplained.com/hyundai-d4cb-2-5-l-engine-review/)).

- *2.5 D4CB CRDi (2,497 cc)*  
  - Common failure points:  
    - Turbocharger wear / VGT sticking / oil‑related turbo failure — workshops report complete turbo failures becoming common around ~100,000 km on neglected or oil‑contaminated examples ([TurbochargersPlus Hyundai iLoad turbo report](https://www.turbochargersplus.com/hyundai-iload-imax-turbocharger-problems-and-solutions/)).  
    - Injectors & injector seat washers leaking (diesel seepage into coolant or combustion‑chamber seat wear) — some workshops recommend periodic replacement of injector seat washers around the 100,000 km mark to prevent leaks ([TurboPros recommendations](https://turbopros.com.au/blogs/news/hyundai-iload-imax-common-problems-and-solutions), GrantWalker service notes).  
    - Timing‑chain tensioner and guide wear — not as catastrophic as interference petrol engines, but tensioner failure will cause premature wear and noisy running; inspection at high km recommended ([GrantWalker D4CB problems](https://grantwalker.com.au/hyundai-d4cb-engine-problems/)).  
  - Preventative maintenance intervals:  
    - Oil and filter: 10,000 km servicing is conservative and recommended for heavily loaded fleet use; many owner manuals specify 10,000–15,000 km depending on driving conditions — err to the shorter interval for tradie work. Hyundai’s service literature and local dealers typically schedule diesel services at 10,000–15,000 km depending on variant — check the brochure/spec sheet and your service book ([Hyundai iLoad brochure/specs PDF](https://www.hyundai.com/content/dam/hyundai/au/en/documents/Hyundai_iLoad_Brochure.pdf)).  
    - Turbo health: use manufacturer‑grade oil and change at 10,000 km intervals if the van sees short trips or dusty conditions. Have the turbo checked if you detect decreased top‑end power, slow boost spooling, or excess oil consumption.  
    - Fuel system: replace fuel filter per Hyundai schedule (often 20,000–30,000 km for diesels in harsh conditions) and consider an injector inspection / cleaning around 100,000 km on higher‑use vans.  
  - What to check at inspection: hold the engine at idle and listen for rattles from the timing chain/tensioner; on the road look for turbo lag, black smoke on hard acceleration (indicative of fueling or turbo issues), and erratic idle or misfire which could point to injectors.

Transmission
- Manual: generally robust if clutch and gearbox serviced sensibly. Van manuals often last longer in heavy‑load usage than cheap city cars, but expect clutch replacements on high‑use vans (~100–200k km depending on duty).
- Automatic (torque converter): the iLoad uses torque‑converter automatics in many model years. Known issues reported by owners include harsh shifts, the transmission “stuck” in 2nd or 3rd gear or elevated revs with little drive — these symptoms sometimes trace back to torque converter shudder, valve body wear or TCM/software calibration rather than catastrophic internals ([CarsGuide transmission problem page](https://www.carsguide.com.au/hyundai/iload/problems/transmission), Go‑Parts torque converter shudder guidance).  
  - Failure modes & diagnostics: transmission shudder on light throttle, slip under load, delayed engagement or limp‑home behaviour are red flags. Fluid change and filter (where serviceable) can help; persistent issues often require a specialist diagnosis and may lead to costly rebuilds if neglected. Check service history for regular ATF changes especially if the van has been used towing or for heavy loads.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Hyundai iLoad?

- NSW dealer market snapshot (live):  
  - Active listings: 2  
  - Price range: AUD $24,950 – $29,950  
  - Median price: AUD $27,450  
  - Year range: 2018 – 2021  
  - Median odometer: 74,032 km  
  - Transmission mix: Automatic: 2  
  Those are the local moment‑in‑time numbers supplied by the MTA NSW scraper — use them as a negotiating baseline when you go quoting numbers to dealers or owners.

- Typical market asking & guide prices context  
  Redbook lists a 2021 iLoad guide range of A$22,700–A$33,450 which aligns with the NSW dealer spread and suggests the median is roughly mid‑range for condition and kilometres ([Redbook 2021 iLoad listing](https://www.redbook.com.au/cars/details/2021-hyundai-iload-auto-my21/SPOT-ITM-559779/)). Carsales and other marketplace sites show 2018‑2019 asking examples in the mid‑$20k–mid‑$30k area — so the NSW figures are on the money ([carsales iLoad listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/hyundai/iload/)).

- Depreciation metrics (how fast does it fall off a cliff?)  
  Vans depreciate differently to passenger cars: cash‑buyers and fleets are sensitive to reliability and fitout, and Toyota HiAce historically holds value best in Australia. Expect the iLoad to lose significant value in the first three years — rough market evidence suggests 30–40% drop from new list prices over three years is common for this class, with HiAce typically better‑holding and European vans varying by spec and demand. For example, used asking prices for late‑2010s iLoads around A$29–36k versus older new price positioning indicate that the iLoad’s drop is typical of mid‑size vans; precise percentages depend on original trim, mileage and service history (see Redbook and Carsales price trend pages for ranges and distributions: [CarValue price stats](https://carvalue.app/Hyundai/iload/all/), [carsales listings](https://www.carsales.com.au/cars/hyundai/iload/)).

- Total cost of ownership (TCO) strategy & analysis  
  How to run the numbers for your situation: TCO for a tradie van must include purchase price, fuel, servicing, tyres, registration/insurances, expected downtime and major service items like turbo or transmission work. Hyundai’s factory consumption figures are a starting point: 7.6 L/100km (manual) and 8.8 L/100km (auto) for the 2.5 CRDi (Hyundai spec sheet). Use these figures and your annual km to model fuel spend. ChooseMyCar and Drive reviews provide running cost commentary and real‑world expectations ([ChooseMyCar iLoad review](https://choosemycar.com/resources/car-reviews/hyundai/iload), [Drive showroom page](https://www.drive.com.au/showrooms/hyundai/iload/)).  
  - Hybrid/EV alternative maths: There is currently no direct hybrid version of the iLoad in Australia and electrified medium‑duty vans tend to carry a significant premium (and in many cases limited payload or range compromises) compared with diesel equivalents. KBB and Edmunds methodology on hybrid payback shows the break‑even depends wholly on fuel prices, annual distance and repair/depreciation differences ([KBB hybrid break‑even methodology](https://www.kbb.com/car-advice/hybrid-vs-gas-break-even-fuel-savings/), [Edmunds hybrid ownership costs](https://www.edmunds.com/fuel-economy/the-real-costs-of-owning-a-hybrid.html)). Bottom line: for most tradies doing high annual kilometres the fuel savings from a specialist electric van can make sense — but that requires a higher upfront spend, owner investment in charging infrastructure, and confidence in residuals. For standard iLoad buyers, diesel remains the simplest low‑capex working solution.
  - Practical TCO tips: insist on a full service history (Hyundai dealer stamps are worth a few thousand dollars in resale), budget for a turbo refresh and injector attention around 120k km if the van has seen hard use, and assume an AT fluid service every 80–100k km for automatic examples used in heavy duty.

- Real-world buying recommendation (who should buy one and which year/trim)  
  Buy a used Hyundai iLoad if you need: a simple, rear‑drive work van with a common diesel drivetrain, easy payload packaging and straightforward servicing. Avoid if you want the latest ADAS safety aids, best long‑term resale, or a luxury cabin. Specific recommendations:
  - Best financial sweet spot: late‑2018 to 2020 examples with full dealer service history and one owner. The NSW median listing (2018–2021 median odometer 74k km, median price A$27,450) reflects a realistic buy price for a tidy example. Cross‑check the VIN for the alternator‑cable recall (2016–2018 affected lots) before purchase ([recall detail](https://www.vehiclerecalls.gov.au/recalls/rec-006247)).  
  - Prefer automatic if your work is often urban and you want lower driver fatigue; prefer manual if you want slightly better fuel economy and cheaper long‑term gearbox maintenance. Note the NSW snapshot shows automatics in the current listings — make sure transmission history is clean and that shifting is crisp.  
  - If you need rock‑solid resale and dealer support, prioritize a HiAce when your budget allows — but if value and upfront cost are king, the iLoad is very competitive (see market comparisons at [CarsGuide](https://www.carsguide.com.au/tradies/ford-transit-custom-hyundai-iload-toyota-hiace-lwb-80531)).

- Final verdict (short & blunt):  
  The Hyundai iLoad is a pragmatic, no‑nonsense tradie van. Buy one if you value payload, low sticker price and widely available parts/service knowledge. Don’t buy one expecting modern safety tech or premium finishes — and inspect carefully for turbo, injector and electrical issues on higher‑kilometre vans. For most buyers, the late‑2018 to 2020 range‑topping value examples with a tidy service book (the NSW median A$27,450 is a realistic negotiating start) are the sweet spot.
