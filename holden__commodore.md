#### 1. Introduction & Market Positioning
- Generations covered (short map)
  - VE (GM Zeta platform) — 2006–2013 — the reboot that went big and heavy. [Wikipedia: VE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE))
  - VF — 2013–2017 — facelifted, last locally built Commodore range. [Wikipedia: VF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VF))
  - ZB — 2018–2020 — the rebadged, imported Commodore (badge-engineered Opel/Vauxhall line), FWD/AWD architecture. [Wikipedia: Commodore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore)

- Product hierarchy
  - Below: smaller Commodore-derivative/entry models and mid‑spec Berlina; above: Calais luxury derivatives and HSV performance family (higher-spec SS, Clubsport, HSV GTS). VE/VF also spawned dedicated luxury and performance trims — the Calais and HSV ranges sit above regular Berlina/SS grade lines. [Wikipedia: VE/VF trim overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE)), [VF trim summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VF))

- Platform & parts commonality
  - VE/VF sit on the GM **Zeta** rear‑wheel‑drive platform — homegrown by Holden. That platform also underpinned the Holden Ute derivatives and even exported designs (Chevrolet Camaro is a Zeta descendant). Platform familiarity boosts local parts availability and independent specialist support. [GM Zeta platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Motors_Zeta_platform), [GM Authority Zeta notes](https://gmauthority.com/blog/gm/gm-platforms/zeta/)

- Market-specific engines, transmissions and drivetrains (high‑level)
  - Typical Australian line-up across VE/VF/ZB eras:
    - **V6** options: smaller V6s and the later **3.6L LFX V6** (common in VF/ZB), plus earlier **Alloytec V6** families in prior series. [VE specs summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE)), [LFX timing issue coverage](https://revhigh.com.au/post/holden-vf-commodore-3-6l-lfx-timing-chain-problems-what-every-owner-needs-to-know)
    - **V8** options: **6.0L/6.2L V8** in mainstream SS/HSV derivatives; HSV performance engines up to **7.0L** in some track-focused cars. [VE engine list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE))
    - Transmission types: 4/5/6‑speed torque‑converter automatics (4L60‑E, 5L40‑E, **6L50 / 6L80**), and manuals (Aisin **AY6**, **Tremec T56** on V8/HSV cars). [VE transmission list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VE))
    - Drive layouts: RWD for VE/VF (the classic layout), ZB moved to FWD/AWD in the imported phase. [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore)

- Marketplace rivals (how buyers compared them when new and in the used market now)
  - Ford Falcon (historic domestic rival), Toyota Camry, Subaru Liberty (Outback/Legacy family), Ford Mondeo — plus, in modern comparisons, mid-size crossovers like Genesis GV80 considered by some shoppers as alternatives. [Drive comparison](https://www.drive.com.au/reviews/holden-commodore-rs-v-ford-mondeo-trend-v-toyota-camry-sx-v-subaru-liberty-2-5i-premium-comparison-test/), [CarsGuide comparisons](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/commodore/vs)

#### 2. What goes wrong with a used Holden Commodore?
A brutal, practical field-guide to the faults you’ll meet in real world Commodores across VE/VF/ZB examples.

- Exterior
  - Panel alignment & history
    - Expect dodgy panel gaps on anything that’s had a body-shop touch-up. Just Commodores threads are full of owners finding door hinge wear and mis-aligned panels after repairs; check for consistent gap widths and paint overspray. [Just Commodores — panel gaps](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/panel-gaps-and-hinges-on-doors-how-much-wiggle-room-for-adjustment.280245/)
  - Paint & clearcoat
    - Clearcoat flaking/fading is a recurring complaint — bonnet and sun‑exposed surfaces are the usual culprits. Don’t be fooled by a shiny polish; tap paint depths and check for peeling clear. [Just Commodores — clearcoat thread](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/clear-coat-flaked-and-paint-faded.304960/)
  - Rust
    - Rust appears where paint chips meet trapped moisture in seams/drain channels. Older VE cars in salty coastal or winter conditions will show it; speak to AutoGuru about common corrosion points and repair costs. [AutoGuru rust guide](https://www.autoguru.com.au/repairs/rust-repair/holden/commodore)
  - Trim vulnerabilities
    - Gloss black and piano‑black trim scratch easily; dash tops and upper plastics suffer UV crazing and cracking on sun‑baked cars. [Go‑Parts dash panel note](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/dashboard-panel-holden-commodore-pontiac-g8-2006-2009)
  - ADAS & safety system quirks
    - VE/VF-era stability/ABS faults crop up (sensor failures or wiring gremlins) and can trip multiple systems (ABS, traction control, cruise). There are forum threads where ‘stability fault’ cascades into other system warnings — an intermittent sensor or wiring fault can be expensive if it needs multiple sensor replacements. [Just Commodores stability discussion](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/stability-fault-causing-abs-and-cruise-control-issues.302808/)
    - For the ZB (imported) models, check for any open recalls — AustralianCar.Reviews lists ZB recall summaries; run the VIN. [ZB recalls summary](https://australiancar.reviews/recalls-faults-holden-zb-commodore-2018-20/)

- Interior
  - Wear & longevity
    - Interior materials on mid‑spec models age like a sunburnt foreman — faded plastics, cracked dash sections, sagging headliners and worn bolsters are common on high‑km cars. AustralianHolden’s restoration guide documents that most interior failures are UV/age-related rather than single catastrophic defects. [Interior restoration guide](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/07/ve-vf-commodore-interior-restoration-guide/)
  - Space & ingress
    - Commodores are generous with entry/exit compared to smaller family sedans — broad door openings and typical full‑size sedan sill heights make getting in/out easy. Door checks can go stiff on older cars; inspect for scratching on lower sills that hides corrosion risk.
  - Electrical behaviour & infotainment
    - **MyLink** infotainment in VF examples shows an ageing pattern: Bluetooth drops, touchscreen lag, freezing and complete unit failures are reported frequently on owner forums. Some units can be revived with software resets but many end up requiring hardware replacement. [VF MyLink problems](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/07/vf-commodore-mylink-problems/), [Just Commodores MyLink threads](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/tags/mylink/)
    - Typical factory screen sizes varied by trim (small non‑touch units on base cars, larger MyLink touchscreens mid‑to‑top). If reverse camera or parking assist is important to you, check operation — these systems sometimes fail with display faults.
  - 12V battery drain patterns
    - Ageing vehicles + factory telematics/locks can produce parasitic drains, especially after aftermarket audio work. Check the car’s sleep behaviour: a tested draw >50–70 mA is suspicious for a car that will go flat overnight after a few days. Use a multimeter test during inspection.

- Mechanically
  - Brand engineering reputation
    - Holden’s engineering track record on VE/VF is good for structural robustness, with local aftermarket and specialist knowledge a major plus. But age and heavy use (rideshare/long haul) push some components — alternators, power steering pumps, cooling components — into repair cycles.
  - High-priority security vulnerabilities
    - Commodores are not immune to modern theft techniques. The CAN‑injection/“headlight tap” attack is a real world threat for many makes (including RWD Holdens) — attackers access wiring and inject CAN frames to spoof key presence. Read up on the general attack mechanics (not Holden‑specific) here: [Can injection explainer](https://kentindell.github.io/2023/04/03/can-injection/) and reporting overview [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2023/04/06/how-thieves-steal-cars-using-vehicle-can-bus/)
    - Community-sourced mitigations: enable Passive Entry Protection where available (VF owners discuss it), and consider an aftermarket CAN‑aware immobiliser. [Just Commodores keyless protection thread](https://forums.justcommodores.com.au/threads/keyless-entry-theft-protection-for-holden-vf-commodore-chev-ss-protect-against-relay-attack.311557/), [Autowatch Ghost II CANbus immobiliser](https://www.streetsoundvision.com.au/autowatch-ghost-ii-canbus-immobiliser-authorized-d)
  - Recommended after-market anti-theft measures
    - Fit a CAN‑aware immobiliser like **Autowatch Ghost II**, steering lock, and consider line‑cut protection on headlamp/indicator wiring. A visible deterrent still reduces opportunistic theft risk.

- Engines
  - For every engine variant discussed in results I’ll mark the engine code/name bold and give targeted failure points, with preventative service guidance and intervals (practical AU advice: 10,000 km oil service cadence suggested for high-stress usage; longer 12–15k for light private use — but be conservative if evidence of past neglect).
  - *Alloytec V6* (earlier V6 family used before LFX)
    - Failure points: timing‑related wear, PCV/tappet issues, oil‑maintenance related failures. AustralianHolden has a deep write‑up on recurring Alloytec faults and their maintenance sensitivities. [Alloytec faults explained](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/19/common-alloytec-faults-explained/)
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil & filter: every 10,000 km for hard use (rideshare, towing); every 12–15,000 km might be adequate for careful private owners using high‑grade oil.
      - Valve/PCV checks and regular cam/tensioner inspections after ~150,000 km if no prior service history.
  - *LFX 3.6L V6* (commonly in VF and ZB)
    - Failure points: timing‑chain stretch and early wear on higher‑km cars; stretched chains can lead to rough idle, MIL and expensive repair bills. RevHigh and other owners have reported LFX timing chain issues on VF cars at higher kilometres. [LFX timing chain problems](https://revhigh.com.au/post/holden-vf-commodore-3-6l-lfx-timing-chain-problems-what-every-owner-needs-to-know)
    - Cooling & plastic housings: cooling system plastic components (bypass valves, thermostat housings) can crack and leak as they age. TorqueBot covers cooling system weaknesses across the Commodore family. [Cooling system faults summary](https://torquebot.io/cars/holden-commodore/common-problems)
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil & filter: every 10,000 km for heavy use; keep to synthetic oil spec recommended by service docs.
      - If car >120–160k km and no timing chain service history — budget for chain/tensioner replacement or a compressive engine diagnosis. AustralianHolden warns timing repairs can be costly enough to write off some cars. [Timing repair warning](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/03/19/the-one-commodore-repair-that-can-write-off-the-car/)
  - *6.0L / 6.2L V8 (and HSV 7.0L)* — big V8s
    - Failure points: routine age-related wear — oil consumption on badly serviced cars, higher cost of normal consumables (spark plugs, coils), clutch/gearbox stress on manual V8s, and potential overheating if cooling system neglected.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil & filter: every 10,000 km (V8 prefer shorter intervals when used hard).
      - Cooling system flush/inspect every 40–60k km.
      - On manual V8s, inspect clutch and driveline for wear if the car has sporty use.
  - *3.0L V6 (earlier small V6s in VE era)*
    - Failure points: older fuel-pump and sensor wear; less torque than the 3.6L so owners often pushed engines harder — watch for oil leaks and noisy lifters on neglected cars.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Oil every 10–12k km; fuel system inspection around 100k km if original parts present.

  - Service interval reality check
    - Factory schedules vary by year/engine, and Holden historically offered 12–15k km intervals for some services; for a used‑car buyer assume a 10,000 km oil/service cadence unless you can verify light‑use logs. For CV/long life items (timing chains/tensioners) inspect at ~100–160k km depending on engine history. Sources: manufacturer pages and independent diagnosis writeups including TorqueBot and AustralianHolden engine guides. [TorqueBot common problems](https://torquebot.io/cars/holden-commodore/common-problems)

- Transmission
  - Automatics (6L50 / **6L80** and earlier 4/5‑speed boxes)
    - Behaviour: generally reliable but age and mileage expose torque‑converter shudder, harsh shifts, solenoid wear and fluid degradation. Owners report a shudder at light throttle around highway speeds — that often points to the torque converter clutch (TCC) or solenoid faults. [Go‑Parts P0741 guide](https://www.go-parts.com/garage/obd-p0741-holden-commodore-2006-2013-3-6l-v6), [BrisbaneTuning 6L80 shudder analysis](https://www.brisbanetuning.com.au/colorado-6l80-torque-converter-shudder), [AustralianHolden VF transmission guide](https://australianholden.com/index.php/2026/06/07/vf-commodore-transmission-problems/)
    - Known failure modes:
      - Torque‑converter clutch shudder (common symptom: vibration around 80–100 km/h).
      - Fluid leaks from seal points (age) and soft shift due to valve‑body/solenoid wear.
    - Preventative maintenance:
      - Fluid & filter change every 60–80k km (more often if towing) — if unknown service history, change fluid as a first, relatively low-cost step before assuming gearbox replacement.
      - If TCC faults present (codes like P0741), expect the likely permanent fix is torque converter replacement in many cases.
  - Manuals (Aisin AY6, Tremec T56)
    - Behaviour: robust units if driven sensibly; higher cost of clutch replacement on V8/HSV variants. Hydraulic clutch systems on older cars can develop leaks and require pedal feel attention.
  - Buying note
    - Listen for shudder/vibration in highway driving tests. A gearbox with slipping or erratic up/down shifts is a red‑flag; automatic rebuilds or replacements are big bills.

#### 3. Should you buy a used Holden Commodore?
Concrete market reality, money math and the buying call.

- NSW dealer market snapshot (live)
  - Active dealer listings in NSW: 18
  - Price range: AUD $125 – $72,995
  - Median price: AUD $15,490
  - Year range: 2010 – 2018
  - Median odometer: 129,494 km
  - Transmission mix: Automatic: 12, Manual: 1, Sports Automatic: 2
  - (Snapshot from the MTA NSW scraper supplied with your brief — use it as the immediate market reality for NSW dealer stock.)
  - Practical read: plenty of bargains, a few pristine collectors and a big spread because Commodores span cheap daily drivers to HSV collectibles.

- Current used entry to ceiling price & ceiling examples
  - Expect road‑car examples from ~ $5k–$25k for VE/VF V6 sedans depending on condition and km; V8/HSV/low‑km examples can command $30k–$70k+ — consistent with the active range up to ~$73k in the NSW snapshot.
  - For a crisp market reference, CarsGuide sample listings show 2013 models ranging ~$6,380–$45,760 depending on specification. [CarsGuide pricing range](https://www.carsguide.com.au/holden/commodore/price/2013)

- Depreciation metrics
  - Historically steep: Commodore and large Australian sedans depreciated heavily since the end of local production; Glass’s and WhichCar have spotlighted heavy drops in resale for newer body style examples (some near‑new 3‑year retain ~36% of new price in extreme examples cited). WhichCar/Drive coverage identifies the Commodore among segment leaders in depreciation post‑production. [WhichCar depreciation note](https://www.whichcar.com.au/advice/which-cars-depreciate-the-most), [Drive historical context](https://www.drive.com.au/)
  - Takeaway: you buy one to avoid depreciation pains — i.e., buy late‑model cheap rather than paying a premium new. The car you buy today will likely be extremely cheap in five years; that’s already baked into current prices.

- Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) strategy
  - Fuel
    - Practical fuel consumption: expect real world numbers in the 8–10 L/100 km ballpark for V6 sedans under mixed driving (Auto‑Data shows ZB 3.6 V6 combined ~8.9–9.3 L/100 km). Owners’ aggregated data (Fuelly) shows a wide spread depending on engine and driving. [Auto‑Data ZB 3.6 spec and consumption](https://www.auto-data.net/en/holden-commodore-sedan-v-zb-3.6i-v6-320hp-awd-automatic-32782), [Fuelly aggregated data](https://www.fuelly.com/car/holden/commodore)
  - Maintenance & repairs
    - Budget for variable spends: tyres, brakes, gearbox service, timing‑chain (if present) and potential infotainment replacement. Major engine or automatic rebuilds are the largest risks; they tend to appear after 150–250k km on neglected cars.
  - Depreciation + servicing maths
    - Use a TCO calculator (Edmunds or government alternatives) to model 3–5 year horizons — include fuel at local petrol prices, insurance, rego, and a conservative allowance for a major engine/transmission repair at some point. [Edmunds TCO tool](https://www.edmunds.com/tco.html), [AFDC cost calc for methodology](https://afdc.energy.gov/calc/)
  - Hybrid/EV premium sane?
    - Holden’s hybrid Commodore history is niche (ECOmmodore concept and experiments) — there are no common factory hybrids in the mainstream VE/VF/ZB used market. Paying a premium today for a rare hybrid Commodore is unlikely to deliver broad fuel savings given scarcity, potential battery replacement cost and lack of used‑market support. WhichCar’s archive on ECOmmodore is mostly a historical curiosity rather than a practical TCO win. [ECOmmodore archive](https://www.whichcar.com.au/features/classic-wheels/wheels-archive-super-coupe-the-sexiest-holden-commodore-all-time-hybrid)
    - For modern fuel savings, compare a conventional VF LFX V6 against a modern hybrid Camry or a small EV — but the math is external to Commodore: use TCO calculators to model your specific annual km and fuel prices. If you do >40–50k km/year and fuel is your biggest line item, a modern hybrid may win. If you do typical Australian family kms (10–20k pa), a well‑sorted V6 Commodore is cheap to buy and service.

- Bottom line recommendation (who should buy what)
  - Buy one if:
    - You want spacious RWD sedan dynamics, easy local specialist support, cheap entry prices and you accept older car ownership realities.
    - You’re buying a VE/VF as a cheap, honest runabout or as an HSV/project — there’s a huge aftermarket and friendly specialist community in Australia.
  - Don’t buy one if:
    - You want the lowest‑risk, turn‑key reliability with minimal maintenance cost — modern Camrys and hybrid crossovers will likely be lower‑stress.
    - You can’t inspect for timing chain history or gearbox shudder — avoid unknown high‑km examples without documentation.
  - Specific sweet-spot recommendation
    - Practical sweet spot: a mid‑spec **VF** (2013–2016) with the **LFX 3.6L V6** and full service history, around the 100k–160k km band. You get improved mechanicals over early VE models, common parts availability, and most running issues (if fixed) are known and affordable. VF cars after a mid‑life service tend to be the best balance of durability, features and price. [VF generation overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holden_Commodore_(VF))
    - If you want performance and can afford it: low‑km HSV examples are collectible but trade liquidity declines — buy only after a specialist inspection and secure provenance.
  - Bargain hunting
    - If your budget is around the NSW median of ~AUD $15,490, expect to buy a high‑km VE/VF V6 with cosmetic wear — that’s fine if you want value, but budget for immediate mechanical checks and likely reinvestment in tyres, front suspension bushes and brakes. (NSW market median figure supplied in the brief.)

- Final tactical checklist before buying
  - Ask for service history focusing on:
    - Oil change records (every 10k or better).
    - Any timing‑chain/tensioner work or engine rebuilds (red‑flag if missing on high km cars).
    - Transmission fluid changes — if none, budget for one immediately.
  - Test drive checklist:
    - Highway run for torque‑converter shudder or clutch slip.
    - Idle and tick-over for roughness (timing chain stretch clues).
    - Test infotainment, reversing camera and all ADAS functions.
  - Physical checks:
    - Lift and inspect for seam corrosion, inner wings and sills.
    - Check under carpet for water ingress (a damp floor mat often hides head gasket or drain issues).
  - Security:
    - If the car is keyless or has passive entry, budget AUD $700–$1,800 to fit a CAN‑aware immobiliser like the **Autowatch Ghost II** for real peace of mind. [Autowatch Ghost II product summary](https://www.streetsoundvision.com.au/autowatch-ghost-ii-canbus-immobiliser-authorized-d)
